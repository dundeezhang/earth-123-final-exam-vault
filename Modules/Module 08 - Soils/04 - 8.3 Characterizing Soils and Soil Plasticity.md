# 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[00 - Module 08 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

> [!important]
> Know the course's four classification components, the field and laboratory texture tests, the exact grain-size values in Table 8.3.1, the four consistency states in water-content order, all three Atterberg limits, and the meaning and calculation of the plasticity index.

## Core Notes

### Characterizing Soils

- Soils can differ strongly in water content, grain size, and capacity to support plants; beach sand and moist topsoil are the course's contrast.
- The course introduces the **Unified Soil Classification System (USCS)** as a consistent worldwide approach and organizes characterization around **colour, texture, structure, and pH**.
- Proper classification matters for infrastructure and foundation design because different soils have different strengths and responses to water.

### Soil Colour

- Colour is an immediate classification clue when a physical sample is available.
- Darker soil often indicates more organic material, higher water content and storage capacity, and greater fertility or capability.
- Lighter soil often indicates lower water content and greater leaching of organics and other important minerals.

### Soil Texture

- **Texture** means both the soil's feel and, more importantly, its average grain size.
- Larger sand particles feel rough or angular. Finer silt- and clay-rich soil feels smoother and softer.
- **Porosity** is the amount of empty space in soil; **permeability** is how easily water moves through the soil medium.

#### Field Texture Tests

| Test | Procedure | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Dry-strength test | Try to break a dry piece of soil with the fingers. | More sand fractures or falls apart more easily; finer silt- or clay-rich soil behaves more like modelling clay or plasticine. |
| Knife test | Cut through intact, reasonably dry soil. | A shiny cut face indicates high clay content; a crumbly, dry face indicates low clay content. |
| Teeth test | An expert cautiously grinds a known-safe fragment between the teeth. | Gritty and rough means less clay and more silt and sand; smooth means mostly clay-sized material with little to no sand. |

> [!warning]
> The course does not recommend the teeth test for general use. Soil may contain pollutants or bacteria; only experts who already know the soil is safe should perform it, without swallowing the sample.

#### Sieve Test and Grain Size

- A laboratory **sieve test** places soil on the coarsest screen in a stack. Shaking allows progressively smaller particles to pass downward until a screen retains them.
- Measuring the material retained at each screen produces a grain-size distribution or curve.
- Table 8.3.1 gives this exact ordered set of entries, all in millimetres:

| Grain-size heading | Fine entry (mm) | Medium entry (mm) | Coarse entry (mm) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Clay | $<0.002$ | - | - |
| Silt | $0.006$ | $0.02$ | $0.06$ |
| Sand | $0.2$ | $0.6$ | $2$ |
| Gravel | $6$ | $20$ | $60$ |
| Cobbles | $200$ | - | - |
| Boulders | $>600$ | - | - |

The table caption explicitly states that samples below $0.002\ \mathrm{mm}$ are clay and samples above $600\ \mathrm{mm}$ are boulders. Preserve the intermediate sequence exactly as printed: $0.006$, $0.02$, $0.06$, $0.2$, $0.6$, $2$, $6$, $20$, $60$, $200$.

- Average human hair diameter is approximately $0.02$ to $0.1\ \mathrm{mm}$, or $20$ to $100\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, comparable to large silt or small sand.
- The course describes a clay particle as about one thousand times finer than a human hair.

### Soil Structure

- **Structure** measures soil density or penetration strength across an area and can also describe small-scale clumpiness or the ability to stick together.
- Higher density or stronger structure can discourage young root growth.
- Repeated agricultural tilling can compact soil, increase density, and reduce crop yields.
- Large clumps can also hinder establishment of strong young root systems.

### Soil pH

- Soil **pH** measures acidity and affects nutrient uptake and plant growth.
- Very high pH means overly alkaline conditions; very low pH means overly acidic conditions. Either can block nutrient absorption and damage or kill vegetation.
- Slightly acidic precipitation and, more commonly, excessive or improper chemical and organic fertilizer use can alter soil pH.

### Soil Plasticity

- **Plasticity** is the ability of soil to undergo unrecoverable deformation at constant volume without cracking, crumbling, or breaking apart.
- High-plasticity soil is moist and mouldable like modelling clay. Low-plasticity soil feels drier and breaks apart.
- Water content controls the consistency state. In order of **decreasing water content**:

| State | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Liquid | Completely saturated, flowing muddy slurry with little to no strength. |
| Plastic | Common for fine-grained soils; deforms under pressure without cracking or falling apart. |
| Semi-solid | Common for medium and coarser soils; initially deforms plastically, then crumbles or breaks. |
| Solid | Quite dry; pressure causes immediate brittle failure rather than deformation. |

#### Atterberg Limits

| Limit | Transition | Testable meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid limit, $LL$ | Liquid to plastic | Upper limit and maximum water content at which plastic behaviour continues; slightly more water produces a strengthless slurry. |
| Plastic limit, $PL$ | Plastic to semi-solid | Lower limit and minimum water content at which plastic behaviour continues; slightly less water produces semi-solid behaviour. |
| Shrinkage limit | Semi-solid to solid | Point below which further drying produces no additional volume reduction; water content is at or near zero. |

- **Figure 8.3.1:** increasing water content moves soil from solid through semi-solid and plastic to liquid. Total volume rises with increasing water content, and the three Atterberg limits mark the state transitions.
- The **plasticity index** is the width of the plastic range. A large $PI$ means soil can absorb much more water before becoming a slurry. A small $PI$ means only a small addition of water is needed to reach liquid behaviour, which is challenging for civil, environmental, and geological engineering.

## Equations and Units

$$
PI=LL-PL
$$

- Subtract the plastic limit from the liquid limit; do not reverse them.
- $PI$ uses the same water-content scale as the two limits. When limits are expressed as percentages, report their difference in percentage points.

> [!example] Worked plasticity-index pattern
> If $LL=52\%$ and $PL=24\%$, then $PI=52-24=28$ percentage points. A second soil with $LL=34\%$ and $PL=28\%$ has $PI=6$ percentage points and therefore needs a much smaller water-content increase to pass through its plastic range and reach liquid behaviour.

## Common Confusions

- **Colour vs. texture:** colour suggests organic matter, water, and leaching; texture is controlled primarily by grain size.
- **Porosity vs. permeability:** porosity measures empty space, while permeability measures ease of water movement.
- **Structure vs. texture:** structure concerns density, penetration strength, and clumpiness; texture concerns particle size and feel.
- **Plasticity vs. plasticity index:** plasticity is the deformation property; $PI$ is the numerical width of the plastic water-content range.
- **Plastic limit vs. liquid limit:** the plastic limit is the lower-water transition; the liquid limit is the higher-water transition.
- **Shrinkage limit:** it is the semi-solid-solid transition, not the point between plastic and semi-solid.
- **Table 8.3.1:** use the values as printed in the saved course table; do not silently replace them with a different classification scale.
