# 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships

## Exam Focus

> [!important]
> Know the four components of soil, the five controls on soil formation, all five phase relationships, and the O-A-E-B-C-R horizon sequence. Be able to select the correct phase ratio and interpret the erosion evidence in Figures 8.1.3 and 8.1.4.

## Core Notes

### Soil, Zones, and Importance

- **Soil** combines mineral matter, organic matter from local vegetation, water, and air in unfilled void spaces.
- **Regolith** is the combined set of soil and rock layers covering Earth's surface.

![Vadose and saturated zones beneath soil](../../Assets/Module%2008/figure-8.1.1-vadose-saturated-zones.jpg)

*Figure 8.1.1. Unsaturated and saturated zones beneath the ground surface. VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** The vadose zone extends from the ground surface to the water table. Figure 8.1.1 groups the capillary fringe with saturated materials and shows groundwater below it. Water moves downward through the vadose zone, then can be stored or move laterally through saturated cracks and voids.

- Soil links living and non-living realms. It supports plants and agriculture, root systems, and subsurface micro- and macro-organisms.
- Soil strength matters to foundations and engineering because weak or compressible soil can fail under structural loads.

### Why Soil Understanding Matters

![Leaning Tower of Pisa](../../Assets/Module%2008/figure-8.1.2-leaning-tower-pisa.jpg)

*Figure 8.1.2. The Leaning Tower of Pisa. Phooey/E+/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** By the third floor, the tower leaned south because its massive foundation stones caused uneven compression of the soil below. The example links soil strength and compressibility directly to foundation performance.

![Mississippi River sediment plume entering the Gulf of Mexico](../../Assets/Module%2008/figure-8.1.3-mississippi-sediment-plume.jpg)

*Figure 8.1.3. Envisat image of the Mississippi River and Gulf of Mexico, annotated by the course author. European Space Agency, 2007.*

**What to notice:** The yellow-green plume traces eroded topsoil and contaminants transported through the watershed into the Gulf. This material contributes to low-oxygen hypoxic conditions that make the area less suitable for marine and coastal life.

### Erosion Evidence on Campus

The course's campus example is a student-created walking path between the Math 3 Building and B. C. Matthews Hall.

![Eroded footpath on the University of Waterloo campus](../../Assets/Module%2008/figure-8.1.4-waterloo-soil-erosion.jpg)

*Figure 8.1.4. Soil erosion on the University of Waterloo campus. Course Author.*

**What to notice:** Foot traffic removed protective grass and compacted the surface, allowing runoff to concentrate into a central channel. The knowledge check identifies five linked observations:

1. **Slope effects:** gravity gets water moving; slope can accelerate erosion.
2. **Water effects:** downslope flow can channelize, deepen, and accelerate, increasing sediment-carrying ability.
3. **Grain-size sorting:** finer grains dominate the path edges, while larger gravel and cobbles are more visible near the centre.
4. **Sediment transport:** lower depths and velocities at the edges move only small particles; higher depths and velocities in the central channel remove sand and larger material, leaving coarse clasts exposed.
5. **People:** foot traffic killed grass and compacted soil, triggering channelization that likely would not otherwise have formed.

### Controls on Soil Formation

| Control | Effect on soil formation |
|---|---|
| Parent material | Bedrock supplies the initial mineral matter. Rock type affects soil type, mineral content, and formation rate. |
| Time | Short development produces shallower soil with larger grains; more time allows more weathering and erosion, smaller grains, and deeper profiles. |
| Climate | Precipitation supplies water for chemical weathering. Cold slows development, while warmth increases it. |
| Plants and animals | Roots break material and affect water movement; dead vegetation adds organic matter; bacteria and fungi decompose material; worms and beetles create pathways and feed on soil and organic matter. |
| Topography | Steeper terrain encourages transport and produces shallower, less-developed profiles. Flat terrain can accumulate thicker soil with more water and organic matter. |

### Phase Relationships in Soils

- Scientists use phase relationships to assess water movement and subsurface storage capacity.
- Important controls include **particle size distribution**, **porosity**, and **soil density**. The course states that higher densities slow water movement.
- Phase relationships combine the masses, $m$, and volumes, $V$, of solids, water, air, and voids.

![Three-phase soil mass and volume diagram](../../Assets/Module%2008/figure-8.1.5-three-phase-soil.png)

*Figure 8.1.5. Three-phase soil diagram showing air, water, and solids. Soil Mechanics, n.d.*

**What to notice:** Air and water together occupy the void volume, while solids occupy the remaining volume. Water and solids contribute mass in the course equations; air mass is omitted from this simplified phase model.

![Soil particles water air and void measures](../../Assets/Module%2008/figure-8.1.5-soil-parts-measures.png)

*Figure 8.1.5. Parts and measures of a soil sample. Course Author and University of Waterloo. The saved course page duplicates this figure number.*

**What to notice:** The sample view links each phase to its matching mass and volume symbol and makes the identity $V_{\text{voids}}=V_{\text{air}}+V_{\text{water}}$ visible. Use the requested ratio to decide whether the denominator is total, solids, or void volume.

### The Soil Profile

- A developed soil has multiple horizons whose number, depth, composition, and properties vary by location.
- In the course's general trend, organic matter and weathering decrease with depth, while grain size increases until bedrock is reached.

![Conceptual soil horizons from organic layer to bedrock](../../Assets/Module%2008/figure-8.1.7-soil-horizons.jpg)

*Figure 8.1.7. Conceptual soil horizons from topsoil to parent rock. VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** Read the idealized profile from surface to depth as O-A-E-B-C-R. Organic matter and weathering generally decrease downward, while particle size increases toward weathered parent material and bedrock.

![Coastal soil profile with visible colour horizons](../../Assets/Module%2008/figure-8.1.8-soil-profile.jpg)

*Figure 8.1.8. Real soil profile with visible upper horizons. Montgomery, 2014, p. 262.*

**What to notice:** Real horizon boundaries are irregular rather than clean bands. O, A, E, and B can be inferred from colour changes, but the deeper C and R horizons are outside this exposure.

| Horizon | Position and testable characteristics |
|---|---|
| O | First horizon; living and decaying organic matter; part of topsoil; often called humus. |
| A | Below O; mineral and organic matter; second topsoil horizon; many roots and micro- and macro-organisms. |
| E | Lighter because downward water movement leaches soluble minerals and organics; transition from topsoil to subsoil. |
| B | First subsoil horizon; fine material leached from above; accumulations of clay and silt because the smallest particles infiltrate downward most readily. |
| C | Newly created soil plus blocky cobbles and boulders not yet broken into smaller sizes; transition from advanced soil above to bedrock below. |
| R | Local geological bedrock rather than soil; parent material for the horizons above. |

> [!tip]
> Memorize the order from surface to depth as **O-A-E-B-C-R**. O and A are topsoil, E is the leached transition, B is the first subsoil, C is weathered parent material, and R is rock.

## Equations and Units

| Quantity | Equation | Meaning and units |
|---|---|---|
| Porosity | $n=\dfrac{V_{\text{voids}}}{V_{\text{total}}}$ | Void volume divided by total sample volume; dimensionless, often expressed as a percent. |
| Void ratio | $e=\dfrac{V_{\text{voids}}}{V_{\text{solids}}}$ | Void volume divided by solid volume; dimensionless. |
| Water content | $w=\dfrac{m_{\text{water}}}{m_{\text{solids}}}$ | Water mass divided by solid mass; dimensionless, often expressed as a percent. |
| Degree of saturation | $S_r=\dfrac{V_{\text{water}}}{V_{\text{voids}}}$ | Water volume divided by void volume; dimensionless, often expressed as a percent. |
| Bulk density | $\rho=\dfrac{m_{\text{total}}}{V_{\text{total}}}$ | Total mass divided by total volume; course unit is $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. |

> [!example] Worked calculation patterns
> 1. **Course porosity check:** $V_{\text{voids}}=1\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ and $V_{\text{total}}=10\ \mathrm{cm^3}$. Then $n=1/10=0.10=10\%$.
> 2. **Course saturation check:** $V_{\text{water}}=5\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ and $V_{\text{voids}}=10\ \mathrm{cm^3}$. Then $S_r=5/10=0.50=50\%$.
> 3. **Void-ratio pattern:** if $V_{\text{voids}}=12\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ and $V_{\text{solids}}=48\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, then $e=12/48=0.25$.
> 4. **Water-content pattern:** if $m_{\text{water}}=8\ \mathrm{g}$ and $m_{\text{solids}}=40\ \mathrm{g}$, then $w=8/40=0.20=20\%$.
> 5. **Bulk-density pattern:** if $m_{\text{total}}=180\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $V_{\text{total}}=0.12\ \mathrm{m^3}$, then $\rho=180/0.12=1500\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$.

The general method is to identify the named phase quantity, select its exact numerator and denominator, cancel matching units, and multiply by 100 only when a percentage is requested.

## Common Confusions

- **Saturated vs. vadose:** the vadose zone is above the water table; the saturated zone includes groundwater and the capillary fringe in Figure 8.1.1.
- **Porosity vs. void ratio:** porosity divides void volume by total volume; void ratio divides it by solid volume.
- **Water content vs. saturation:** water content uses masses; degree of saturation uses volumes.
- **Density vs. porosity:** density is mass per total volume; porosity is the fraction of total volume occupied by voids.
- **O vs. A:** both contribute to topsoil, but O is dominated by organic matter while A mixes mineral and organic matter.
- **E vs. B:** E is the lighter leached transition; B is the first subsoil horizon where fine clay and silt accumulate.
- **C vs. R:** C contains weathered soil and large rock fragments; R is bedrock and is not soil.
