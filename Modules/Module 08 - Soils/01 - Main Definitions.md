# Main Definitions

| Term | Definition | Course section | Example / where / application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil | A combination of mineral matter, organic matter, water, and air. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | Moist topsoil supporting plant roots contains mineral grains, decaying vegetation, pore water, and air-filled voids. |
| Mineral matter | The physical soil material at the surface that is not rock. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | Sand, silt, and clay grains mixed with organics in the A horizon are mineral matter. |
| Organic matter | Material from local vegetation, including roots, humus, leaf litter, and decomposition products. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | Leaf litter, decaying roots, and humus concentrate in the O horizon. |
| Regolith | The combined layers of soils and rocks that cover Earth's surface. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | In Figure 8.1.7, the soil profile and weathered rock layers over local bedrock form the surface cover. |
| Unsaturated zone / vadose zone | The zone above the water table in which soil voids are not completely filled with water; much soil life resides here. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | Figure 8.1.1 places it between the ground surface and water table, where water generally moves downward through partly filled voids. |
| Saturated zone | The zone in which soil voids are filled with water; Figure 8.1.1 groups groundwater and the capillary fringe within it. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | Below the water table, groundwater can be stored or move laterally through cracks and voids. |
| Water table | The boundary at depth between the vadose zone above and saturated groundwater below. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | A hydrologist uses its depth to separate downward vadose-zone flow from groundwater storage and lateral flow. |
| Capillary fringe | The layer shown immediately above groundwater and included in the saturated zone in Figure 8.1.1. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | In Figure 8.1.1 it is the narrow saturated layer directly above the groundwater body. |
| Hypoxic zone | Water with low oxygen content; the northern Gulf of Mexico example is linked to transported soil, nutrients, and pollution. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships\|8.1 opening section]] | Mississippi River sediment, urban pollution, and fertilizer reach the northern Gulf of Mexico, where low oxygen drives away marine animals. |
| Parent material | The base geological material from which weathering and erosion create soil particles. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Controls on Soil Formation\|8.1 Controls on Soil Formation]] | Local bedrock in the R horizon supplies the initial mineral matter for the horizons above. |
| Topography | Surface slope; steep terrain favours transport and shallower soils, whereas flatter terrain can accumulate thicker, wetter, more organic deposits. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Controls on Soil Formation\|8.1 Controls on Soil Formation]] | On the sloping University of Waterloo footpath, downslope water channelizes and strips finer sediment. |
| Particle size distribution | The proportions of particle sizes such as clay, sand, and gravel in a soil. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Phase Relationships in Soils\|8.1 Phase Relationships in Soils]] | Weighing the material retained on each sieve yields the sample's grain-size distribution. |
| Porosity, $n$ | The ratio of void volume to total sample volume: $n=V_{\text{voids}}/V_{\text{total}}$. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Phase Relationships in Soils\|8.1 Phase Relationships in Soils]] | The course sample with $1\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of voids in $10\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ total volume has $n=10\%$. |
| Void ratio, $e$ | The ratio of void volume to solid volume: $e=V_{\text{voids}}/V_{\text{solids}}$. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Phase Relationships in Soils\|8.1 Phase Relationships in Soils]] | For $12\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of voids and $48\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of solids, $e=12/48=0.25$. |
| Water content, $w$ | The ratio of water mass to solid mass: $w=m_{\text{water}}/m_{\text{solids}}$. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Phase Relationships in Soils\|8.1 Phase Relationships in Soils]] | A sample with $8\ \mathrm{g}$ of water and $40\ \mathrm{g}$ of solids has $w=20\%$. |
| Degree of saturation, $S_r$ | The ratio of water volume to void volume: $S_r=V_{\text{water}}/V_{\text{voids}}$. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Phase Relationships in Soils\|8.1 Phase Relationships in Soils]] | The course sample with $5\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of water in $10\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of voids has $S_r=50\%$. |
| Bulk density, $\rho$ | Total sample mass divided by total sample volume: $\rho=m_{\text{total}}/V_{\text{total}}$, reported in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Phase Relationships in Soils\|8.1 Phase Relationships in Soils]] | A $180\ \mathrm{kg}$ sample occupying $0.12\ \mathrm{m^3}$ has $\rho=1500\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$. |
| Soil density | A measure affecting water movement through soil; the course states that higher density slows movement. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#Phase Relationships in Soils\|8.1 Phase Relationships in Soils]] | Repeated foot traffic compacts the Waterloo path soil, producing a denser surface through which water moves more slowly. |

| Soil horizon | A layer in a developed soil with a distinct composition and set of characteristics. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#The Soil Profile\|8.1 The Soil Profile]] | In Figure 8.1.8, colour gradients distinguish the inferred O, A, E, and B layers. |
| Topsoil | The O and A horizons near the surface. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#The Soil Profile\|8.1 The Soil Profile]] | The Mississippi plume in Figure 8.1.3 contains valuable topsoil eroded from farmland in the watershed. |
| Subsoil | The horizons beneath the topsoil; B is identified as the first subsoil horizon. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#The Soil Profile\|8.1 The Soil Profile]] | In Figure 8.1.7, the clay- and silt-enriched B horizon begins the subsoil below E. |
| Humus | A name often used for the organic-rich O horizon. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#The Soil Profile\|8.1 The Soil Profile]] | The dark, decomposing surface material beneath leaf litter is the profile's humus-rich O layer. |
| O horizon | The first horizon; rich in living and decaying organic matter and part of the topsoil. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#The Soil Profile\|8.1 The Soil Profile]] | In Figure 8.1.7, it is the surface layer of litter and organic material above A. |
| A horizon | A topsoil horizon containing mineral and organic matter, roots, and many micro- and macro-organisms. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#The Soil Profile\|8.1 The Soil Profile]] | Plant roots and soil organisms occupy this mixed mineral-organic layer directly below O. |

| B horizon | The first subsoil horizon, with fine material leached from above and accumulations of clay and silt. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#The Soil Profile\|8.1 The Soil Profile]] | Clay and silt carried down from upper horizons accumulate in this layer beneath E. |

| Leaching | Downward transport by water of soluble minerals, organics, and fine material from upper horizons. | [[02 - 8.1 Introduction to Soils and Phase Relationships#The Soil Profile\|8.1 The Soil Profile]] | Leaching lightens the E horizon and transfers fine clay and silt into the B horizon. |
| Soil degradation | Loss or decline in valuable topsoil through processes such as erosion, desertification, contamination, fertilizer overuse, and intensified agriculture. | [[03 - 8.2 Soil Erosion, Degradation, and Weathering Processes\|8.2 opening section]] | In the Figure 8.2.1 knowledge check, Southeast Asia has the greatest soil loss or degradation among the four listed regions. |
| Desertification | A form of soil degradation identified by the course. | [[03 - 8.2 Soil Erosion, Degradation, and Weathering Processes\|8.2 opening section]] | The Sahara Desert is a comparison setting in the course's Figure 8.2.1 knowledge check on global soil loss. |
| Erosion | Physical removal and transport of soil, sediment, or other weathered material from where it formed to a new location. | [[03 - 8.2 Soil Erosion, Degradation, and Weathering Processes#Soil Erosion\|8.2 Soil Erosion]] | The yellow-green Mississippi plume shows farmland topsoil carried hundreds of kilometres and deposited in the Gulf of Mexico. |
| Weathering | Physical, chemical, or biological processes that break rocks down to form soil. | [[03 - 8.2 Soil Erosion, Degradation, and Weathering Processes#Soil Weathering\|8.2 Soil Weathering]] | Exposed rock, cooled lava, volcanic ash, and dunes begin weathering when open to the atmosphere. |
| Physical / mechanical weathering | Breakdown into smaller pieces that retain the original parent material's characteristics except size. | [[03 - 8.2 Soil Erosion, Degradation, and Weathering Processes#Soil Weathering\|8.2 Soil Weathering]] | Freezing water expands in cracks and breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its mineral composition. |
| Chemical weathering | Breakdown of internal mineral composition that converts some or all parent material into new and different minerals. | [[03 - 8.2 Soil Erosion, Degradation, and Weathering Processes#Soil Weathering\|8.2 Soil Weathering]] | Water enables chemical reactions and ion transfer that convert parent minerals into different minerals. |
| Chemical fertilizer | A mined or synthetically manufactured nutrient supplement. | [[03 - 8.2 Soil Erosion, Degradation, and Weathering Processes#Soil Erosion\|8.2 Soil Erosion]] | Excess application can alter local soil and water chemistry for years and pollute groundwater, surface water, and the atmosphere. |
| Organic fertilizer | Fertilizer commonly made from animal waste or composted plant material. | [[03 - 8.2 Soil Erosion, Degradation, and Weathering Processes#Soil Erosion\|8.2 Soil Erosion]] | The course points to organic fertilizer contamination of drinking-water resources in Walkerton, Ontario. |
| Unified Soil Classification System (USCS) | The system the course uses to characterize soils consistently through colour, texture, structure, and pH. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity\|8.3 opening section]] | Scientists record colour, texture, structure, and pH to compare site soils before infrastructure or foundation design. |
| Soil colour | A classification clue: darker colour often indicates more organic matter, water content, storage capacity, and fertility; lighter colour often indicates less water and more leaching. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Colour\|8.3 Soil Colour]] | Dark moist topsoil suggests organic matter and water storage, while the leached E horizon appears lighter. |
| Soil texture | A soil's average grain size and the feel produced by that grain size. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Texture\|8.3 Soil Texture]] | Beach sand feels rough or angular, while silt- and clay-rich soil feels smooth and soft. |
| Permeability | A measure of how easily water moves through a soil medium. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Texture\|8.3 Soil Texture]] | A hydrologist assesses permeability to judge how readily groundwater can pass through a sampled subsurface soil. |
| Dry-strength test | A field test that tries to break dry soil by hand; sand-rich material breaks more easily, whereas fine or clay-rich material behaves more like plasticine. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Texture\|8.3 Soil Texture]] | A dry chunk that fractures easily between the fingers indicates more sand; plasticine-like resistance indicates finer material. |
| Knife test | A cut-surface test for reasonably dry soil; a shiny face indicates high clay content and a crumbly, dry face indicates low clay content. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Texture\|8.3 Soil Texture]] | In the field, a scientist cuts an intact dry sample and reads a shiny face as clay-rich. |
| Teeth test | A cautious expert-only texture test; gritty means less clay and more silt and sand, while smooth indicates mostly clay-sized particles. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Texture\|8.3 Soil Texture]] | An expert testing a known-safe sample interprets grit as silt or sand and smoothness as mostly clay. |
| Sieve test | A laboratory test in which stacked coarse-to-fine screens separate a sample into grain-size groups. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Texture\|8.3 Soil Texture]] | Shaking a sample through the stack and measuring each retained fraction produces a grain-size curve. |
| Soil structure | Soil density or penetration strength; at small scales it also describes clumpiness and the ability to stick together. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Structure\|8.3 Soil Structure]] | Repeated agricultural tilling can compact soil and create dense clumps that hinder young roots and reduce yields. |
| Soil pH | A measure of soil acidity that influences nutrient uptake and plant growth. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil pH\|8.3 Soil pH]] | Excess fertilizer can shift pH high or low enough to block nutrient absorption and damage vegetation. |
| Soil plasticity | The ability to undergo unrecoverable deformation at constant volume without cracking, crumbling, or breaking apart. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Moist, high-plasticity soil can be moulded like modelling clay and retains its new shape. |
| Liquid state | A completely saturated, flowing muddy slurry with little to no strength. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Above the liquid limit, a wet sample flows as a muddy slurry rather than supporting a load. |
| Plastic state | A state common in fine-grained soils: the sample deforms under pressure without cracking or falling apart. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Between $PL$ and $LL$, a fine-grained sample can be pressed into a new shape without cracking. |
| Semi-solid state | A state common in medium and coarser soils: the sample first deforms plastically, then crumbles or breaks. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Below $PL$, a medium- or coarse-grained sample deforms briefly before crumbling. |
| Solid state | A dry state in which deformation causes immediate brittle failure. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Below the shrinkage limit, pressure on the dry sample causes immediate brittle breakage. |
| Atterberg limits | The water-content transitions between liquid, plastic, semi-solid, and solid consistency states. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Figure 8.3.1 places the shrinkage, plastic, and liquid limits along an increasing water-content axis. |
| Liquid limit, $LL$ | The liquid-plastic transition and maximum water content at which soil can still exhibit plastic behaviour. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Adding slightly more water than $LL$ turns the plastic sample into a strengthless slurry. |
| Plastic limit, $PL$ | The plastic-semi-solid transition and minimum water content at which soil can still exhibit plastic behaviour. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Removing slightly more water below $PL$ makes the sample behave as a semi-solid. |
| Shrinkage limit | The semi-solid-solid transition, below which further drying causes no more volume reduction. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | Once a drying sample crosses this limit, additional water loss no longer decreases its total volume. |
| Plasticity index, $PI$ | The width of the plastic water-content range: $PI=LL-PL$. | [[04 - 8.3 Characterizing Soils and Soil Plasticity#Soil Plasticity\|8.3 Soil Plasticity]] | For $LL=52\%$ and $PL=24\%$, the soil has $PI=28$ percentage points. |

## Commonly Confused Terms

- **Soil vs. regolith:** soil is the four-part material of mineral matter, organic matter, water, and air; regolith also includes the rock layers covering the surface.
- **Porosity vs. void ratio:** both use $V_{\text{voids}}$ in the numerator, but porosity divides by total volume and void ratio divides by solid volume.
- **Porosity vs. permeability:** porosity is how much empty space exists; permeability is how easily water moves through the material.
- **Water content vs. degree of saturation:** water content compares water mass with solid mass; saturation compares water volume with void volume.
- **Weathering vs. erosion:** weathering breaks material down; erosion removes and transports it.
- **Physical vs. chemical weathering:** physical weathering changes size while retaining composition; chemical weathering changes mineral composition.
- **Plasticity vs. plasticity index:** plasticity is a deformation behaviour; $PI$ is the numerical difference between liquid and plastic limits.
- **O, A, and E horizons:** O and A are topsoil; E is the lighter, leached transition toward the subsoil.
