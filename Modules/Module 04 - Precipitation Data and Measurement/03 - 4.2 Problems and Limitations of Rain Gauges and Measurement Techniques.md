# 4.2 Problems and Limitations of Rain Gauges and Measurement Techniques

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

## Exam Focus

Identify why gauges under- or mismeasure precipitation, match each error with its mitigation, explain the special problems of snowfall measurement, and calculate snow density from a core sample.

## Core Notes

Accurate and precise precipitation data are prerequisites for understanding regional processes, climate-change effects, models, and simulations. Even a well-designed network with good instruments and ample labour will still produce some errors; poor input data lead to poor forecasts and predictions.

### Limitations of rain gauges

| Problem | Mechanism | Mitigation or design response |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical failure and observer error | Equipment can fail unpredictably, and observers can make many kinds of mistakes. | These errors cannot be fully prevented. Monitoring and quality control remain necessary. |
| Wind effects | A gauge obstructs smooth airflow; turbulence carries rain around or past the opening, producing under-catch. Wind speed also increases with height. | Keep gauges fairly near the ground and use shields to slow wind near the opening. |
| Placement and orientation | Oblique rainfall can miss the opening. Catch deficiency decreases as opening size increases. | Keep the funnel opening horizontal to the ground surface, including on a slope. |
| Evaporation | Collected water may evaporate before a non-recording gauge is read. Warm gauges and internal air movement increase loss. | Limit internal airflow; float a thin oil film on the sample; use reflective material or light colours to reduce solar heating. |
| Gauge placement | Nearby topography, vegetation, or infrastructure disturbs wind and makes the sample unrepresentative. | Prefer a flat, open, low-wind site at least $4\times$ the height of the nearest object away from that object. |

- **Figure 4.2.1:** Memorize the $4\times$-object-height placement rule.

### Wind-shield measurement techniques

Mechanical and observer errors are difficult to eliminate, but gauge shape and shielding can reduce wind-related losses.

- **Figure 4.2.2:** Compare (a) an unshielded gauge, (b) a rigid Nipher-type shield, and (c) an Alter-type shield. The arrows show how wind moves around each arrangement.
- The purpose of both shield designs is to slow air near the collection opening, maximize catch, and reduce wind losses.

### Measuring snowfall

Snowfall is harder to quantify than rain because wind can remobilize snow after it reaches the surface. Drifts and exposed ground can coexist after one event, so a point measurement may poorly represent the final areal distribution. A gauge network gives the best attempt at event-scale measurement.

**Snow gauges**

- A standard non-recording snow gauge is a hollow metal cylinder about 5 inches (13 cm) in diameter.
- It is commonly raised to about 3 feet (1 m) above ground to prevent burial and may be placed still higher where extreme snow depth is expected.
- Most recording and non-recording snow gauges use Nipher-type shields to reduce turbulence at the mouth.
- Captured snow is melted to measure its **water equivalent**, which helps assess possible flooding and increased river flow.
- Recording snow gauges can use Tipping Bucket, weighing, or float designs, but they must be heated so collected snow melts.

**Alternative snow measurements**

- A metered stick measures snow depth. With an assumed average snow density, depth can be converted to water equivalent.
- Networks of graduated stakes can be read remotely by telescope or helicopter.
- A snow corer is a hollow tube of known size. Its cross-sectional area, sample depth, and mass provide snow density and help determine the liquid water contained in the snow mass.

## Equations and Units

For a snow core with cross-sectional area $A$ and sampled depth $d$:

$$
V=A d
$$

$$
\rho=\frac{m}{V}
$$

> [!example] Worked snow-core pattern
> Given $A=10\ \text{cm}^2$, $d=50\ \text{cm}$, and $m=0.1\ \text{kg}=100\ \text{g}$:
> $$V=(10\ \text{cm}^2)(50\ \text{cm})=500\ \text{cm}^3$$
> $$\rho=\frac{100\ \text{g}}{500\ \text{cm}^3}=0.2\ \text{g/cm}^3$$

Keep mass and volume units compatible. A result in $\text{g/cm}^3$ requires mass in grams and volume in cubic centimetres.

## Common Confusions

- **Wind under-catch vs. evaporation loss:** wind diverts precipitation before collection; evaporation removes water after collection.
- **Horizontal to the ground vs. horizontal to a slope:** the course specifies orienting the opening relative to the local ground surface, even when that surface is angled.
- **Snowfall through the air vs. snow on the ground:** falling snow may be uniform, but post-event wind redistribution makes surface accumulation nonuniform.
- **Snow depth vs. water equivalent:** a ruler provides depth; collected and melted snow provides liquid-water equivalent.
- **Mass vs. volume:** the corer's area and depth determine volume; the measured mass is then divided by that volume.
