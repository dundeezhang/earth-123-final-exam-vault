# 4.1 Introduction to Precipitation Data and Rain Gauges

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

## Exam Focus

Know how precipitation is expressed, how study conditions control gauge-network design, the distinction between Primary and Co-operative networks, and the operating principles and limitations of non-recording, weighing, float, Tipping Bucket, and optical instruments.

## Core Notes

### Why precipitation data matters

- Data can be reported as depth (mm, cm, m), area ($m^2$, $km^2$), volume ($cm^3$, $m^3$, $km^3$), or rate (mm/hour, cm/day).
- Uses include detecting climate-change impacts, planning crop irrigation, forecasting weather, and designing stormwater-management systems.
- Rainfall can vary over very short distances. A dry location and a significant rainfall event may occur only a few streets apart, so one point gauge is not necessarily representative.
- Datasets may contain anomalous, erroneous, omitted, or missing values. Estimation and interpolation are needed when gauges fail or no gauge exists at a required location.

### Measuring rainfall

Three network-design considerations control rainfall measurement:

1. The study-area or watershed size and the purpose of the measurements.
2. The precipitation type and gauge design, chosen with precipitation, topography, and required accuracy in mind.
3. The number and distribution of gauges needed for the desired accuracy or data resolution.

Small areas require fewer gauges and may permit equidistant grid placement. Large watersheds may require many gauges over many square kilometres, with placement constrained by terrain, topography, private land, and access.

> [!important]
> Long-duration, large-area events are generally less spatially variable than short-duration, small-area events. Mountainous settings with local orographic storms therefore require especially careful, dense network design; open, flat regions may permit wider and more regular spacing.

Local topography, buildings, infrastructure, gauge design, and gauge location can still modify wind flow and local observations.

### Primary and Co-operative Networks

- A **Primary Network** contains official gauges with rigorous monitoring and maintenance by government authorities.
- A **Co-operative Network** contains many more official and unofficial stations. Individual stations may have less rigorous maintenance, but the greater number of records improves spatial coverage and contour resolution.
- **Figure 4.1.1:** Compare the sparse Primary Network with the denser Co-operative Network. The key lesson is the tradeoff between controlled station quality and much higher spatial resolution.

### Rain-gauge types

The simplest gauge is an upright hollow cylinder with a closed base. Gauges are divided into two broad categories.

**Non-recording gauges**

- Require a person to visit, measure, and record the sample.
- Commonly provide total precipitation over periods from a day or two to several months.
- Usually have a 3.5-5.0 inch (about 8-13 cm) opening and are mounted about 1 foot (about 30 cm) above the ground.
- Near-ground placement allows levelling and reduces regional wind and local wind-eddy interference.
- Disadvantages: labour and cost, difficult site maintenance, and inability to record rainfall intensity.
- **Figure 4.1.2:** Recognize the physical form and installation of a non-recording gauge.

**Recording gauges**

- Automatically generate a time-based digital record and can measure depth and intensity.
- **Weighing gauge:** a tared electric scale continuously measures added sample weight. Water density and collector dimensions allow weight to be converted to depth.
- **Float gauge:** an electric pulley records float height; the known collector dimensions and water depth provide volume and total precipitation depth.
- **Tipping Bucket gauge:** two balanced buckets tip alternately after receiving a known volume. A logger counts tips over time, allowing total depth or intensity to be calculated.
- **Figure 4.1.3:** Distinguish weighing, float, and Tipping Bucket mechanisms.
- **Figure 4.1.4:** Connect the outer funnel and cover of a real Tipping Bucket gauge to its internal tipping mechanism.

> [!warning]
> A Tipping Bucket does not record collected precipitation until the threshold volume causes a tip. A small event may be missed entirely, and the recorded start or end of an event may be shifted.

Radar and weather satellites extend remote measurement to local and global scales. When particle characteristics matter, an **optical disdrometer** measures rain or snow particle size, shape, velocity, and precipitation intensity.

- **Figure 4.1.5:** Know that the optical disdrometer provides particle-level measurements that ordinary collection gauges do not.

## Equations and Units

Rainfall intensity is precipitation depth divided by elapsed time:

$$
I=\frac{P}{\Delta t}
$$

Use the readings that belong to the requested interval. If each timestamp reports the **preceding** 15 minutes, the 08:00-09:00 interval uses the 08:15, 08:30, 08:45, and 09:00 rows.

> [!example] Worked Tipping Bucket pattern
> The source values for those four rows are $0.0$, $0.4$, $0.2$, and $0.4$ mm. Therefore,
> $$P=0.0+0.4+0.2+0.4=1.0\ \text{mm}$$
> $$I=\frac{1.0\ \text{mm}}{1\ \text{h}}=1.0\ \text{mm/h}$$

## Common Confusions

- **Total depth is not intensity:** a total becomes an intensity only after division by its time interval.
- **A timestamp may label the prior interval:** do not include the 08:00 reading in an 08:00-09:00 sum when each reading covers the preceding 15 minutes.
- **Recording does not mean error-free:** automatic gauges add temporal detail but retain mechanical, placement, and design limitations.
- **More stations do not guarantee equal quality:** the Co-operative Network improves coverage, while Primary Network stations receive more rigorous maintenance.
- **Tipping Bucket threshold vs. zero rain:** no tip can mean that some precipitation was collected but did not reach the bucket threshold.
