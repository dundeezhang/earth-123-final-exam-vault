# 4.3 Quantitative Analysis of Precipitation Data

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

## Exam Focus

Choose and apply the correct technique for missing point data, long-term record changes, equal-weight areal averaging, contour-based volume, and area-weighted Thiessen estimation. Show unit conversions and include only gauges or subareas that belong to the study region.

## Core Notes

Precipitation datasets support engineering and infrastructure design, water-supply and demand analysis, agriculture, local and regional water balances, and climate-change studies. Analyses may examine means, maxima, minima, trends, event frequency, extremes, and relationships between precipitation and topography, vegetation, or structures.

Common techniques are:

- interpolation at an ungauged or failed station;
- adjustment of long-term data after station, environment, instrument, or technique changes;
- estimation of average precipitation over a defined area and time; and
- observation of how precipitation form and environment affect areal distribution and volume.

### Interpolation

Gauges cannot cover every location. Missing or erroneous point data can be estimated from reliable neighbouring gauges, particularly when long-term historical records are available.

For an unknown station $x$ and $n$ reliable stations:

$$
P_x=\frac{N_x}{n}\left(\frac{P_1}{N_1}+\frac{P_2}{N_2}+\cdots+\frac{P_n}{N_n}\right)
$$

- $P_i$: recorded precipitation at reliable station $i$.
- $P_x$: missing precipitation at the target station.
- $N_i$ and $N_x$: long-term historic precipitation at the reliable and target stations.
- $n$: number of reliable stations in the bracketed sum.

> [!example] Course interpolation example
> Station 2 has $N_2=52$ cm. The three reliable station pairs $(P_i,N_i)$ are $(40,45)$, $(58,65)$, and $(37,40)$ cm.
> $$
> P_2=\frac{52}{3}\left(\frac{40}{45}+\frac{58}{65}+\frac{37}{40}\right)=46.91\ \text{cm}\approx47\ \text{cm}
> $$

An isohyetal contour map can also provide a visual, less accurate interpolation.

*Figure 4.3.1: Isohyetal contour map used to estimate precipitation at point A (Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo).*

**What to notice:** Point A lies between the 0.75 and 0.80 cm contours. Its position relative to nearby values supports an estimate of about 0.78 cm.

### Adjustment of Long-Term Data

Vegetation growth, new buildings, population growth, relocation, or a change in instrument or measurement technique can alter a station record over decades. A station-versus-neighbour average plot can expose the change as a break in slope.

![Double-mass plot showing a change in station trend](../../Assets/Module%2004/impact-moved-gauge-dataset_5.jpg)

*Figure 4.3.2: Mock dataset showing the effect of moving or updating a gauging station (Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo).*

**What to notice:** The slope changes from 0.98 before the move to 0.65 after it. The break marks a change in collection conditions, not an abrupt regional precipitation shift.

- The course example uses a pre-change slope of 0.98 and a post-change slope of 0.65:

$$
K=\frac{\text{slope after change}}{\text{slope before change}}=\frac{0.65}{0.98}=0.66
$$

Multiply the pre-change observations by 0.66 to bring them into line with the current, post-change trend. The process can be reversed if the goal is to place new data on the old basis.

### Estimation: Arithmetic Average

The arithmetic method gives every gauge inside the study area equal weight:

$$
\bar P=\frac{p_1+p_2+\cdots+p_G}{G}
$$

It is quick and can be accurate in flat areas with relatively uniform precipitation. Rough terrain, varying topography, and large regions can produce substantial errors.

![Gauges inside and outside an arithmetic-average study area](../../Assets/Module%2004/arithmetic-average_5.png)

*Figure 4.3.3: Arithmetic-average example with gauging stations inside and outside the study area (Dingman, 2002, p. 121).*

**What to notice:** Only the gauges labelled 2, 4, and 5 fall inside the boundary, so those three values receive equal weight. Outside gauges must be excluded even though they appear on the map.

> [!example] Figure 4.3.3
> The included readings are 2, 4, and 5 units:
> $$\bar P=\frac{2+4+5}{3}=3.67\ \text{units}$$

![Drainage basin gauge readings for arithmetic averaging](../../Assets/Module%2004/drainage-basin_5.jpg)

*Course practice diagram: Rain-gauge readings within and around a drainage basin (Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo).*

**What to notice:** Gauges 2, 3, and 4 lie inside the main watershed boundary, while gauges 1 and 5 lie outside. Apply the arithmetic average only to the three included readings.

### Estimation: Isohyetal Method

An isohyetal map combines gauge observations into contours of equal precipitation depth. For each subregion between two contours:

1. Calculate the area between the bounding contours.
2. Average the two bounding contour depths.
3. Convert depth to a length unit compatible with area.
4. Compute subregion volume as area times mean depth.
5. Sum all subregion volumes for the total region.

![Gauge points and precipitation belts on an isohyetal map](../../Assets/Module%2004/isohyetal-contour-map_5.jpg)

*Figure 4.3.4: Isohyetal map with gauging stations and equal-precipitation contours (Dingman, 2002, p. 125).*

**What to notice:** Dots are gauges, contour lines are isohyets, and each belt lies between two precipitation depths. The shaded belt's area is paired with the mean of its bounding isohyets.

![Highlighted belt between 4.0 and 4.5 cm isohyets](../../Assets/Module%2004/isohyetal-contour-map-precipitation-values-cm_5.png)

*Figure 4.3.5: Isohyetal contour map with precipitation values in centimetres (Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo).*

**What to notice:** The highlighted $25\ \text{m}^2$ belt is bounded by 4.0 and 4.5 cm contours, so its representative depth is their mean. Convert that depth to metres before calculating volume.

> [!example] Figure 4.3.5
> A highlighted $25\ \text{m}^2$ region lies between 4.0 and 4.5 cm contours.
> $$\bar P=\frac{4.0+4.5}{2}=4.25\ \text{cm}=0.0425\ \text{m}$$
> $$V=(25\ \text{m}^2)(0.0425\ \text{m})=1.0625\ \text{m}^3\approx1.06\ \text{m}^3$$

### Estimation: Thiessen Polygon Method

Thiessen polygons provide an area-weighted estimate based on the spacing of neighbouring gauges.

1. Connect each gauge to every adjacent gauge with straight lines.
2. Draw perpendicular bisectors at the midpoint of the connecting lines; these form a polygon around each gauge.
3. Calculate each polygon's area as a percentage or fraction of the total area.
4. Multiply the gauge depth by its polygon's area fraction.
5. Sum all weighted contributions.

![Thiessen polygons around precipitation gauges](../../Assets/Module%2004/thiessen-polygon-method_5.png)

*Figure 4.3.6: Thiessen Polygon Method for estimating areal precipitation (Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo).*

**What to notice:** Dashed lines connect adjacent gauges, while solid perpendicular bisectors form each gauge's polygon. A gauge receives the weight of its polygon's fraction of the total area.

- **Table 4.3.1:** Seven area fractions (30%, 10%, 5%, 15%, 5%, 30%, 5%) sum to 100%. Their weighted precipitation contributions (1.80, 0.70, 0.37, 0.93, 0.25, 1.95, 0.30) sum to 6.30. Because area fractions are dimensionless, the estimate retains the original precipitation-depth unit and should be read as **6.30 cm**. The saved page's label of "6.3 cm per square kilometre" is dimensionally inconsistent.

## Equations and Units

| Task | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Missing-station interpolation | $P_x=\dfrac{N_x}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{P_i}{N_i}$ |
| Adjustment factor | $K=\dfrac{\text{slope after}}{\text{slope before}}$ |
| Arithmetic average | $\bar P=\dfrac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}p_g$ |
| Mean depth between isohyets | $\bar P_j=\dfrac{P_{j,\mathrm{inner}}+P_{j,\mathrm{outer}}}{2}$ |
| Precipitation volume | $V=A\times\text{depth}$ |
| Thiessen area-weighted estimate | $\bar P=\sum_i w_iP_i$, with $\sum_iw_i=1$ |

Unit checks:

- $1\ \text{cm}=0.01\ \text{m}$, so divide centimetres by 100 before multiplying by $\text{m}^2$.
- $\text{m}^2\times\text{m}=\text{m}^3$.
- Convert percentages to fractions before weighting: $30\%=0.30$.
- The weighted Thiessen result retains the precipitation-depth unit.

## Common Confusions

- **Interpolation vs. averaging:** interpolation estimates a missing point; arithmetic, isohyetal, and Thiessen methods estimate an area.
- **Current precipitation $P$ vs. normal precipitation $N$:** use the recorded event or annual totals in the numerator and each station's long-term normal in the denominator.
- **Number of map gauges vs. included gauges:** arithmetic averaging uses only stations inside the selected region.
- **Isohyet value vs. belt value:** the depth assigned to a belt is the average of its two bounding contours.
- **Depth vs. volume:** a weighted average can remain a depth; an actual volume requires multiplication by total area with compatible units.
- **Adjustment-factor direction:** after/before corrects old observations toward the new basis; reversing the target basis requires reversing the correction.
