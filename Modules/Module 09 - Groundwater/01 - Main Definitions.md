# Main Definitions

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]
Module: [[00 - Module 09 Overview]]

| Term | Definition | Course section | Example / where / application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Groundwater | Water beneath the land surface that fully saturates pores or voids in the ground. | 9.1; Week 9 glossary | In the Okanagan cross-section, groundwater saturates pore spaces in buried sand and gravel and occupies bedrock fractures. |
| Water table | The upper surface of the saturated groundwater zone. | 9.1, water-cycle and Okanagan groundwater figures; 9.3 | Water levels in a stream-valley piezometer array locate the water table across the valley. |
| Unsaturated zone | Subsurface region above the water table whose voids are not fully water-filled. | 9.1-9.3 | Beneath a losing stream separated from the water table, seepage crosses partly air-filled sediment before reaching groundwater. |
| Infiltration | Downward entry of surface water into the ground. | 9.1; 9.5 | Rain and snowmelt infiltrate at the surface of the Oak Ridges Moraine. |
| Percolation | Downward subsurface flow under gravity from the unsaturated zone to the water table or saturated zone. | 9.1; Week 9 glossary | After entering moraine soil, water percolates downward toward a saturated sand-and-gravel aquifer. |
| Recharge | Addition of water to a groundwater reservoir, commonly by infiltrating precipitation or snowmelt. | 9.1-9.2; 9.5 | Infiltrating rain and snowmelt recharge the glacial aquifers of the Oak Ridges Moraine. |
| Discharge zone | Place where groundwater leaves the subsurface system, such as a spring, stream, river, or lake. | 9.3; 9.5 | Lower-elevation outlets on the Oak Ridges Moraine discharge groundwater to springs, streams, and rivers. |
| Aquifer | Permeable subsurface layer that readily stores and transmits groundwater. | 9.2; Week 9 glossary | Layered sand-and-gravel aquifers in the Waterloo Moraine store and transmit groundwater beneath the university area. |
| Aquitard | Very-low-permeability layer that restricts water movement into or out of an aquifer. | 9.2-9.3; Week 9 glossary | A clay aquitard in the Waterloo Moraine cross-section restricts vertical exchange between aquifer layers. |
| Confining layer | Layer with little to no permeability that essentially blocks groundwater flow. | 9.2; Week 9 glossary | The low-permeability material over Waterloo Moraine Aquifer 3 caps and pressurizes the deep groundwater. |
| Unconfined aquifer | Aquifer open to the surface, without an overlying aquitard, and with a shallow water table. | 9.2, Unconfined Aquifer | Waterloo Moraine Aquifer 1 is the shallow, surface-connected aquifer in the course cross-section. |
| Confined aquifer | Aquifer overlain by an impermeable layer or layers and commonly under pressure at depth. | 9.2, Confined Aquifer | Waterloo Moraine Aquifer 3 lies beneath confining material and is identified as a possible drinking-water source. |
| Semi-confined aquifer | Leaky aquifer bounded on one or both sides by a semi-permeable layer that permits limited flow. | 9.2, Semi-Confined Aquifer | On the right side of the Waterloo Moraine cross-section, Aquifer 2 leaks through a semi-permeable boundary. |
| Karst aquifer | Aquifer formed by dissolution of carbonate rock, with cracks, fractures, voids, caverns, or tunnels carrying water. | 9.2, Karst Aquifers | At Walkerton, fractures in karst bedrock linked contaminated shallow water near Well #5 to deeper supply aquifers. |
| Moraine | Surface accumulation of material pushed and deposited by glacial action; sand and gravel moraines can store groundwater. | 9.2, The Glacial Connection | The Oak Ridges Moraine stores infiltrated precipitation and feeds many springs, streams, and rivers. |
| Hydraulic gradient | Difference in hydraulic head between two measurement points divided by their distance; effectively the water-table slope. | 9.3; Week 9 glossary | Between Wells A and C, a 3 m head drop over 100 m gives $i=0.030$ toward C. |
| Perched water table | Local saturated zone held above the main water table by a low-permeability layer, described as an impermeable aquitard in the course example. | 9.3, perched water tables | A local clay lens can supply a shallow well while a nearby well must drill deeper to reach the regional water table. |
| Piezometer | Shallow groundwater well or hollow tube extending into the water table and used to measure groundwater level. | 9.3, Definition | Three piezometers across a stream valley provide water-level elevations for mapping the water table and its slope. |
| Porosity ($n$) | Ratio of void volume to total sample volume. | 9.3, phase relationships | The Wells A-to-C calculation uses $n=0.25$, meaning voids occupy 25% of the bulk material. |
| Void ratio ($e$) | Ratio of void volume to solid volume. | 9.3, phase relationships | On a soil phase diagram, $40\ \text{cm}^3$ of voids and $100\ \text{cm}^3$ of solids gives $e=0.40$. |
| Water content ($w$) | Ratio of water mass to solid mass. | 9.3, phase relationships | A soil sample is weighed wet and dry so water mass can be compared with the remaining solid mass. |
| Degree of saturation ($S_r$) | Ratio of water volume to void volume. | 9.3, phase relationships | A phase diagram with every void water-filled gives $S_r=1$, matching saturated material below the water table. |
| Bulk density ($\rho$) | Total mass divided by total volume, measured in $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. | 9.3, phase relationships | A hydrologist divides a field sample's total wet mass by its bulk volume to obtain density. |
| Specific yield | Proportion of water that drains from and can be extracted from a sample. | 9.3, Table 9.3.1 | Course Table 9.3.1 shows sand yielding 22% of its bulk volume as drainable water. |
| Specific retention | Proportion of water retained within a sample after drainage. | 9.3, Table 9.3.1 | Course Table 9.3.1 shows clay retaining 48% of its bulk volume after drainage. |
| Permeability | Ability of soil or rock to transmit material through connected pore spaces. | 9.3; Week 8 glossary | Gravel transmits water readily through large connected pores, while clay retains water in small poorly connected pores. |
| Equipotential contour | Line joining locations with equal water-table elevation or hydraulic head. | 9.3, Figure 9.3.6 | A hydrologist connects equal heads measured by piezometers to map the hydraulic field around a contamination site. |
| Flow line | Predicted groundwater movement path; in homogeneous geology it crosses equipotential contours at right angles. | 9.3, Figure 9.3.6 | On the landfill map, a flow line traces the plume downgradient across equipotential contours toward the river. |
| Hydraulic head ($h$) | Water's potential energy at a measurement point; elevation head plus pressure head. | 9.4; Week 9 glossary | At Well A, the water-level elevation gives a hydraulic head of 398 m. |
| Elevation head ($z$) | Elevation of the measurement point above a selected datum. | 9.4; Week 9 glossary | The bottoms of Wells A, B, and C are 384 m above the selected datum. |
| Pressure head ($h_p$) | Height water rises above the measurement point because of pressure. | 9.4; Week 9 glossary | At Well A, water stands 14 m above the 384 m well bottom, so $h_p=14\ \text{m}$. |
| Potentiometric surface | Geographic distribution of hydraulic heads, shown as a line or surface to which confined water can rise. | 9.4; Week 9 glossary | In the confined-aquifer figure, wells rise toward one pressure surface, which lies above ground at the flowing well. |
| Artesian well | Well in a confined aquifer where pressure raises water above the aquifer; it flows at the surface if head exceeds ground elevation. | 9.4, saved aquifer figures | The saved cross-section shows a flowing artesian well where the potentiometric surface stands above land surface. |
| Hydraulic conductivity ($K$) | Measure, in units of velocity, of how readily a material transmits water. | 9.4, saved conductivity figures; 9.5 | The Wells A-to-C calculation converts $K=2.5\times10^{-5}\ \text{m/s}$ to $2.16\ \text{m/day}$. |
| Darcy's Law | Relationship used to calculate water flow through a porous medium from $K$, area, and hydraulic gradient. | 9.4; Week 9 glossary | For Wells A to C, $q=Ki=(2.16)(0.030)=0.0648\ \text{m/day}$; multiplying by area would give $Q$. |
| Darcy specific discharge ($q$) | Flow per unit cross-sectional area, calculated in the Assignment 5 notes as $q=Ki$. | 9.4, Assignment 5 notes | The A-to-B interval has $q=(2.16)(0.020)=0.0432\ \text{m/day}$. |
| Groundwater velocity ($v$) | Average pore-water speed estimated by dividing specific discharge by porosity: $v=q/n$. | 9.4, Assignment 5 notes; 9.5 | For A to C, $v=0.0648/0.25=0.2592\ \mathrm{m/day}$, giving about 386 days to travel $100\ \mathrm{m}$. |
| Gaining stream | Stream replenished by groundwater; the water table meets the stream surface. | 9.5, Figure 9.5.2 | In the course cross-section, groundwater arrows point into the channel where the water table intersects the stream. |
| Losing stream | Stream that loses surface water to the subsurface while maintaining flow. | 9.5, Figure 9.5.2 | Stream water seeps through the bed to recharge groundwater while surface flow continues downstream. |
| Flow-through stream | Stream gaining groundwater on one side and losing water on the lower-gradient side. | 9.5, Figure 9.5.2 | In rapidly changing mountainous topography, groundwater can enter one bank and leave through the opposite lower-gradient bank. |
| Contaminant plume | Spreading body of contaminated groundwater moving downgradient. | 9.5, Figure 9.5.4 | The course landfill plume moves 100 m toward a river at about 50 cm/day, reaching it in roughly 200 days. |

## Commonly Confused Terms

- **Porosity vs. permeability:** porosity measures void volume; permeability depends strongly on whether voids connect. Clay can have high porosity but low permeability.
- **Specific yield vs. specific retention:** yield drains and can be collected; retention remains trapped. In Table 9.3.1, their percentages sum to porosity.
- **Hydraulic head vs. hydraulic gradient:** head is potential energy at a point; gradient is head change divided by distance.
- **Water table vs. potentiometric surface:** the water table is the top of an unconfined saturated zone; the potentiometric surface is the level to which pressured water would rise.
- **Confined vs. semi-confined:** a confined aquifer has an effectively impermeable boundary; a semi-confined aquifer leaks through a semi-permeable boundary.
- **Darcy specific discharge vs. groundwater velocity:** specific discharge is flow per bulk area; groundwater velocity accounts for flow through pore space by dividing by porosity.
- **Gaining vs. losing stream:** gaining receives groundwater; losing supplies groundwater.
