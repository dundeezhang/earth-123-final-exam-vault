# 9.2 Aquifers and the Glacial Connection

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 09 - Groundwater/00 - Module 09 Overview|Module 9 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Compare the four aquifer types, relate confining layers to pressure and contamination risk, and explain how glacial deposits and moraines created important southern Ontario groundwater stores.

## Core Notes

### Aquifers and geology

- Aquifers are subsurface regions that store and transmit groundwater.
- Many aquifers contain saturated sand and gravel deposited, buried, and modified by repeated glacial action.
- Karst aquifers are different: water occupies dissolution openings in carbonate rock rather than pore spaces among sand and gravel grains.

### Unconfined Aquifer

- Open to the ground surface and not covered by an impervious layer or aquitard.
- The water table occurs at shallow depth; a hole may slowly fill from its sides and bottom after intersecting it.
- Recharge commonly comes from precipitation and overland flow.
- Easy access makes these aquifers useful water sources, but proximity to the surface makes them highly contamination-prone.
- The course says they are not used as safe human drinking-water sources, although they may supply crops and some farm animals.

![Conceptual cross-section of an unconfined aquifer](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-2-1-unconfined-aquifer.png)

*Figure 9.2.1: A conceptual diagram of an unconfined aquifer. © Course Author and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** The water table is shallow and no low-permeability layer covers the aquifer. Surface recharge can reach it directly, which also leaves it exposed to contamination.

### Confined Aquifer

- Not exposed at the surface; an impermeable layer or sequence such as clay overlies it.
- The confining beds, or aquitards, strongly restrict vertical water flux and protect deeper water from surface contamination.
- The overlying materials act as a cap, and groundwater may also arrive from elevated recharge zones, producing pressure at lower elevation.
- The course identifies confined aquifers as the preferred class for fresh human drinking water.

![Unconfined and confined aquifers separated by a clay aquitard](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-2-2-confined-aquifers.png)

*Figure 9.2.2: A conceptual diagram showing what a series of unconfined and confined aquifers could look like. Note the confining bed or aquitard, shown as a clay layer, between aquifers. VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** Compare the shallow unconfined aquifer with the deeper confined aquifer. The clay aquitard restricts vertical flow and helps protect the deeper water from surface contamination.

### Semi-Confined Aquifer

- Also called a **leaky aquifer**.
- Bounded on one or both sides by a semi-permeable layer that allows limited flow instead of stopping it completely.
- Leakage can recharge an aquifer being pumped.
- Because leakage also permits surface contamination, the course considers these less desirable for human use than confined aquifers.

![Semi-confined aquifer with leakage through a semi-permeable boundary](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-2-3-semi-confined-aquifer.png)

*Figure 9.2.3: A conceptual diagram of a semi-confined aquifer. © Course Author and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Trace the limited flow through the semi-permeable boundary. Leakage can replenish a pumped aquifer, but it can also carry contaminants into the aquifer.

### Karst Aquifers

- Rainwater dissolves carbonate rock such as limestone, enlarging cracks, fractures, voids, caverns, and tunnels.
- Large openings can transport large amounts of water rapidly over long distances, like underground rivers.
- Rapid surface-to-subsurface transport also spreads contamination quickly and far.
- Collapse risk makes drilling and extraction less safe and can threaten surface buildings and infrastructure.

![Cross-section of karst terrain with dissolution openings and groundwater](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-2-4-karst-aquifer.png)

*Figure 9.2.4: A conceptual cross-section of karstic terrain and a karst aquifer. VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** Water occupies large voids and fractures in carbonate rock rather than granular pore spaces. These connected openings explain both rapid groundwater movement and rapid contaminant transport.

### The Glacial Connection

- Glaciers pushed soils, sands, and gravels into large deposits; later deposits buried some of them.
- Infiltrating water accumulated in voids until these deposits became saturated aquifers.
- At depth, water can be pressurized beneath overlying materials and accessed by drilling.
- Surface glacial deposits called **moraines** also store freshwater.
- On the Oak Ridges Moraine, infiltrating rain and snowmelt are slowed, combined, and channelled to lower-elevation outlets that become springs, streams, and rivers.
- The Waterloo Moraine underlies the University of Waterloo area and contains layered aquifer systems.

![Map of the Oak Ridges Moraine and rivers originating from it](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-2-5-oak-ridges-moraine-map.png)

*Figure 9.2.5: Map showing the location of the Oak Ridges Moraine in Ontario. Toronto Star, 2011.*

**What to notice:** Many mapped rivers originate directly on the moraine instead of the surrounding landscape. This pattern shows the moraine acting as a major recharge, storage, and discharge system.

![General cross-section of the layered Waterloo Moraine aquifer system](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-2-6-waterloo-moraine-cross-section.jpeg)

*Figure 9.2.6: General cross-section of the Waterloo Moraine. Blackport et al., 2014, p. 124.*

**What to notice:** Aquifer 1 is unconfined, Aquifer 2 changes from confined to semi-confined across the section, and Aquifer 3 is confined. Use the intervening aquitards to judge where deeper groundwater receives the most protection.

## Common Confusions

- An aquitard restricts flow; it is not the water-bearing aquifer itself.
- Semi-confined does not mean no exchange: leakage is its defining feature.
- Karst stores water in dissolution openings, not in granular pore spaces.
- High karst flow is not automatically desirable; it also means rapid contaminant transport and collapse hazards.
- A moraine can be a surface landform and a major groundwater-storage/recharge feature.
