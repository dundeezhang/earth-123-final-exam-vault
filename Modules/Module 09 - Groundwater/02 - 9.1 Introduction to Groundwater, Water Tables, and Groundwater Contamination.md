## Core Notes

### Groundwater in the hydrological cycle

- The course glossary defines groundwater as all water beneath the land surface that fully saturates pores or voids.
- The saved hydrological-cycle figure shows **infiltration** as downward flow into the ground, **percolation** as continued subsurface flow, and **groundwater** as subsurface flow and storage.
- Recharge moves water into groundwater storage. Groundwater can later discharge to springs, streams, rivers, lakes, and the ocean.
- The water table separates the unsaturated material above from the saturated material below.

### Reading a groundwater system

The saved Okanagan groundwater-system figure connects the following features:

- Upland recharge areas accept infiltration.
- Water tables slope through valley sides toward lower elevations.
- Water is stored in sand-and-gravel aquifers, buried gravel-sand aquifers, and fractures in bedrock.
- Groundwater emerges at springs and can recharge a river.
- Shallow dug wells, high-yield deep wells, low-yield bedrock wells, and valley-floor infrastructure intercept different parts of the system.

### Contamination

- Groundwater is connected to surface processes, so material carried by infiltrating or percolating water can enter groundwater storage.
- Subsurface geology controls storage and pathways: connected pores and fractures permit movement, while low-permeability layers impede it.

## Common Confusions

- Groundwater is not an isolated underground lake; the saved regional diagram shows storage in pores, granular aquifers, and rock fractures.
- Infiltration is entry into the ground; percolation is continued downward flow toward the saturated zone.
- The water table is a boundary surface, not the full thickness of groundwater storage.
- A surface contaminant does not remain at the surface when an infiltration or fracture pathway is available.
