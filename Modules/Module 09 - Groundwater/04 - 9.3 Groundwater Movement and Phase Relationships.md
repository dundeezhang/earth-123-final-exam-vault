# 9.3 Groundwater Movement and Phase Relationships

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]
Module: [[00 - Module 09 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Use water-table slope and piezometer data to infer groundwater movement, apply soil phase relationships, and distinguish porosity, permeability, specific yield, and specific retention.

## Core Notes

### Water-Table Controls on Movement

- Groundwater movement is generally controlled by topographic slope or by a hydraulic gradient analogous to water-table slope.
- Water-table depth varies with climate, soils, and rocks: it may be shallow in swamps and marshes or deep in deserts.
- In homogeneous geology, the water table roughly parallels surface topography.
- Water infiltrates downward to the water table, then percolates downslope toward an outlet such as a spring, stream, or lake unless an aquitard blocks it.
- Increasing pore pressure in deeper saturated zones can deflect downward flow upward toward lower pressure near the surface.

![Water table and groundwater flow paths beneath a sloping river valley](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-3-1-water-table-valley.png)

*Figure 9.3.1: A conceptual cross-sectional diagram of a water table in a sloped river valley. VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** The water table broadly follows the land surface, while groundwater paths curve through the subsurface toward the valley outlet. Deeper pressure can turn initially downward flow back upward near the stream.

### Hydraulic Gradient

Hydraulic gradient is the change in hydraulic head per unit distance, analogous to the slope of the water-table surface. Groundwater moves from higher hydraulic head toward lower hydraulic head; the gradient controls how rapidly head changes along that path.

$$
i=\frac{\Delta h}{L}
$$

The sign depends on the chosen coordinate direction. State flow direction by comparing heads, and use $|\Delta h|/L$ when a magnitude is requested.

### Perched Water Tables

- A local low-permeability layer, described as an impermeable aquitard in the course example, can trap water above the main water table.
- A well intercepting perched water may falsely suggest that the regional water table is shallow.
- A nearby well may miss the perched lens and require deeper, more expensive drilling and redesign.

![Perched water table, springs, and successful and unsuccessful wells](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-3-2-perched-water-table.png)

*Figure 9.3.2. A conceptual cross-sectional diagram of a perched water table, a successful well, and an unsuccessful well. Tarbuck & Lutgens, 2002, p. 308.*

**What to notice:** The local aquitard holds a perched lens above the regional water table and creates springs at its edges. A nearby well can miss this lens, so one shallow water level does not establish the regional water-table depth.

### Piezometers and Water-Table Behaviour

- A **piezometer** is a shallow groundwater well or hollow tube extending from the surface into the water table.
- Slits or holes at its base allow groundwater into the casing.
- An array measures water levels at several locations; elevation differences and spacing give hydraulic gradient.
- Arrays can show whether a water table is stable, rising, or falling.
- At one site, piezometers that intercept the same hydraulic zone should show similar water levels; horizontally separated piezometers may differ because of hydraulic gradient.
- Where pressure pushes groundwater toward the surface, piezometers of different depths can show the same near-surface water elevation.

![Three piezometers locating a water table beside a stream](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-3-3-three-piezometers.png)

*Figure 9.3.3. A conceptual cross-sectional drawing of a stream valley with three piezometers installed on the left bank. The dashed blue line shows the water table. © Course Author and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Compare the water-surface elevations across the three piezometers to locate the sloping water table. Their elevation difference and horizontal spacing provide the hydraulic gradient.

![Piezometer array showing downward groundwater movement](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-3-4-downward-flow-piezometers.png)

*Figure 9.3.4. A conceptual diagram showing downward movement of water using a local array of piezometers. © Course Author and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Progressively deeper water levels with piezometer depth indicate a downward hydraulic gradient. Equal levels in the deepest wells would instead show that they intercept the same water table.

### Phase Relationships

| Quantity | Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Porosity | $n=\dfrac{V_{\text{voids}}}{V_{\text{total}}}$ | Fraction of total volume occupied by voids. |
| Void ratio | $e=\dfrac{V_{\text{voids}}}{V_{\text{solids}}}$ | Void volume relative to solid volume. |
| Water content | $w=\dfrac{m_{\text{water}}}{m_{\text{solids}}}$ | Water mass relative to solid mass. |
| Degree of saturation | $S_r=\dfrac{V_{\text{water}}}{V_{\text{voids}}}$ | Fraction of void volume filled with water. |
| Bulk density | $\rho=\dfrac{m_{\text{total}}}{V_{\text{total}}}$ | Total mass per total volume, $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. |

![Soil sample diagram relating air, water, solids, mass, and volume](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-3-5-soil-phase-relationships.png)

*Figure 9.3.5. A conceptual diagram of a soil sample and the five most-used phase relationships in soil science. © Course Author and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Separate the sample into solid, water, and air volumes before choosing an equation. Porosity and void ratio use volume terms, while water content uses water mass relative to solid mass.

### Specific Yield, Retention, and Permeability

- **Specific yield** is the water collected after drainage through a sample.
- **Specific retention** is water remaining trapped in the sample.
- Porosity matters, but pore connection strongly controls flow.
- Clay has many small, poorly connected pores: high porosity but low permeability and high retention.
- Gravel has large, connected pores: high permeability and high yield despite lower porosity than clay.

**Table 9.3.1:**

| Material | Porosity (%) | Specific yield (%) | Specific retention (%) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Clay | 50 | 2 | 48 |
| Sand | 25 | 22 | 3 |
| Gravel | 20 | 19 | 1 |
| Limestone | 20 | 18 | 2 |
| Sandstone | 11 | 6 | 5 |
| Granite | 0.1 | 0.09 | 0.01 |
| Basalt | 11 | 8 | 3 |

### Groundwater Flow Maps

- Water-table elevations can be mapped as dashed equipotential contours; predicted paths are flow lines.
- In homogeneous geology, flow lines cross equipotential contours at right angles.
- Such maps help predict contaminant movement and direct mitigation.

![Map of piezometers, equipotential contours, and groundwater flow lines](../../Assets/Module%2009/9-3-6-equipotential-flow-map.png)

*Figure 9.3.6. A map of water-table depths or equipotential contours, shown with dashed red lines, and predicted flow lines, shown with solid black lines. © Course Author and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Each piezometer measurement helps define an equal-head contour. In homogeneous material, predicted groundwater paths cross those contours at right angles and point toward decreasing head.

## Equations and Units

- Phase ratios are dimensionless and may be reported as fractions or percentages.
- Bulk density uses $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$.
- Hydraulic gradient $dh/dl$ is dimensionless because both head and distance are lengths.
- For Table 9.3.1, porosity equals specific yield plus specific retention.

## Common Confusions

- Water-table depth is not necessarily ground-surface elevation, even when the surfaces are roughly parallel.
- A perched water table is local and discontinuous; it is not the regional water table.
- High porosity does not guarantee high permeability.
- Specific yield is not total porosity because some water is retained.
- Equipotential contours show equal head; flow lines show movement and cross those contours at $90^\circ$ in homogeneous material.
