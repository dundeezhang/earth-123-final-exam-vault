# 9.3 Groundwater Movement and Phase Relationships

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 09 - Groundwater/00 - Module 09 Overview|Module 9 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Use water-table slope and piezometer data to infer groundwater movement, apply soil phase relationships, and distinguish porosity, permeability, specific yield, and specific retention.

## Core Notes

### Water-table controls on movement

- Groundwater movement is generally controlled by topographic slope or by a hydraulic gradient analogous to water-table slope.
- Water-table depth varies with climate, soils, and rocks: it may be shallow in swamps and marshes or deep in deserts.
- In homogeneous geology, the water table roughly parallels surface topography.
- Water infiltrates downward to the water table, then percolates downslope toward an outlet such as a spring, stream, or lake unless an aquitard blocks it.
- Increasing pore pressure in deeper saturated zones can deflect downward flow upward toward lower pressure near the surface.

**Figure 9.3.1:** Follow a curved groundwater path beneath a sloping valley toward a stream or lake.

### Hydraulic gradient

The course defines hydraulic gradient as the slope of the water-table surface. Water tends to travel from high gradient/head toward low gradient/head.

$$
i=\frac{dh}{dl}
$$

The sign indicates direction; the gradient magnitude is rise/run.

### Perched water tables

- An impermeable local aquitard, such as clay, can trap water above the main water table.
- A well intercepting perched water may falsely suggest that the regional water table is shallow.
- A nearby well may miss the perched lens and require deeper, more expensive drilling and redesign.

**Figure 9.3.2:** Distinguish the perched lens and springs above its aquitard from the deeper true water table; compare successful and unsuccessful wells.

### Piezometers and water-table behaviour

- A **piezometer** is a shallow groundwater well or hollow tube extending from the surface into the water table.
- Slits or holes at its base allow groundwater into the casing.
- An array measures water levels at several locations; elevation differences and spacing give hydraulic gradient.
- Arrays can show whether a water table is stable, rising, or falling.
- If two deep wells intercept the same water table, their water-surface elevations should match.
- If pressure pushes groundwater toward the surface, piezometers of different depths can show the same near-surface water elevation.

**Figure 9.3.4:** Progressively deeper water levels indicate downward movement through an unsaturated subsurface; equal levels in the deepest wells would indicate interception of the water table.

### Phase relationships

| Quantity | Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Porosity | $n=\dfrac{V_{voids}}{V_{total}}$ | Fraction of total volume occupied by voids. |
| Void ratio | $e=\dfrac{V_{voids}}{V_{solids}}$ | Void volume relative to solid volume. |
| Water content | $w=\dfrac{m_{water}}{m_{solids}}$ | Water mass relative to solid mass. |
| Degree of saturation | $S_r=\dfrac{V_{water}}{V_{voids}}$ | Fraction of void volume filled with water. |
| Bulk density | $\rho=\dfrac{m_{total}}{V_{total}}$ | Total mass per total volume, $\text{kg}/\text{m}^3$. |

**Figure 9.3.5:** Relate water, air, solids, voids, mass, and volume to the five equations.

### Specific yield, retention, and permeability

- **Specific yield** is the water collected after drainage through a sample.
- **Specific retention** is water remaining trapped in the sample.
- Porosity matters, but pore connection strongly controls flow.
- Clay has many small, poorly connected pores: high porosity but low permeability and high retention.
- Gravel has large, connected pores: high permeability and high yield despite lower porosity than clay.

**Table 9.3.1:**

| Material | Porosity (%) | Specific yield (%) | Specific retention (%) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Clay | 50 | 2 | 48 |
| Sand | 25 | 22 | 3 |
| Gravel | 20 | 19 | 1 |
| Limestone | 20 | 18 | 2 |
| Sandstone | 11 | 6 | 5 |
| Granite | 0.1 | 0.09 | 0.01 |
| Basalt | 11 | 8 | 3 |

### Groundwater flow maps

- Water-table elevations can be mapped as dashed equipotential contours; predicted paths are flow lines.
- In homogeneous geology, flow lines cross equipotential contours at right angles.
- Such maps help predict contaminant movement and direct mitigation.

**Figure 9.3.6:** Piezometer measurements produce equipotential contours and perpendicular flow lines.

## Equations and Units

- Phase ratios are dimensionless and may be reported as fractions or percentages.
- Bulk density uses $\text{kg}/\text{m}^3$.
- Hydraulic gradient $dh/dl$ is dimensionless because both head and distance are lengths.
- For Table 9.3.1, porosity equals specific yield plus specific retention.

## Common Confusions

- Water-table depth is not necessarily ground-surface elevation, even when the surfaces are roughly parallel.
- A perched water table is local and discontinuous; it is not the regional water table.
- High porosity does not guarantee high permeability.
- Specific yield is not total porosity because some water is retained.
- Equipotential contours show equal head; flow lines show movement and cross those contours at $90^\circ$ in homogeneous material.
