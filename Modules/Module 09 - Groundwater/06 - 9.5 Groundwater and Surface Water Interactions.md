# 9.5 Groundwater and Surface Water Interactions

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 09 - Groundwater/00 - Module 09 Overview|Module 9 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Classify groundwater-stream interactions, identify contamination pathways, and calculate contaminant velocity and travel time using hydraulic conductivity, porosity, and hydraulic gradient.

## Core Notes

### Recharge and discharge

- Groundwater and surface water are interconnected parts of the hydrological cycle.
- Precipitation infiltrates and recharges groundwater. Before infiltrating, runoff can cross roads, buildings, and parking lots and collect contaminants.
- Some subsurface environments filter contaminants naturally, but not all do.
- Groundwater returns to the surface at springs and supplies streams, rivers, and lakes.

**Figure 9.5.1:** Follow recharge from the surface into groundwater and discharge from groundwater back to surface-water bodies.

### Four stream interactions

1. **Gaining stream:** groundwater replenishes the stream; the water-table elevation equals the stream surface.
2. **Losing stream:** surface water infiltrates and recharges groundwater while the stream continues to flow.
3. **Losing stream above a perched/separated water table:** water leaves the stream more slowly and then moves away through the unsaturated subsurface.
4. **Flow-through stream:** groundwater enters on one side and leaves on the lower-gradient side. The course calls this rare in nature but notes it in mountainous, rapidly changing topography.

**Figure 9.5.2:** Determine gaining or losing behaviour from the relative water-table and stream elevations and arrow directions.

### Contamination pathways

Common examples listed by the course:

- Bacteria or viruses in agricultural runoff or from mismanaged septic systems.
- Excess nutrients from fertilizer use.
- Saltwater incursion or infiltration in coastal settings.
- Winter road salt. Waterloo averages about **6,500 tonnes each winter**, up to **210 kg per kilometre of road**.
- Industrial chemical spills and disposal sites.
- Pesticides and pharmaceuticals from agriculture, lawns and gardens, and wastewater treatment plants.

**Figure 9.5.3:** Surface and subsurface pollution sources can affect both groundwater and surface water through connected pathways.

### Contaminant velocity

The course modifies Darcy's flux to estimate pore-water or plume velocity without needing total impacted-area dimensions:

$$
v=-\left(\frac{K}{n}\right)\left(\frac{dh}{dl}\right)
$$

where $v$ is groundwater velocity, $K$ is hydraulic conductivity, $n$ is porosity, and $dh/dl$ is hydraulic gradient. The negative sign denotes movement toward decreasing head; use the magnitude for travel speed.

### Worked example: landfill plume

Given:

- $K=10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}$
- $n=35\%=0.35$
- Head drop $=2\ \text{m}$ over $100\ \text{m}$, so $|dh/dl|=0.02$
- River distance $=100\ \text{m}$

$$
|v|=\left(\frac{10^{-4}}{0.35}\right)(0.02)=5.7\times10^{-6}\ \text{m/s}
$$

This is approximately **50 cm/day**, so the plume takes about **200 days** to travel $100\ \text{m}$.

**Figure 9.5.4:** Use equipotential values on the map to calculate a 2% gradient and trace the plume from the landfill toward the river.

### Course check calculation

For $K=1.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}$, $n=0.40$, and a $4/100=0.04$ gradient:

$$
|v|=\left(\frac{1.5\times10^{-3}}{0.40}\right)(0.04)=1.5\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}
$$

## Equations and Units

- Convert porosity percent to a decimal before calculation.
- Hydraulic gradient is dimensionless.
- $K$ and $v$ use velocity units, commonly $\text{m/s}$.
- Travel time follows $t=L/|v|$ after all units are made consistent.

## Common Confusions

- Gaining and losing describe the stream's water balance, not whether the surrounding aquifer is desirable.
- A losing stream can still maintain downstream surface flow.
- A flow-through stream gains and loses water simultaneously on opposite sides.
- Darcy specific discharge and pore-water velocity differ because actual flow is restricted to pore space.
- The minus sign gives direction toward lower head; travel-time calculations use speed magnitude.
