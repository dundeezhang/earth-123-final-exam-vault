# 12.2 Evidence, Data, and Impacts of Climate Change

Up: [[Modules/Module 12 - Climate Change and Hydrology/00 - Module 12 Overview|Module 12 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Know the independent evidence streams, their key values and timescales, how models test causation, and the major hydrological and societal impacts discussed in the course.

## Core Notes

### Evidence and Data

- Natural controls such as Milankovitch cycles, plate tectonics, and volcanism act over long periods and cannot explain the speed of current change; the course identifies human greenhouse-gas emissions from industry, energy, and transportation as the missing cause.
- The course states that anthropogenic warming since the Industrial Revolution has nearly reached **+1.5°C** and that a further rise of only a few degrees would disrupt the narrow climatic range on which ecosystems and agriculture depend.
- Childhood memories are poor climate evidence because people rarely record complete values and tend to remember extreme snowstorms, droughts, cold snaps, or warm periods.
- Instrumental data have been collected since the **1860s**, beginning with a weather station at the Royal Society in London. Modern evidence also uses digital thermometers, satellites, photography, GIS, and ocean buoys.

### Atmospheric CO$_2$: Keeling Curve

- Figure 12.2.1 records monthly atmospheric CO$_2$ at Mauna Loa from **1958 to July 2022**; the record is named for Dr. Charles Keeling.
- Mauna Loa is high in the atmosphere, remote from major industrial sources, and surrounded by the Pacific Ocean, so it samples well-mixed air. Other stations show the same trend.
- July 2022 CO$_2$ was just under **420 ppm**, about **140 ppm** above the preindustrial natural level of **280 ppm**.
- The long-term record rises year over year. Its annual saw-tooth pattern reflects seasonal Northern Hemisphere vegetation; the ocean-dominated Southern Hemisphere has less land and fewer trees and does not cancel that pattern.

### Ice Cores and Past Climate Proxies

- Greenland and Antarctic ice cores preserve bubbles of ancient atmosphere. Ice depth estimates age; extracted bubble air gives the CO$_2$ concentration at that time.
- Figure 12.2.2 combines ice-core CO$_2$ with direct measurements after 1958 across **800,000 years** and shows how anomalous modern concentrations are.
- Other saved-page proxies are rock and sediment records, changes in soil and vegetation distribution, $^{18}\mathrm{O}$ and $^{16}\mathrm{O}$ ratios in fossil marine shells, and fossils of organisms restricted to narrow climate ranges.

### Atmospheric Temperatures and Attribution

- Atmospheric temperatures have been recorded globally for over **150 years**. Land datasets fluctuate and differ in detail but share an upward trend (Figure 12.2.3); satellite datasets show the same trend (Figure 12.2.4).
- A temperature increase alone does not identify its cause. Scientists therefore compare observations with climate models.
- Natural-only model runs do not match modern observed temperatures. Models including both natural processes and human-released greenhouse gases match much more closely (Figure 12.2.5), supporting the conclusion that natural processes are not the sole cause and humans are the main cause of current change.
- The course says the roughly ten warmest years in the record all occurred since **1998**. In its saved 2022 dataset, **2021 ranked sixth**, while **2016 and 2020** ranked hottest (Figure 12.2.6).

### Ocean Temperatures and Heat Content

- Thousands of **ARGO** floats measure temperature, ocean heat content, salinity, pressure, and current direction throughout the oceans.
- The upward trend occurs at the surface and at greater depths.
- Ocean heat content is accumulated energy, not water temperature, although more heat content raises overall water temperature. Figure 12.2.7 uses $10^{21}$ J.
- The course compares that scale with about $10^{16}$ J from a 1-megaton nuclear warhead.

### Climate Change Impacts

| Impact | Course outcome |
|---|---|
| Sea-level rise | Loss of low-lying land and possible saltwater intrusion into coastal freshwater aquifers, especially for coastal cities and small island nations. |
| Disease vectors | Mosquitoes and ticks can expand into formerly cold areas; mosquitoes spread malaria in warmer regions. |
| Heat waves | More deaths, especially among poor and elderly people in developing countries. |
| Ecosystems | Altered growing, flowering, and migration patterns; rapid habitat change can contribute to extinction. |
| Agriculture | Potentially longer, more productive seasons nearer the poles, but reduced production in tropical and equatorial regions. |
| Atmospheric hazards | Scientists expect greater severity, although the hurricane and tornado count records shown do not yet have a clear dramatic trend. |

### Polar and Alpine Ice Loss

- Ice volumes are declining at both poles and in mountain regions.
- Frozen freshwater from South American and Himalayan ice supplies developing regions; unanticipated loss could leave millions struggling to obtain safe freshwater.
- Reduced Northern Hemisphere ice could open the Northwest Passage for shorter Europe-Asia shipping, lowering travel time, fuel use, cost, and emissions on those trips.

### Rise in Sea Levels

- Natural tides, winds, and topography must be separated from the long-term signal.
- Global sea level is rising at almost **4 mm/year**, measured by satellites and direct coastal observations (Figure 12.2.8).
- The early twentieth-century rate was approximately **1-2 mm/year**; the current rate has roughly doubled and is accelerating.
- About half the world’s population lives within **100 km** of a coastline.
- The saved page reports projections of at least **60 cm by 2100**, with a worst-case rise of **over 3 m**.

### Atmospheric Hazards

- Geological hazards such as earthquakes and volcanic eruptions are not affected by climate change.
- The course expects added energy to increase the severity of atmospheric and related hazards.
- Atlantic hurricane counts from **1967-2021** (Figure 12.2.9) are not yet clearly increasing or decreasing, despite an expectation of more large storms.
- U.S. tornado totals from **1950-2022** (Figure 12.2.10; reference line **144.7 tornadoes/year**) show at most a slight annual increase, not a dramatic overall change.

## Equations and Units

For a constant-rate baseline,

$$
\Delta h = rt
$$

where $\Delta h$ is sea-level change, $r$ is rise rate, and $t$ is elapsed time. At $r=4\ \mathrm{mm/year}$:

- $10$ years: $4\times10=40\ \mathrm{mm}=4\ \mathrm{cm}$.
- $100$ years: $4\times100=400\ \mathrm{mm}=40\ \mathrm{cm}$.
- $1000$ years: $4\times1000=4000\ \mathrm{mm}=4\ \mathrm{m}$.

> [!warning]
> These reproduce the saved page’s constant-rate examples, not forecasts. The same page says the rate is accelerating, so a linear extrapolation can underestimate future rise.

- Concentration: ppm; July 2022 increase above preindustrial level was approximately $420-280=140\ \mathrm{ppm}$.
- Ocean heat-content axis: $10^{21}\ \mathrm{J}$. Course comparison: about $10^{16}\ \mathrm{J}$ for a 1-megaton nuclear warhead.

## Common Confusions

- **Weather vs. climate evidence:** memories of individual events do not replace complete, long-term measurements.
- Seasonal CO$_2$ decline does not reverse the long-term rise.
- **Warming vs. attribution:** temperature records show warming; the natural-only versus natural-plus-human model comparison tests its cause.
- **Ocean temperature vs. heat content:** Figure 12.2.7 measures energy in joules, not temperature, though the quantities are related.
- **Hazard count vs. severity:** unclear hurricane and tornado count trends do not contradict the lesson’s expectation of increasing severity.
- **Constant rate vs. projection:** $4\ \mathrm{mm/year}$ is useful for arithmetic, but the course explicitly says the rate is accelerating.
