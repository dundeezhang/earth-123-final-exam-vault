# 12.1 Introduction to Climate Change and Greenhouse Gases

Up: [[Modules/Module 12 - Climate Change and Hydrology/00 - Module 12 Overview|Module 12 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Distinguish climate from weather, explain the greenhouse effect, compare natural climate drivers with present anthropogenic forcing, and identify the documented hydrological changes.

## Core Notes

### Climate Change and the Greenhouse Effect

- The course attributes present climate alteration mainly to human-produced CO$_2$ and other greenhouse gases from industry, energy, and transportation.
- Added CO$_2$ and CH$_4$ increase average land- and ocean-surface temperatures and are linked to migration and habitat disruption, freshwater-ice loss, and declining surface-water sources.
- Solar energy enters the atmosphere and warms land and water. Greenhouse-gas molecules impede radiant heat as it attempts to escape to space, leaving more energy in the atmosphere and raising air, land, and sea temperatures.
- Greenhouse-gas research extends from Eunice Foote in the early nineteenth century to the creation of the IPCC in 1988.
- Observed biological responses listed by the course include later or halted migration, longer flowering and growing seasons, and species expansion into new areas.

### Climate vs. Weather

- Climate change refers to patterns lasting at least **three decades**.
- Month-to-month and even year-to-year variations are weather.
- Individual cold spells or heat waves cannot establish a climate trend by themselves.

### Natural Climate Drivers

| Driver | Mechanism | Relevance to present warming |
|---|---|---|
| Solar output | Changes energy entering Earth’s system. | Satellite-era output has shown slight cooling, not the observed warming direction. |
| Orbit | Eccentricity, obliquity, and precession alter insolation over long timescales. | Current orbital position should exert slight cooling. |
| Plate tectonics | Continental positions alter ocean currents and long-term heat transport. | Plate movement is too slow to explain rapid modern warming. |
| Volcanism | Volcanic ash, dust, and fine particles can reflect incoming solar energy and cool the surface. | Humans emit about **60 times more CO$_2$ each year** than all volcanic eruptions combined; the 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption caused about **0.5°C** cooling for **3-4 years**. |

### Anthropogenic Forcing

- CO$_2$ and CH$_4$, identified by the course as the dominant climate-influencing gases, have increased sharply since the Industrial Revolution, approximately 1750.
- Major sources include industry, energy, and transportation.
- Natural drivers continue to operate, but their magnitude, timing, or direction does not explain the observed rapid warming.
- The saved page also lists nitrous oxide (printed there as NO$_2$) and chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) as less impactful greenhouse gases.

**What to notice:** The greenhouse mechanism was understood well before modern climate models. Burning carbon-rich fuel adds CO$_2$ to the atmosphere, which reduces the rate at which outgoing long-wave energy escapes to space.

### Hydrological Changes

Reducing incoming solar energy would reduce global evapotranspiration and the water vapour available for precipitation. More energy can increase global evapotranspiration and atmospheric water vapour, which can increase precipitation. The relationship is complex: for example, increased evaporation and cloud cover could reduce temperatures in some tropical regions.

![Conceptual hydrological cycle showing major water storages and flows](../../Assets/Module%2012/12.1.2-hydrological-cycle.png)

*Figure 12.1.2. Conceptual hydrological cycle. Source: VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** Solar energy drives evaporation and transpiration, while gravity moves precipitation, runoff, and groundwater toward lower elevations. Climate warming can therefore change several connected terms at once, including atmospheric moisture, snow storage, runoff timing, and evapotranspiration.

Documented changes listed in the course:

- More frequent and intense heavy precipitation, including some regions with declining total precipitation.
- Reduced snow cover, especially in spring and summer.
- Higher lake-water temperatures.
- Shorter annual lake and river ice-cover duration in Northern Hemisphere middle and high latitudes.
- Increased actual ET over many dry regions of the United States from 1950 to 2000.
- Increased annual runoff at high latitudes and in some U.S. regions, with decreases in West Africa, southern Europe, and southern South America.
- More winter precipitation falling as rain and altered river-flow timing.
- Earlier snowmelt.
- Fewer frost, cold-day, and cold-night events, with more hot days and nights.
- A decreased diurnal temperature range from 1950 to 2004, followed by little change from 1979 to 2004 because maximum and minimum temperatures rose at similar rates.

## Common Confusions

- Major hurricanes are not climate drivers; they are potential responses to a warmer system.
- A decrease in diurnal temperature **range** does not mean maximum and minimum temperatures are falling.
- The saved page prints nitrous oxide as NO$_2$; retain that as the course-page wording rather than silently substituting a different formula.
- Natural climate variation does not disprove anthropogenic warming.
