# Main Definitions

| Term | Definition | Course section | Example / where / application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water balance | A quantitative accounting of the inflows and outflows of water in a defined system. | 7.1, Introduction | Figure 7.1.2 balances six lake fluxes to obtain a net gain of $16{,}001\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$. |
| System | The bounded subject of a water balance; examples range from a plant or lysimeter to a lake, watershed, ocean, or Earth. | 7.1, Introduction | Applied Problem 3 treats the $10\ \text{km}^2$ Hydro Lake in the Waterloo Region Grand River watershed as the bounded system. |
| Input / inflow | Water entering the system, represented in the lake balance by $P$, $Q_{\text{in}}$, and $G_{\text{in}}$. | 7.1, Calculating a Water Balance | Figure 7.1.2 supplies the lake with $10\ \text{mm/day}$ of rain, $25\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ of streamflow, and $1\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ of groundwater. |
| Output / outflow | Water leaving the system, represented by $ET$, $Q_{\text{out}}$, and $G_{\text{out}}$. | 7.1, Calculating a Water Balance | Figure 7.1.2 removes $2\ \text{mm/day}$ by $ET$, $24\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ at the outlet, and $1\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ through groundwater. |
| Storage, $S$ | Water held within the system. Its volume rises or falls as inputs and outputs fluctuate. | 7.1, Introduction | In the hypothetical lake in Figure 7.1.1, the water already occupying the lake is storage. |
| Change in storage, $\Delta S$ | Total inputs minus total outputs over the selected time interval; positive values are increases and negative values are decreases. | 7.1, Calculating a Water Balance; Water Balance Example | The Figure 7.1.2 calculation gives $\Delta S=+16{,}001\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$, so lake storage increases. |
| Precipitation, $P$ | The precipitation input to the system, initially measured as a water depth per unit time in the examples. | 7.1, Calculating a Water Balance | Five October measurements at Hydro Lake, $35$, $42$, $38$, $47$, and $43\ \text{mm}$, are averaged to $41\ \text{mm}$. |
| Evapotranspiration, $ET$ | The water output attributed to evapotranspiration, initially measured as a water depth per unit time in the examples. | 7.1, Calculating a Water Balance | Hydro Lake's October $ET$ of $25\ \text{mm}$ becomes $250{,}000\ \text{m}^3$, or $336.022\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$. |
| Surface-water inflow, $Q_{\text{in}}$ | Surface runoff or streamflow entering the system. | 7.1, Calculating a Water Balance | At October steady state, the Hydro Lake balance is rearranged to find $Q_{\text{in}}=134.946\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$. |
| Surface-water outflow, $Q_{\text{out}}$ | Surface water leaving through an outlet or downstream river. | 7.1, Calculating a Water Balance | Hydro Lake's measured outlet flow is $350\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$ before Applied Problem 3 substitutes a new outflow. |
| Groundwater inflow, $G_{\text{in}}$ | Groundwater entering the system from the local water table. | 7.1, Introduction; Calculating a Water Balance | Figure 7.1.2 shows $1\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ entering the lake from groundwater. |
| Groundwater outflow, $G_{\text{out}}$ | Water leaving the system through the subsurface, including infiltration through a lake bottom in the conceptual example. | 7.1, Introduction; Calculating a Water Balance | Figure 7.1.1 depicts subsurface loss as water infiltrating through the lake bottom into the ground. |
| Depth measurement | A linear water amount such as mm/day or m/day; it must be multiplied by area to become a volume rate. | 7.1, Water Balance Example - Solution, Steps 2-3 | In Figure 7.1.2, $10\ \text{mm/day}$ of precipitation is first converted to $0.010\ \text{m/day}$. |
| Volume rate | A three-dimensional flow amount per time, such as m$^3$/day or m$^3$/hr. | 7.1, Water Balance Example - Solution, Step 3 | Multiplying $0.010\ \text{m/day}$ by the $2{,}000{,}000\ \text{m}^2$ lake area gives $20{,}000\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$. |
| Lake-surface elevation change, $\Delta h$ | The vertical change in lake level calculated by dividing storage-volume change by lake area. | 7.1, Water Elevation Change | Dividing $16{,}001\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ by $2{,}000{,}000\ \text{m}^2$ gives a lake-level rise of about $8\ \text{mm/day}$. |
| Steady state | A condition used in the Hydro Lake problems in which storage does not change, so $\Delta S=0$ and total inputs equal total outputs. | 7.1, Applied Water Balance Calculations - Problems 3-4 | October Hydro Lake is set to steady state so the known fluxes can be used to solve its unknown inflow. |
| Watershed | In the knowledge check, a valley-like area bounded by raised topographic edges, so surface water does not flow into it across those boundaries as it can flow into a lake or river. | 7.1, Check Your Knowledge | The $3\ \text{km}^2$ knowledge-check watershed omits $Q_{\text{in}}$ and yields $\Delta S=32{,}978.8\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$. |
| Lysimeter | A small system whose measured storage and water fluxes allow an unknown term such as evaporation to be solved by rearranging the water balance. | 7.1, Calculating a Water Balance | A hydrologist monitors storage, groundwater, surface-water fluxes, and rainfall in a lysimeter, then solves the remaining balance term for evaporation. |

## Commonly Confused Terms

- **Storage volume vs. storage change:** $S$ is water held in the system; $\Delta S$ is the gain or loss during the chosen interval.
- **Depth vs. volume:** mm/day and m/day are linear rates; m$^3$/day is a volume rate. Multiply a depth rate by area before combining it with discharge.
- **Volume change vs. elevation change:** $\Delta S$ has volume units; $\Delta h=\Delta S/A$ has length units.
- **Inflow vs. outflow signs:** inflows are added and outflows are subtracted, regardless of whether a term is surface water or groundwater.
- **Steady state vs. no water movement:** steady state means no net storage change, not that every flux is zero.
