# 7.1 Introduction to Quantitative Water Balance Calculations

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]
Module: [[00 - Module 07 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

- Build and rearrange a complete water balance for a defined system.
- Classify precipitation, surface-water, groundwater, evapotranspiration, and storage terms correctly.
- Convert depth fluxes to volume fluxes using area before combining terms.
- Calculate both storage-volume change and lake-surface elevation change, with the correct sign and units.
- Apply simplifying assumptions such as equal groundwater flows and steady state.
- Work consistently across daily, hourly, monthly, millimetre, metre, square-kilometre, and cubic-metre units.

## Core Notes

### Water Balances Across Scales

A **water balance** is a quantitative calculation of water entering and leaving a system. The system can be a plant, lysimeter, stream, river, lake, field, watershed, city, country, ocean, or Earth. Different scientists often emphasize different scales: climate scientists may study countries or the planet, hydrologists often focus on rivers and lakes, and biologists or ecologists may study local habitats.

![Hydrologic cycle with a hypothetical lake circled](../../Assets/Module%2007/7.1.1-hydrologic-cycle-lake.png)

*Figure 7.1.1. A hypothetical lake highlighted within the hydrologic cycle. VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** The red circle defines the lake as the system. Any water crossing that boundary is an input or output, while water remaining inside it is storage.

Use the figure to identify:

- **Inputs:** precipitation, surface water from rivers or streams, and groundwater from the local water table.
- **Outputs:** evaporation or evapotranspiration, surface water through a downstream outlet, and subsurface loss through the lake bottom.
- **Storage:** water already in the lake, which rises or falls as inputs and outputs fluctuate.

The figure does not draw a surface-water outlet, but the source stresses that many lakes have one. A missing arrow in a conceptual figure is not proof that the physical term is zero.

### Calculating a Water Balance

For the course's lake model:

- Inputs: $P$, $Q_{\text{in}}$, and $G_{\text{in}}$.
- Outputs: $ET$, $Q_{\text{out}}$, and $G_{\text{out}}$.

$$
\Delta S=(P+Q_{\text{in}}+G_{\text{in}})-(ET+Q_{\text{out}}+G_{\text{out}})
$$

The equation can be rearranged for any unknown balance term. For example, if storage change, precipitation, and the surface-water and groundwater flows are known, evaporation can be calculated. This is the operating principle of a lysimeter.

### Worked Course Example: Figure 7.1.2

![Conceptual lake water balance with labelled fluxes](../../Assets/Module%2007/7.1.2-lake-water-balance.png)

*Figure 7.1.2. Conceptual water balance connected to a lake system. petovarga/iStock/Getty Images; Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** The arrow directions determine the signs of $P$, $ET$, $Q$, and $G$. The depth rates $P$ and $ET$ must be multiplied by lake area before they can be combined with the volumetric $Q$ and $G$ rates.

Given a lake area of $2\ \text{km}^2$:

| Variable | Value |
|---|---:|
| $P$ | $10\ \text{mm/day}$ |
| $ET$ | $2\ \text{mm/day}$ |
| $Q_{\text{in}}$ | $25\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ |
| $Q_{\text{out}}$ | $24\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ |
| $G_{\text{in}}$ | $1\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ |
| $G_{\text{out}}$ | $1\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ |

**Step 1: Substitute and simplify.** Equal groundwater rates have opposite signs and cancel:

$$
\Delta S=(P+Q_{\text{in}})-(ET+Q_{\text{out}})
$$

**Step 2: Match depth units.**

$$
10\ \text{mm/day}=0.010\ \text{m/day}
$$

$$
2\ \text{mm/day}=0.002\ \text{m/day}
$$

**Step 3: Convert depth rates to volume rates.**

$$
2\ \text{km}^2=2{,}000{,}000\ \text{m}^2
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta S
&=[(0.010)(2{,}000{,}000)+25]-[(0.002)(2{,}000{,}000)+24]\\
&=20{,}025-4{,}024\\
&=16{,}001\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}
\end{aligned}
$$

The positive result is an **increase** in lake storage.

### Water Elevation Change

Divide the volume change by area:

$$
\Delta h=\frac{\Delta S}{A}
=\frac{16{,}001\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}}{2{,}000{,}000\ \text{m}^2}
=0.0080005\ \text{m/day}\approx 8\ \text{mm/day}
$$

Thus the course example's lake surface rises by approximately $8\ \text{mm/day}$. The same rearrangement can solve any other missing balance variable.

### Embedded Knowledge Check

![Watershed water balance with precipitation, evapotranspiration, discharge, and groundwater fluxes](../../Assets/Module%2007/7.1-knowledge-check-watershed-balance.png)

*Course knowledge-check watershed diagram. Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo; VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images (watershed); tarras79/iStock/Getty Images (buildings).*

**What to notice:** The topographic divide is the system boundary, so the diagram has surface-water outflow but no surface-water inflow across that divide. Convert $P$ and $ET$ from depth rates to volume rates before combining them with $Q_{\text{out}}$, $G_{\text{in}}$, and $G_{\text{out}}$.

The watershed diagram gives $A=3\ \text{km}^2$, $P=19\ \text{mm/day}$, $ET=8\ \text{mm/day}$, $Q_{\text{out}}=21\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$, $G_{\text{in}}=0.3\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$, and $G_{\text{out}}=0.5\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$.

$$
\Delta S=[(0.019)(3{,}000{,}000)+0.3]-[(0.008)(3{,}000{,}000)+21+0.5]
=32{,}978.8\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}
$$

The correct choice is $32{,}978.8\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$. Surface-water inflow is omitted because, in the course's watershed model, raised topographic boundaries prevent surface water from entering across the watershed edge as it could enter a river or lake.

### Applied Calculation Patterns

![Conceptual lake water balance used for Problem 1](../../Assets/Module%2007/7.1.2-lake-water-balance.png)

*Figure 7.2.1. The course reuses the conceptual lake water-balance diagram for Problem 1. petovarga/iStock/Getty Images; Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Problem 1 assigns equal groundwater inflow and outflow, so those two arrows cancel algebraically. The remaining storage change is controlled by area-scaled precipitation and evapotranspiration plus the net surface-water flow.

> [!example] Problem 1 - Daily Lake Balance
> $A=4\ \mathrm{km^2}$, $P=12\ \mathrm{mm/day}$, and $ET=1.5\ \mathrm{mm/day}$. All $Q$ and $G$ terms are in $\mathrm{m^3/day}$: $Q_{\text{in}}=17$, $Q_{\text{out}}=14$, and $G_{\text{in}}=G_{\text{out}}=0.75$.
>
> $$\Delta S=[(0.012)(4{,}000{,}000)+17]-[(0.0015)(4{,}000{,}000)+14]=42{,}003\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$$
>
> $$\Delta h=42{,}003/4{,}000{,}000=0.01050075\ \text{m/day}\approx10.5\ \text{mm/day}$$
>
> Storage and elevation both increase.

![Conceptual lake water balance used for Problem 2](../../Assets/Module%2007/7.1.2-lake-water-balance.png)

*Figure 7.2.2. The course reuses the conceptual lake water-balance diagram for Problem 2. petovarga/iStock/Getty Images; Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Problem 2 assigns unequal groundwater flows, so keep both groundwater terms in the balance. Their net contribution is $+0.4\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$ before the larger area-scaled atmospheric terms are included.

> [!example] Problem 2 - Daily Lake Balance
> $A=11\ \mathrm{km^2}$, $P=7\ \mathrm{mm/day}$, and $ET=6\ \mathrm{mm/day}$. All $Q$ and $G$ terms are in $\mathrm{m^3/day}$: $Q_{\text{in}}=29$, $Q_{\text{out}}=32$, $G_{\text{in}}=0.5$, and $G_{\text{out}}=0.1$.
>
> $$\Delta S=[(0.007)(11{,}000{,}000)+29+0.5]-[(0.006)(11{,}000{,}000)+32+0.1]=10{,}997.4\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$$
>
> $$\Delta h=10{,}997.4/11{,}000{,}000\approx0.0009998\ \text{m/day}\approx1.00\ \text{mm/day}$$
>
> Storage and elevation both increase.

> [!example] Problem 3 - October Steady State and Changed Outflow
> $A=10\ \text{km}^2$; mean $P=(35+42+38+47+43)/5=41\ \text{mm}$; $ET=25\ \text{mm}$; $Q_{\text{out}}=350\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$; $G_{\text{in}}=G_{\text{out}}$; October has $744$ hours.
>
> Monthly $P$ volume $=(0.041)(10{,}000{,}000)=410{,}000\ \text{m}^3$, or $551.075\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$. Monthly $ET$ volume is $250{,}000\ \text{m}^3$, or $336.022\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$.
>
> At steady state, $0=P+Q_{\text{in}}-ET-Q_{\text{out}}$, so $Q_{\text{in}}=134.946\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$.
>
> With a new $Q_{\text{out}}=0.03\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}=108\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$:
>
> Because the original $350\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$ outflow was part of a steady-state balance, the new net storage rate is $350-108=242\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$:
>
> $$\Delta S=(242)(744)=180{,}048\ \text{m}^3$$
>
> Storage increases.

> [!example] Problem 4 - April Steady State and Changed Outflow
> $A=12\ \text{km}^2$; mean $P=(25+32+28+37+33)/5=31\ \text{mm}$; $ET=20\ \text{mm}$; $Q_{\text{out}}=275\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$; $G_{\text{in}}=G_{\text{out}}$; April has $720$ hours.
>
> Monthly $P$ volume $=(0.031)(12{,}000{,}000)=372{,}000\ \text{m}^3$, or $516.667\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$. Monthly $ET$ volume is $240{,}000\ \text{m}^3$, or $333.333\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$.
>
> At steady state, $Q_{\text{in}}=333.333+275-516.667=91.667\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$.
>
> With a new $Q_{\text{out}}=0.02\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}=72\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$:
>
> Because the original $275\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$ outflow was part of a steady-state balance, the new net storage rate is $275-72=203\ \text{m}^3/\text{hr}$:
>
> $$\Delta S=(203)(720)=146{,}160\ \text{m}^3$$
>
> Storage increases.

## Equations and Units

### Core Equations

$$
\Delta S=(P+Q_{\text{in}}+G_{\text{in}})-(ET+Q_{\text{out}}+G_{\text{out}})
$$

$$
V=(\text{water depth})(\text{surface area})
$$

$$
\Delta h=\frac{\Delta S}{A}
$$

For steady state with equal groundwater inflow and outflow:

$$
0=P+Q_{\text{in}}-ET-Q_{\text{out}}
$$

$$
Q_{\text{in}}=ET+Q_{\text{out}}-P
$$

### Conversion Checklist

| Conversion | Relationship |
|---|---:|
| Millimetres to metres | divide by $1{,}000$ |
| Square kilometres to square metres | multiply by $1{,}000{,}000$ |
| Depth rate to volume rate | multiply by area |
| $\mathrm{m^3/s}$ to $\mathrm{m^3/hr}$ | multiply by $3{,}600$ |
| Monthly total to hourly rate | divide by hours in the month |
| Volume change to elevation change | divide by area |

> [!important]
> Carry full precision through intermediate steps. Round only the reported result, and always state whether storage or elevation increased or decreased.

## Common Confusions

- **Mixing dimensions:** a depth rate and a volume rate cannot be added until the depth rate is multiplied by area.
- **Mixing time bases:** daily, hourly, monthly, and per-second terms must be converted to one common interval.
- **Losing signs:** every inflow is positive and every outflow is subtracted in the stated equation.
- **Canceling unequal terms:** only equal and opposite terms cancel. Problem 2's groundwater flows, $0.5$ and $0.1\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$, do not cancel.
- **Misreading steady state:** $\Delta S=0$ does not mean precipitation, evapotranspiration, or discharge is zero.
- **Confusing volume and elevation:** a large volume change may correspond to a small elevation change when lake area is large.
- **Treating the figure as exhaustive:** Figure 7.1.1 omits a drawn lake outlet even though $Q_{\text{out}}$ is part of the full balance.
- **Watershed vs. lake inflow:** the course knowledge check excludes surface inflow across a watershed divide; a lake may receive streamflow from outside its own shoreline boundary.
