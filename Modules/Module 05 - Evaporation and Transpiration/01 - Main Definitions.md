# Main Definitions

| Term | Definition | Course section | Example / where / application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evaporation | Conversion of a liquid, or a solid through sublimation, into vapour or gas; reported as a rate or accumulated depth. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | A constant rate of $4\ \mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}$ for 3 hours gives an accumulated evaporation depth of $12\ \mathrm{mm}$. |
| Sublimation | Direct transfer of water from a solid such as snow or ice to atmospheric vapour. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | Figure 5.1.2 includes snowpack water moving directly to the atmosphere as an ET pathway. |
| Interception | Precipitation captured on vegetation or human structures and available for evaporation. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | Rain held on a leaf canopy or human infrastructure can evaporate before reaching the soil. |
| Transpiration | Release of water within vegetation to the atmosphere as vapour through stomata. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | Mature late-season crops in Figure 5.3.3 lose more water through their greater leaf area. |
| Stomata | Small openings, mainly on the underside of leaves, that permit water vapour and other gases to pass out of a plant. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | Figure 5.1.1 shows these underside leaf openings, which generally open in daylight and close at night. |
| Guard cell | The structure around a stoma that controls whether the opening is open or closed. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | In Figure 5.1.1, the guard cells surround the stomatal pore and regulate its opening. |
| Evapotranspiration (ET) | Combined evaporation from free water, interception, ground, and other surfaces plus transpiration from vegetation. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | A hydrologist combines lake, stream, soil, and interception evaporation with plant transpiration in a water-resource model. |
| Water balance | Accounting of water gains, such as precipitation, and losses, such as evaporation, within a system. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | Lake Mead had a negative balance when evaporation and energy-production losses exceeded precipitation, stream inflow, and groundwater inflow. |
| Condensation | Return of water from vapour or gas to the liquid state when air is completely saturated. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | At $10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $e_s\approx1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$ and $e_a\approx2.75\ \mathrm{kPa}$ give $e_s-e_a=-1.75\ \mathrm{kPa}$, so condensation occurs. |
| Saturated air mass | Air whose vapour pressure equals the saturation vapour pressure at its temperature; relative humidity is 100%. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | At $10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$, air with $e_a=e_s\approx1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$ is at 100% relative humidity. |
| Saturation vapour pressure, $e_s$ | Vapour pressure at the saturation point for a given temperature. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | Figure 5.1.6 gives $e_s\approx1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$ at $10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. |
| Actual vapour pressure, $e_a$ | Vapour pressure of the air mass being evaluated. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | Figure 5.1.6 uses $e_a\approx0.5\ \mathrm{kPa}$ for unsaturated air above water at $10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. |
| Vapour pressure deficit (VPD) | Difference $e_s-e_a$; a positive value permits evaporation and a negative value indicates condensation. | [[02 - 5.1 Introduction to Evaporation and Transpiration\|5.1]] | With $e_s=1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$ and $e_a=0.5\ \mathrm{kPa}$, VPD is $0.5\ \mathrm{kPa}$ and evaporation occurs. |
| Relative humidity | Degree of atmospheric saturation; lower relative humidity corresponds to greater capacity for additional vapour at the current temperature. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | In Figure 5.2.1, relative humidity rises as air crosses the lake, so average evaporation falls toward the downwind shore. |
| Solar radiation | Incoming solar energy and the most important meteorological control on evaporation and transpiration. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | Course evaporation rates are generally highest on summer days and lowest on winter nights. |
| Temperature | Measure of energy that sets saturation vapour pressure and therefore affects the vapour pressure deficit; it is not energy itself. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | A hydrologist compares $e_a$ with the temperature-specific $e_s$ before deciding whether cold water can still evaporate. |
| Humidity | Atmospheric moisture condition that controls how much more water can evaporate; lower humidity generally supports a higher evaporation rate. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | Dry air at the upwind shore in Figure 5.2.1 produces the lake's highest local evaporation rate. |
| Wind | Air movement and turbulence that carry moist air away from a water or leaf surface and mix it with drier air, maintaining the vapour pressure gradient up to the potential-rate limit. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | Waterloo's prevailing west-to-east winds carry air across the long axis of Lake Ontario in Figure 5.2.2. |
| Albedo | Reflectiveness of a surface; greater reflection leaves less incoming radiation available for heating and evaporation. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | The course assigns sediment-rich water a higher albedo, slightly reducing absorbed radiation and evaporation. |
| Water quality | Freshness or salinity, reflectiveness, and sediment content of water; increasing salinity decreases evaporation when other conditions are equal. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | In the course ranking exercise, a freshwater lake evaporates faster than an otherwise comparable saline water body. |
| Water depth | Control on heat storage: shallow water warms and cools quickly, while deep water changes slowly and can release stored energy in cool periods or at night. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | A deep lake can continue evaporating at night by releasing heat stored during warmer periods. |
| Water-body size and shape | Controls the distance and orientation over which moving air contacts water; longer contact humidifies the air and lowers average evaporation rate. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | Lake Ontario presents a longer west-to-east water path than Lake Michigan under Waterloo's prevailing winds. |
| Soil moisture content | Actual water available in soil; declining soil moisture lowers soil evaporation and limits transpiration. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]]; [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | In Figure 5.3.4, Canadian soil evaporation is lowest in summer when soil and surface moisture are lowest. |
| Water table | Upper level of local groundwater; evaporation is greatest when it is at the ground surface, and roots that reach it gain access to more water. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]]; [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | Deep late-season crop roots in Figure 5.3.3 reach the local water table and gain a larger water supply. |
| Soil colour | Surface colour control on absorbed solar radiation; darker soil generally absorbs more energy and has a higher evaporation rate. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | Under equal moisture and weather, the course predicts more evaporation from dark soil than light soil. |
| Vegetation cover | Plant cover that shades soil, blocks wind, and increases local humidity, thereby reducing soil evaporation. | [[03 - 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation\|5.2]] | Mature crops in Figure 5.3.3 shade and shelter the ground, reducing soil evaporation relative to the exposed early-season field. |
| Vegetation type | Plant species present; the course states that species has little effect on regional total transpiration when coverage is complete. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | Completely covered fields of wheat, barley, and hay may transpire similar regional totals over time. |
| Plant colour | Leaf-colour control on absorbed energy; lighter leaves reflect more solar energy, while darker leaves absorb more. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | Light-coloured leaves reflect more incoming energy and therefore have lower transpiration potential than dark leaves. |
| Vegetation density | Thickness of plant or leaf cover; denser cover provides more leaf area and supports higher transpiration and evaporation rates. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | The dense late-season crop canopy in Figure 5.3.3 supports more transpiration than sparse early-season plants. |
| Leaf dimensions | Leaf width and surface area; broader leaves provide more area for transpiration and evaporation. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | A broad-leaved plant exposes more transpiring surface than a plant with smaller leaves under the same weather. |
| Stage of growth | Plant size and maturity, which affect the amount of water a plant can pull from soil and transpire. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | Figures 5.3.2 and 5.3.3 contrast low-transpiring young crops with higher-transpiring mature crops. |
| Hydrophytes | Plants whose roots extend directly into water; a water lily is the course example. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | A water lily rooted directly in a water body is the named course example. |
| Mesophytes | Land plants whose roots obtain soil moisture and may or may not reach the local water table; examples include many trees and crops. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | Pine, oak, maple, and most crops are course examples of terrestrial mesophytes. |
| Xerophytes | Arid-climate plants with shallow, dense roots that capture soil moisture percolating from the surface; cactus and sage brush are examples. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | Cactus and sage brush use shallow, dense roots to capture percolating moisture in arid settings. |
| Phreatophytes | Plants with roots extending into the local water table and extracting large daily quantities; cedars and willows are examples. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | Cedars and willows near water or swampy areas can draw large daily quantities from the water table. |
| Soil storage capacity | Capacity of soil to hold water; lower capacity means lower potential transpiration. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | A hydrologist expects crops in low-storage soil to become water-limited sooner during a dry period. |
| Soil permeability | Ability of groundwater to flow through soil; higher permeability replenishes root-zone water more quickly after plant uptake. | [[04 - 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration\|5.3]] | In permeable soil, groundwater can replace root-zone water quickly as mature crops withdraw it. |

## Commonly Confused Terms

- **Evaporation vs. transpiration:** evaporation is a phase change from water, soil, interception, and other surfaces; transpiration is plant-mediated vapour loss through stomata.
- **Evaporation vs. evapotranspiration:** ET includes evaporation **and** transpiration.
- **Saturation vapour pressure vs. actual vapour pressure:** $e_s$ is the saturation threshold at a temperature; $e_a$ is the air's current vapour pressure.
- **Rate vs. total volume:** a larger water body can have a lower average evaporation rate but a larger total evaporated volume.
- **Vegetation type vs. vegetation structure:** species may have little regional effect under complete coverage, while colour, density, leaf dimensions, growth stage, and roots still affect transpiration.
- **Xerophyte vs. phreatophyte:** xerophytes exploit shallow soil moisture; phreatophytes reach down to the water table.
