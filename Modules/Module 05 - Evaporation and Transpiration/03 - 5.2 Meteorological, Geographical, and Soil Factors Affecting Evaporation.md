# 5.2 Meteorological, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Evaporation

## Exam Focus

- Explain the four meteorological, three geographical, and four soil controls on evaporation.
- Rank conditions or water bodies by expected average evaporation rate.
- Explain why wind, water-body size, and vegetation cover have conditional rather than unlimited effects.
- Use Figures 5.2.1 and 5.2.2 to connect wind direction, distance across water, humidity, average rate, and total volume.

## Core Notes

### Why the Controls Matter

- Meteorological, geographical, and soil conditions determine where evaporation occurs, how much water can evaporate, and the rate at which it happens.
- These controls help identify the origins of atmospheric vapour, assess climate-related changes, quantify evaporation, and calculate regional water balances.

### Evaporation and the Weather

1. **Solar radiation:** the most important factor. More intense or abundant solar energy produces more evaporation and transpiration. Rates are generally highest during summer days and lowest during winter nights.
2. **Temperature:** sets saturation vapour pressure and therefore the vapour pressure deficit between water and air. Temperature measures energy but is not energy itself, so it is less important than solar radiation. Evaporation can occur at low temperature whenever air is below saturation, although it may be slow.
3. **Humidity:** evaporation rate is directly proportional to the vapour pressure gradient. A larger deficit, or lower relative humidity, allows more evaporation. The atmosphere's capacity to hold additional evaporated water decreases as temperature falls.
4. **Wind:** turbulence mixes moist near-surface air with drier air above and carries new vapour away, maintaining the gradient. Without wind, air immediately above water can saturate quickly. The evaporation rate increases with wind velocity only until the potential rate set mainly by radiation, temperature, and relative humidity is reached. Wind contributes little when no evaporation energy is available.

### Evaporation and Geography

1. **Water quality:** increasing salinity decreases evaporation when other conditions are equal, so fresh water evaporates faster than saline water. Oceans still provide the most atmospheric vapour because of their enormous area. The course states that sediment-rich water has higher albedo, admits slightly less radiation, and therefore evaporates slightly less than cleaner water.
2. **Water depth:** shallow water warms quickly and follows seasonal air-temperature patterns. Deep water needs more energy and time to warm or cool, but it can release stored energy in cool months or at night and continue evaporation when incoming radiation is insufficient.
3. **Surface size and shape:** average evaporation rate generally decreases as water-body size increases because moving air becomes progressively wetter across the surface.

- **Figure 5.2.1:** initially dry air produces a high evaporation rate at the upwind shore. Relative humidity rises toward saturation across the lake, so the average rate decreases over a longer path of contact.
- Shape and orientation matter because wind direction controls the length of contact between air and water.
- **Figure 5.2.2:** prevailing winds near Waterloo generally move west to east. Lake Ontario exposes a longer water-surface path to those winds than Lake Michigan.
- A larger body can have a lower average evaporation rate while still losing a greater total volume because its total area is larger.

### Evaporation and the Soil

Soils and other land surfaces cover approximately one-third of Earth. Their global evaporation contribution is smaller than the oceans', but soil evaporation remains important for agriculture and forestry watering decisions.

1. **Soil moisture content:** the most important direct soil control. It is the actual available soil water; evaporation rate falls as this water decreases.
2. **Depth to the water table:** evaporation is greatest when the water table reaches the ground surface and continuously supplies water. A deeper water table weakens or removes that supply.
3. **Soil colour:** dark soils absorb more solar radiation and generally evaporate more water than light soils.
4. **Vegetation cover:** shade reduces solar energy at the ground; plants block wind and slow the removal of moist air; daytime transpiration raises local relative humidity. All three effects reduce potential soil evaporation.

> [!tip]
> To rank evaporation, check energy first, then vapour pressure deficit and wind, then water quality/depth/shape, and finally surface water availability and cover.

## Equations and Units

The course describes evaporation rate as directly proportional to the vapour pressure gradient:

$$
\text{evaporation rate}\propto \mathrm{VPD}=e_s-e_a
$$

- Vapour pressure and VPD are measured in kPa.
- A larger positive VPD supports a higher rate if energy and water remain available.
- Wind maintains VPD by removing moist air, but it does not create solar energy or bypass the potential evaporation limit.

> [!example] Worked comparison pattern
> For summer average evaporation rate, a **small, shallow freshwater lake** outranks a large, deep saline water body: small size limits the distance over which the air becomes more humid, shallow water warms quickly, and fresh water evaporates faster than saline water when other conditions are equal. This is the answer identified by the course knowledge check.

## Common Confusions

- **Temperature is not solar energy:** it is a measurement of energy and a control on saturation vapour pressure.
- **Cold does not prohibit evaporation:** evaporation can occur at low temperature if the air is unsaturated.
- **More wind is not always more evaporation:** once the potential rate is reached, faster vapour removal causes no further increase.
- **Smaller average rate does not mean smaller total loss:** large area can produce a large total volume despite a lower rate.
- **Vegetation adds transpiration but reduces soil evaporation:** shading, wind shelter, and local humidity explain the latter effect.
- **Dark soil and sediment-rich water behave differently in the course:** dark soil absorbs more radiation, while the course attributes greater albedo and slightly lower evaporation to dirtier water.
