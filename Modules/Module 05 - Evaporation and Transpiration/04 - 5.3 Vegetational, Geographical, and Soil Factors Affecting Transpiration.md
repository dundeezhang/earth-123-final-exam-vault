# 5.3 Vegetational, Geographical, and Soil Factors Affecting Transpiration

## Exam Focus

- Explain how stomatal opening, plant structure, roots, and soil-water properties control transpiration.
- Distinguish hydrophytes, mesophytes, xerophytes, and phreatophytes.
- Apply the course ET energy accounting equation.
- Compare early- and late-season agricultural fields using Figures 5.3.2 and 5.3.3.
- Interpret the Canadian annual evaporation, transpiration, and total ET curves in Figure 5.3.4.

## Core Notes

### Scope

- Transpiration moves large quantities of water from the subsurface and surface to the atmosphere across tropical and temperate rainforests, agricultural regions, and much of Earth's vegetated land.
- Marine vegetation and microscopic plankton also transpire in the oceans.
- The meteorological, geographical, and soil controls from section 5.2 continue to matter; this section adds the specifically vegetational controls.

### Controls on Transpiration

1. **Stomatal openings:** transpiration duration is directly related to how long stomata remain open. Closed stomata permit virtually no transpiration; when open, meteorological controls regulate evaporation from the leaf underside. Main opening controls are incoming solar energy/day length, air temperature, and relative humidity.
2. **Vegetation type:** species differ in stomatal size and distribution, but the course states that vegetation type has little effect on regional total transpiration when coverage is complete. Wheat, barley, and hay may transpire similar amounts over time and space; this remains an active research area.
3. **Soil factors:** less soil moisture means less transpiration, and lower soil storage capacity means lower potential transpiration.
4. **Soil permeability:** after root uptake removes water, groundwater must replenish the root zone. Higher permeability allows faster inflow toward the roots.

![Closed stoma and open pore on a leaf](../../Assets/Module%2005/stromata-diagram_5.png)

*Figure 5.3.1: closed and open stomata on the underside of a leaf. ttsz/iStock/Getty Images.*

**What to notice:** Water vapour, oxygen, and carbon dioxide move through the open pore; closing the stoma nearly stops transpiration.

### Confirmed Vegetation Characteristics

- **Plant colour:** light leaves reflect more solar energy and reduce transpiration and evaporation potential; dark leaves absorb more.
- **Density:** denser or thicker cover provides more leaf area and supports higher transpiration and evaporation rates.
- **Leaf dimensions:** broader leaves provide more surface area than smaller leaves and therefore permit more transpiration and evaporation.
- **Stage of growth:** larger, more mature plants can pull and use more soil water than saplings or bushes.
- **Root types:** root architecture controls which water supply a plant can access.

| Root group | Water access and setting | Course examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrophytes | Roots extend directly into water; plants often grow in a water body. | Water lily |
| Mesophytes | Roots use soil moisture and may or may not reach the local water table; generally terrestrial. | Pine, oak, maple, most crops |
| Xerophytes | Shallow, dense roots capture soil moisture percolating from the surface; arid climates. | Cactus, sage brush |
| Phreatophytes | Roots extend into the local water table and extract large daily quantities; land near water or swampy areas. | Cedars, willows |

### Evapotranspiration Energy Accounting

Total incoming solar energy is distributed among evaporation of interception, transpiration, soil evaporation, and net losses including heat and albedo.

- Interception includes water on vegetation or human infrastructure.
- Transpiration is plant water loss associated with photosynthesis.
- Soil and other surfaces contribute direct evaporation.
- Net losses include reflected energy (albedo) and energy that raises the internal temperature of a plant, surface, or water body.
- Separating the individual terms is difficult because all components are dynamic, simultaneous, and spread over large areas.

### Agricultural Crop Production

![Early-season field with small plants and exposed soil](../../Assets/Module%2005/conceptual-agricultural-field-small-plants600x450_5.jpg)

*Figure 5.3.2: early-season field with small crops, exposed soil, and the local water table. Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Small leaf area and shallow roots limit transpiration, while exposed soil and unblocked wind promote soil evaporation.

![Late-season field with mature plants and deep roots](../../Assets/Module%2005/conceptual-agricultural-field-large-plants600x450_5.jpg)

*Figure 5.3.3: late-season field with mature crops, greater cover, and the local water table. Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Greater leaf area and deeper roots increase transpiration, while ground cover and wind shelter reduce soil evaporation and raise local humidity.

- ET can remain high while its partition changes: early-season loss is weighted toward soil evaporation, while late-season loss is weighted toward transpiration.

### Annual Canadian Pattern

![Annual Canadian evaporation transpiration and total ET curves](../../Assets/Module%2005/annual-fluctuation-in-evapotranspiration_5.png)

*Figure 5.3.4: idealized annual evaporation, transpiration, and total ET in Canada. Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Evaporation peaks in spring and fall when surface moisture is high, while transpiration peaks during summer growth and drives the summer maximum in total ET. Transpiration varies much more through the year and falls to zero in winter.

## Equations and Units

The course's word equation is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Total Solar Energy Available for ET}={}&\text{Evaporation of Interception}\\
&+\text{Transpiration}\\
&+\text{Evaporation from Soil}\\
&+\text{Net Losses including Heat and Albedo}
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!example] Worked energy-balance pattern
> If total available solar energy is 120 energy units and interception evaporation, transpiration, and soil evaporation use 20, 50, and 30 units, respectively, then net losses are $120-(20+50+30)=20$ energy units. The numbers are illustrative; the component relationship is the one taught in section 5.3.

## Common Confusions

- **Complete cover vs. plant traits:** complete coverage can make species identity relatively unimportant at regional scale, while colour, density, leaf size, growth stage, and roots still matter.
- **Xerophyte vs. phreatophyte:** a xerophyte has shallow dense roots; a phreatophyte reaches down to groundwater.
- **High transpiration can coincide with low soil evaporation:** mature crops increase leaf loss but shade and shelter the soil.
- **The ET energy accounting has more than water-loss terms:** it also includes heat and albedo losses from the incoming solar-energy total.
- **Canadian evaporation and transpiration do not peak together:** evaporation peaks in spring/fall; transpiration and total ET peak in summer.
