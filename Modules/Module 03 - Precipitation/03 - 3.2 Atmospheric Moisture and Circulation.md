# 3.2 Atmospheric Moisture and Circulation

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

Module: [[00 - Module 03 Overview]] | Definitions: [[01 - Main Definitions]]

## Exam Focus

- Connect global circulation and latitude to wet equatorial, dry subtropical, and dry polar zones.
- Reproduce the six-step cloud-formation sequence.
- Explain adiabatic cooling physically and apply all three course lapse rates.
- Calculate relative humidity and identify saturation temperature from a saturated-vapour-pressure graph.

## Core Notes

### Atmospheric moisture supply and movement

Precipitation and evaporation continually circulate water in all phases through the atmosphere. Nearly **90% of global evaporation originates from oceans**. The remainder comes from warm fresh waters such as streams, rivers, and lakes, and from lush vegetation such as tropical rain forests. Evaporation and atmospheric moisture supply are greatest in warm equatorial areas, especially over oceans.

Large-scale air movement is driven mainly by:

- **temperature gradients:** higher equatorial and lower polar temperatures drive planet-scale transport from warm toward cold regions;
- **Earth's west-to-east rotation:** changing radius and conservation of angular momentum help form large circulation patterns and can produce westerly winds.

### The three circulation cells

At the equator, intense solar energy heats land and ocean surfaces, increasing evaporation and lifting warm, moist air. The rising air cools and condenses, potentially producing clouds and heavy precipitation. After moisture is removed, air descends and warms near $30^\circ$ N and S. This dry, warming air accelerates evaporation and promotes arid environments.

The course describes:

1. **Tropical cell:** warm equatorial air rises and moves poleward, cools, descends near $30^\circ$ N, then branches into equatorward return flow and continued northward flow.
2. **Middle cell:** circulates air between the tropical and polar cells through the temperate zone.
3. **Polar cell:** air rises near $60^\circ$ N, moves poleward, cools, and descends near the pole.

At cell transition zones, rising air favours storm clouds and heavy precipitation; descending air favours dry lands and deserts.

### Six steps of cloud formation

1. Evaporation from fresh and ocean waters and transpiration from biological activity release water vapour.
2. Warm, moist air rises and cools **adiabatically** as lower pressure lets the parcel expand. The cooling is caused by expansion, not direct heat transfer to colder surrounding air.
3. Cooling raises relative humidity toward saturation. At saturation, vapour changes toward liquid-water or ice nuclei and cloud formation begins.
4. At saturation, water molecules condense on a **condensation nucleus** such as atmospheric dust or salt.
5. Droplets grow by further condensation and by collision and merging in turbulent air.
6. Once droplets are large enough to overcome buoyant updrafts, gravity pulls them down as precipitation. Falling droplets may break apart or re-evaporate. Typical raindrop size is about **0.1-3 mm**.

### Phase and droplet behaviour in the figures

- **Figure 3.2.3:** cooling alone moves a parcel from evaporation/unsaturated conditions at A and B, to exact saturation at C, to condensation at D.
- **Figure 3.2.4, warm cloud:** water vapour and droplets exchange through condensation and evaporation; droplets that become large and heavy enough fall as rain.
- **Figure 3.2.4, cold cloud:** supercooled droplets, ice crystals, water vapour, and sublimation processes contribute to particles that eventually fall as snow.

## Equations and Units

### Lapse rates

| Condition | Course cooling rate with ascent |
|---|---:|
| Dry air | $1.0^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| Wet air | $0.6^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| Environmental atmosphere | $0.65^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ |

Use:

$$
T_2=T_1-\Gamma\Delta z
$$

when ascent is expressed in the same height unit used by $\Gamma$.

> [!example] Worked lapse-rate patterns
> A dry parcel starting at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and rising $500\ \mathrm{m}$ cools by $(1.0)(500/100)=5.0^\circ\mathrm{C}$, so $T_2=15.0^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
>
> A wet parcel with the same start and ascent cools by $(0.6)(500/100)=3.0^\circ\mathrm{C}$, so $T_2=17.0^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
>
> Using the environmental rate, the cooling is $(0.65)(500/100)=3.25^\circ\mathrm{C}$, so $T_2=16.75^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

### Relative humidity and saturation

$$
R_h=\frac{\text{vapour pressure of the air}}{\text{saturated vapour pressure}}=\frac{e}{e_s}
$$

$R_h$ is dimensionless. A ratio of $1.0$ is $100\%$ and means the air is saturated. Warm air can hold more moisture per unit volume than cold air, so $e_s$ depends on temperature.

> [!important] Read the saturation graph in two directions
> At a fixed temperature, read $e_s$ and compute $R_h=e/e_s$. At a fixed vapour pressure $e$, move along the temperature axis until the saturated-vapour-pressure curve equals $e$; that temperature is where the parcel reaches saturation.

> [!example] Worked relative-humidity pattern
> At $18^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the course graph gives $e_s\approx2.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$. If $e=1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$,
> $$
> R_h=\frac{1.0}{2.0}=0.5=50\%.
> $$

> [!example] Worked saturation-temperature pattern
> Hold $e=1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$ constant and cool the parcel until the saturated-vapour-pressure curve reaches $e_s=1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$. The course graph gives about $7^\circ\mathrm{C}$, where $R_h=1$.

## Common Confusions

- **Adiabatic cooling is not direct heat loss.** Lower pressure permits expansion, which lowers parcel temperature.
- **The dry lapse rate is larger than the wet rate.** Over the same ascent, dry air has the greater temperature decrease in the course values.
- **Ratio vs. percent:** $R_h=0.5$ and $R_h=50\%$ describe the same condition; saturation is $1.0$ or $100\%$.
- **Cooling can produce saturation without adding vapour.** As temperature falls, $e_s$ falls until it equals the parcel's existing $e$.
- **Saturation is necessary but a nucleus is still required.** Dust and salt provide surfaces for condensation.
- **Rising and descending air have opposite moisture effects.** Rising air cools and may precipitate; descending air warms and favours aridity.
