# Main Definitions

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home|Home]]

Module: [[Modules/Module 03 - Precipitation/00 - Module 03 Overview|Overview]]

| Term | Definition | Course section | Example / where / application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Precipitation                    | Any form of atmospheric moisture transferred to Earth's surface; the course includes rain, snow, hail, sleet, fog, dew, and frost.                                          | 3.1                             | Dew deposited on grass counts as precipitation even though it does not fall from a cloud. |
| Liquid-phase precipitation       | Rainfall, drizzle, or dew; it immediately becomes an active part of the hydrologic cycle.                                                                                   | 3.1                             | Rain reaching a roof or the ground can immediately contribute to runoff. |
| Solid-phase precipitation        | Snow, hail, or frost; it tends to remain immobile until melting.                                                                                                            | 3.1                             | A winter snowpack stores surface water until spring melt releases it. |
| Convective lifting / convection  | Uneven surface heating makes an air mass buoyant so it rises, cools, and condenses while drawing replacement air in below.                                                  | 3.1                             | A hot, humid summer day on the Canadian Prairies can produce a short, local thunderstorm with intense rain and hail. |
| Orographic lifting               | Forced ascent of an air mass over elevated topography, causing cooling, condensation, and precipitation.                                                                    | 3.1                             | Mountainous British Columbia is the course knowledge-check setting where topography strongly favours this process. |
| Precipitation shadow             | The drier leeward region shown in the course's orographic-lifting figure after precipitation occurs on the windward side.                                                   | 3.1                             | In Figure 3.1.3, air loses moisture on the windward slope and descends into a drier area behind the mountain. |
| Convergence lifting              | As winds converge toward a low-pressure centre, air is forced upward because it cannot move into the ground.                                                                | 3.1                             | Figure 3.1.4 shows horizontal winds meeting at a low-pressure centre, turning upward, and forming clouds. |
| Frontal lifting                  | Less-dense warm air rises over advancing cold air or another opposing-temperature air mass, cools, and condenses.                                                           | 3.1                             | This is the most prominent precipitation process in mid-latitude southern Ontario. |
| Hydrologic storage, $S$          | Water held temporarily in reservoirs such as lakes, aquifers, ice caps, glaciers, and oceans; $\Delta S$ is change in storage over time.                                    | 3.1 embedded hydrological cycle | A hydrologist records snowpack accumulation as an increase in storage, $\Delta S>0$, before melt begins. |
| Evaporation                      | Transfer of liquid water from a surface to atmospheric water vapour.                                                                                                        | 3.1-3.2                         | Oceans supply nearly $90\%$ of global evaporation in the course material. |
| Transpiration                    | Release of water vapour to the atmosphere through biological activity and vegetation.                                                                                       | 3.1-3.2                         | Lush vegetation such as tropical rain forest adds atmospheric moisture through transpiration. |
| Sublimation                      | Direct movement of solid snow or ice into gaseous water vapour, as described by the embedded hydrological cycle.                                                            | 3.1-3.2                         | A winter or high-elevation water balance includes snow lost directly to vapour without melting. |
| Temperature gradient             | The equator-to-pole temperature difference that helps transport air masses from warmer to colder regions.                                                                   | 3.2                             | Warm equatorial air is transported toward colder polar regions in the global circulation shown in Figure 3.2.1. |
| Tropical cell                    | Circulation in which warm equatorial air rises, moves poleward, cools, descends near $30^\circ$ latitude, and partly returns equatorward.                                   | 3.2                             | Equatorial ascent produces a precipitation maximum, while descent near $30^\circ$ N/S helps create subtropical dry belts. |
| Middle cell                      | Atmospheric circulation between the tropical and polar cells in the temperate zone.                                                                                         | 3.2                             | Southern Ontario lies in the mid-latitudes where this temperate-zone circulation and frontal precipitation are prominent. |
| Polar cell                       | Circulation in which air rises near $60^\circ$ latitude, moves poleward, cools, and descends near the pole.                                                                 | 3.2                             | Figure 3.2.2 shows precipitation dropping sharply toward the poles where cold, descending air is dry. |
| Transition zone                  | Boundary between circulation cells; rising-air zones favour precipitation while descending-air zones favour dryness.                                                        | 3.2                             | Near $30^\circ$ N/S, descending air at a cell boundary corresponds to major desert belts. |
| Adiabatic cooling                | Cooling caused by gas expansion as an air parcel rises into lower pressure, without direct heat loss to the surrounding atmosphere.                                         | 3.2                             | A rising parcel expands in lower pressure and cools toward the saturation point marked C in Figure 3.2.3. |
| Adiabatic lapse rate             | Decrease in air-parcel temperature with altitude; the course gives $1.0^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ for dry air and $0.6^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ for wet air. | 3.2                             | A dry parcel rising $500\ \mathrm{m}$ from $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$ cools by $5^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $15^\circ\mathrm{C}$. |
| Environmental lapse rate         | General atmospheric temperature decrease of $0.65^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ used because moisture varies dynamically.                                                | 3.2                             | Over a $500\ \mathrm{m}$ rise, a hydrologist estimates ambient cooling of $3.25^\circ\mathrm{C}$. |
| Vapour pressure, $e$             | The vapour pressure of the air; it is the numerator in the relative-humidity equation.                                                                                      | 3.2                             | In the course calculation, air at $18^\circ\mathrm{C}$ has $e=1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$. |
| Saturated vapour pressure, $e_s$ | Vapour pressure at which air is saturated at a given temperature; the course describes it as the maximum moisture pressure the atmosphere can hold at that temperature. | 3.2 | The course graph gives $e_s\approx2.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$ at $18^\circ\mathrm{C}$. |
| Relative humidity, $R_h$         | Ratio of vapour pressure to saturated vapour pressure at the same temperature: $R_h=e/e_s$.                                                                                 | 3.2                             | With $e=1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$ and $e_s=2.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$, $R_h=0.5=50\%$. |
| Saturation                       | State at which $e=e_s$, so $R_h=1.0=100\%$ and condensation can begin.                                                                                                      | 3.2                             | Air with $e=1.0\ \mathrm{kPa}$ reaches saturation at about $7^\circ\mathrm{C}$ on the course graph. |
| Condensation nucleus             | A seed, such as atmospheric dust or salt, on or around which water molecules bind during condensation.                                                                      | 3.2                             | The *Snowtime* video shows a small dust or soot particle at the centre of a snow crystal. |
| Snowfall                         | Frozen precipitation formed by growth of an ice crystal around a seed until gravity overcomes atmospheric buoyancy.                                                         | 3.3                             | In a cold cloud, an ice crystal grows through condensation and collisions, then falls once gravity overcomes the updraft. |
| Sleet                            | Semi-frozen precipitation produced when temperatures below a cloud are close to freezing.                                                                                   | 3.3                             | Falling snow that partly melts in near-freezing air below the cloud reaches the ground as sleet. |
| Hail                             | Larger frozen particles that remain frozen and continue growing.                                                                                                            | 3.3                             | A strong convective summer thunderstorm can keep frozen particles aloft long enough for them to grow into hail. |
| Snow cover / snowpack            | Snow accumulated on the ground; it stores surface water until melting and may be redistributed by wind.                                                                     | 3.3                             | Wind can move deposited snow across the Canadian Prairies, creating sharply different depths over short distances. |
| Snow water equivalent (SWE)      | Depth of liquid water contained in a snowpack.                                                                                                                              | 3.3                             | Figure 3.3.1 shows that $10$ inches of snow at an SLR of $2{:}1$ contains $5$ inches SWE. |
| Snow density ratio, $D_s$        | Water-equivalent depth divided by snow-cover depth; it is dimensionless when both depths use the same unit.                                                                 | 3.3                             | A $30\ \mathrm{cm}$ pack containing $3\ \mathrm{cm}$ SWE has $D_s=3/30=0.10$, or $10\%$. |
| Snow-liquid ratio (SLR)          | Snow depth divided by SWE, often written as a ratio such as $20{:}1$; it is the reciprocal of the snow density ratio.                                                       | 3.3                             | A $20$-inch pack at $20{:}1$ contains $20/20=1$ inch SWE. |
| Flash flood                      | A flood produced by rare precipitation in an arid region; such events make running water an important landscape-shaping force even in deserts.                              | 3.4                             | A rare desert storm can send sudden runoff through normally dry channels and reshape the landscape. |
| Drought                          | A dry period or series of drier days that reduces a region's water balance and may require conservation.                                                                    | 3.4                             | An urban response is to restrict lawn and garden watering or backyard-pool filling to limited days. |
| Arid region / desert             | A region receiving no more than $25\ \mathrm{cm}$ of total precipitation per year.                                                                                          | 3.4                             | A site receiving $20\ \mathrm{cm}$ of rain and snow in a year meets the course's general arid-region threshold. |
| Semi-arid / steppe               | The transitional dry-land class shown around many deserts in Figure 3.4.1.                                                                                                  | 3.4                             | Figure 3.4.1 maps steppe belts bordering many of the world's arid desert cores. |
| Polar desert                     | A cold desert where cold air's limited moisture-holding ability produces very low precipitation.                                                                            | 3.4                             | Polar regions qualify as deserts despite low temperatures because their annual precipitation is extremely small. |

## Commonly Confused Terms

- **Precipitation vs. rainfall:** precipitation is the broader category; rainfall is only its liquid rain form.
- **Liquid vs. solid precipitation:** liquid water enters active flow immediately; solid water is temporary storage until melting.
- **Convective vs. convergence lifting:** convection starts with uneven surface heating; convergence starts with winds meeting at a low-pressure centre.
- **Orographic vs. frontal lifting:** mountains mechanically force air upward in orographic lifting; contrasting air masses force warm air upward at a front.
- **Vapour pressure vs. saturated vapour pressure:** $e$ is the air's actual value; $e_s$ is the temperature-dependent maximum.
- **Dry vs. wet lapse rate:** dry air cools faster with ascent in the course values, $1.0$ rather than $0.6^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$.
- **Snowfall vs. snow accumulation:** falling snow can be relatively uniform, but wind can make the deposited snowpack highly variable.
- **Density ratio vs. SLR:** $D_s=\mathrm{SWE}/\text{snow depth}$; $\mathrm{SLR}=\text{snow depth}/\mathrm{SWE}$. They are reciprocals.
- **Arid vs. hot:** aridity is based on limited precipitation, so both hot subtropical and cold polar deserts occur.
