## Highest-Yield Exam Points

1. **Precipitation** is any atmospheric moisture transferred to Earth's surface, including rain, drizzle, snow, hail, sleet, fog, dew, and frost under the course's broad definition.
2. Liquid precipitation immediately joins active hydrologic flow, while solid precipitation generally remains stored and immobile until melting.
3. The four lifting mechanisms are **convective**, **orographic**, **convergence**, and **frontal** lifting; each has a different trigger, spatial pattern, and event character.
4. Cold fronts have a steeper gradient and tend to produce more intense, shorter rain; warm fronts tend to produce less intense, longer rain. Frontal lifting dominates mid-latitudes such as southern Ontario.
5. Nearly 90% of global evaporation originates from oceans. Atmospheric circulation is driven mainly by equator-to-pole temperature gradients and Earth's west-to-east rotation.
6. Rising air near the equator favours condensation and heavy precipitation; descending, warming, dry air near $30^\circ$ N and S favours arid climates. Precipitation also drops sharply toward the poles.
7. Rising air cools adiabatically as pressure falls and the parcel expands. Course lapse rates are $1.0^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ for dry air, $0.6^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ for wet air, and $0.65^\circ\mathrm{C}/100\ \mathrm{m}$ for the environmental rate.
8. Saturation occurs when vapour pressure equals saturated vapour pressure: $R_h=1.0=100\%$. Condensation also requires nuclei such as atmospheric dust or salt.
9. Snowfall forms like rainfall but remains frozen from cloud to surface. Snowfall may be fairly uniform while falling, yet wind redistribution makes ground accumulation highly variable.
10. Snow depth alone does not determine stored water. Snow water equivalent (SWE), snow density ratio, and snow-liquid ratio (SLR) must be interpreted together.
11. A fresh snowfall has a typical course density ratio near $0.06$ or $6\%$; wind-packed snow may approach or exceed $0.3$ or $30\%$.
12. An arid region receives no more than $25\ \mathrm{cm}$ of annual precipitation. Deserts can be hot or polar, and rare flash floods make running water an important geomorphic force in deserts.

## Equations and Quantitative Skills

### Relative humidity

$$
R_h=\frac{e}{e_s}
$$

where $e$ is the air's vapour pressure and $e_s$ is saturated vapour pressure at the same temperature. Report either a ratio or multiply by $100$ for percent.

### Lapse-rate cooling

$$
\Delta T=-\Gamma\Delta z
$$

Select the course rate for dry air, wet air, or the environmental atmosphere; keep height units consistent with the rate.

### Snow density and water equivalent

$$
D_s=\frac{d_w}{d_s}=\frac{\mathrm{SWE}}{\text{snow depth}}
$$

$$
\mathrm{SLR}=\frac{\text{snow depth}}{\mathrm{SWE}}=\frac{1}{D_s}
$$

Use the same length units in numerator and denominator. A larger density ratio means more water per unit snow depth; a larger SLR means less water per unit snow depth.

### Climate thresholds and table reading

- Memorize the general arid threshold: annual precipitation $\le 25\ \mathrm{cm}$.
- For the dry/humid boundary table in section 3.4, use both average annual temperature and the season of maximum rainfall.
