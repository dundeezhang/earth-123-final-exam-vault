# 3.3 Snowfall and Snow Cover Processes and Issues

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home|Home]]

Module: [[Modules/Module 03 - Precipitation/00 - Module 03 Overview|Overview]] | Definitions: [[Modules/Module 03 - Precipitation/01 - Main Definitions|Definitions]]

## Exam Focus

- Explain how snow forms and how below-cloud temperature changes its final phase.
- Distinguish snowfall uniformity from post-deposition snow-cover variability.
- Identify the measurement problems and physical controls that make snow hydrology difficult.
- Calculate density ratio, SWE, and SLR and interpret what each says about stored water.

## Core Notes

### Formation and phase

Snowfall forms through essentially the same process as rainfall, except that the air remains much colder from cloud to surface, keeping water solid. An ice crystal grows around a seed through continued condensation and collisions. Once gravity overcomes its buoyancy, it descends to the ground.

Below the cloud:

- air above freezing can melt falling snow into rain;
- temperatures close to freezing can produce semi-frozen **sleet**;
- particles that remain frozen and continue growing can become **hail**.

The *Snowtime* reverse-melting video reveals the complex crystal structure and the small, often circular seed at each centre. The seed may be atmospheric dust or soot.

Snowfall depends on the same lifting mechanisms as rain:

- convergence and/or frontal lifting;
- convective lifting;
- orographic lifting.

### Hydrologic importance

Outside high elevations and polar regions, snowfall is a small portion of total annual precipitation. Hydrologists focus on:

- lateral distribution of snow cover, which can vary over short distances;
- accumulation before melting, important to water balances and floods;
- melt rate, also important to water balances and floods.

Snowfall events may be more uniform and less turbulent than rainfall while descending, but accumulated snow is easily moved by wind. This redistribution creates large differences over short distances and makes ground measurement difficult.

Snow that remains solid at the surface is **stored surface water** and does not directly participate in active hydrologic flow until it melts.

### Measurement challenges

Large-area snow monitoring is difficult because:

1. falling snow is hard to capture in a collection gauge;
2. wind redistributes deposited snow irregularly;
3. density and packing vary greatly, changing total water content.

A gauging network must therefore be designed with detailed knowledge of the study region.

### Terrain and accumulation

- In mountains, a larger share of precipitation falls as snow. Accumulation varies with elevation, wind direction, slope aspect, and local topography.
- In low, open, flat regions such as the Canadian Prairies, accumulation can be more uniform, but wind and small topographic differences still matter.
- The national snow-cover knowledge check identifies **ocean currents and water bodies** as the primary control among the listed choices, reinforcing the atmospheric moisture source.

### Snow density and stored water

Snow depth alone is not enough to determine water storage. Fresh, light snow and wet, packed snow of the same depth can have very different SWE.

General course values:

- new fresh snowfall: density ratio around $0.06$ or $6\%$;
- wind-packed snow: density ratios approaching or exceeding $0.3$ or $30\%$.

Density is affected by:

- heat exchange with the ground or atmosphere;
- pressure from overlying snow;
- wind redistribution, drifting, and packing;
- liquid water from rain or melting;
- human movement and packing.

![Two snowpacks comparing snow depth, snow-liquid ratio, and water equivalent](../../Assets/Module%2003/3.3-snow-liquid-ratio-swe.jpg)
*Figure 3.3.1: Snow-liquid ratio and snow-water equivalent. The COMET Program/UCAR (1997-2021).*

**What to notice:** Use $\mathrm{SLR}=\text{snow depth}/\mathrm{SWE}$: the $10$-inch pack at $2{:}1$ stores $5$ inches SWE, while the deeper $20$-inch pack at $20{:}1$ stores only $1$ inch SWE.

## Equations and Units

Let $d_w$ be water-equivalent depth and $d_s$ be snow-cover depth.

$$
D_s=\frac{d_w}{d_s}=\frac{\mathrm{SWE}}{\text{snow depth}}
$$

When both depths use the same unit, $D_s$ is dimensionless. Multiply by $100$ to express percent.

$$
\mathrm{SWE}=D_s(\text{snow depth})
$$

$$
\mathrm{SLR}=\frac{\text{snow depth}}{\mathrm{SWE}}=\frac{1}{D_s}
$$

For an SLR of $n{:}1$:

$$
\mathrm{SWE}=\frac{\text{snow depth}}{n}
$$

> [!example] Worked density pattern
> A $30\ \mathrm{cm}$ snowpack with $3\ \mathrm{cm}$ SWE has
> $$
> D_s=\frac{3}{30}=0.10=10\%.
> $$

> [!example] Worked Figure 3.3.1 pattern
> Left: $10\ \mathrm{in}/2=5\ \mathrm{in}$ SWE and $D_s=5/10=0.50$.
>
> Right: $20\ \mathrm{in}/20=1\ \mathrm{in}$ SWE and $D_s=1/20=0.05$.

## Common Confusions

- **Snowfall vs. snow cover:** snowfall describes particles descending; snow cover is what remains and can be redistributed after deposition.
- **Uniform fall does not mean uniform accumulation.** Wind can move snow after it lands.
- **Depth is not water content.** SWE and density determine how much liquid water the pack contains.
- **Density ratio and SLR move oppositely.** Denser, wetter snow has a larger $D_s$ but a smaller SLR.

> [!warning] Figure 3.3.1 wording inconsistency
> The course prose says the left pack's SLR is larger, but the figure labels are $2{:}1$ on the left and $20{:}1$ on the right. The displayed SWE values and the course density equation confirm that the left pack has the **smaller SLR but larger density and SWE**.
