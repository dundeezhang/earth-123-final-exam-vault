# 11.3 Direct Effects of Glaciation: Erosional and Depositional

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 11 - Glaciers and Glaciation/00 - Module 11 Overview|Module 11 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Classify landforms by alpine or continental setting and by erosional or depositional origin, then identify the process and ice-flow information recorded by each feature.

## Core Notes

### Erosion and Deposition Through a Glacial Cycle

- Erosional features are often created during glacier growth and expansion as ice advances.
- Depositional features are often created during recession as ice melts and releases meltwater and sediment.
- Alpine glaciers and continental glaciers produce distinguishable groups of landforms.
- **Abrasion** occurs as moving ice grinds against valley walls and floors. Continental glaciers grind against the ground below because they are not normally confined by topography.
- Abrasion is the main glacial erosion process identified in the course.
- A glacier can transport eroded sediment above the ice, within it, or along its base.

### Depositional Features in Alpine Glacier Environments

A **moraine** is an unsorted and unconsolidated pile of sediment deposited through glacier advance and recession. This material is also called **glacial till**.

| Moraine | Position and formation |
|---|---|
| Lateral | Elongated low mound next to a valley glacier, parallel to downslope ice flow; produced by abrasion against confining valley walls. |
| Medial | Dark central or near-central sediment strip formed where two or more lateral moraines merge as tributary glaciers join. |
| Terminal or end | Large sediment pile bulldozed at the glacier toe and left when expansion stops. |
| Recessional moraine system | Series of adjacent terminal or end moraines created through repeated recession and deposition. |

These deposits help reconstruct the sequence of glacier movement, landscape development, and climate change after the ice recedes or disappears.

### Erosional Features in Alpine Glacier Environments

- Glaciers transform stream-carved **V-shaped valleys** into **U-shaped valleys** by eroding and bulldozing the valley. A U-shaped valley is a strong sign of past glaciation.
- **Hanging valley:** upper tributary valley left above a glacially deepened U-shaped valley. It has a steep drop to the lower valley and often, but not always, produces a waterfall.
- **Cirque:** steep-sided bowl-like or amphitheatre-like recess carved at a glacial valley head; snow, ice, or water may occupy its base.
- **Arete:** sharp ridge, often extending from a horn or peak, that separates glacial valleys or watersheds.
- **Horn:** sharp mountain apex produced after cirques and other erosional processes cut around a peak.

### Erosional and Depositional Features in Continental Glacier Environments

Continental ice acts like an immense bulldozer that clears topsoil and sediment down to bedrock. The course emphasizes widespread depositional features and treats the **drumlin** as the most common erosional feature of ice sheets.

#### Drumlins

- Advancing ice can reorganize the surface beneath it into short, streamlined hills shaped like inverted spoons.
- Drumlins provide evidence of the direction of ice advance.
- Drumlin fields appear as groups of low rolling hills; the course points to fields around the Waterloo Region.
- Their origin is still debated because their formation has not been directly witnessed, but the course says most glacial scientists now attribute them to erosion by advancing ice that modifies and reorganizes surface material.

#### Meltwater Deposition

- Melting continental ice releases enormous water flows over, beneath, and through the ice.
- Meltwater transports and deposits large sediment volumes.
- Much deposition occurs below the glacier **terminus**, where meltwater is released and slows.
- The deposited material is **glacial outwash** and can form eskers, kettles, and kames.

| Landform | Definition and process |
|---|---|
| Esker | Long sinuous ridge of partially sorted sand and gravel. The deposits were beds of streams or rivers on top of or within the ice and remain on the land surface after the ice melts. |
| Kettle | Small circular pond or lake where melting ice leaves a local depression that fills with meltwater. |
| Kame | Small conical hill made mainly of sand-sized material. It forms where meltwater exits a glacial river or channel, slows rapidly, and deposits partially sorted sediment. |

These landforms can be identified long after local ice disappears. Carbon or radiometric dating can provide more precise information about when landforms formed and where glaciers existed.

## Common Confusions

- **Erosion vs. deposition:** abrasion removes material; moraines and outwash are deposited material.
- **Till vs. outwash:** moraine till is unsorted and unconsolidated; meltwater outwash can be partially sorted.
- **Moraine positions:** lateral at the sides, medial in the middle after ice flows merge, terminal at the toe.
- **Hanging valleys:** waterfalls are common but are not required.
- **Drumlin vs. esker:** a drumlin is a streamlined hill used to infer ice advance; an esker is a sinuous ridge representing a former glacial stream bed.
- **Esker vs. kame:** both can contain partially sorted meltwater sediment, but an esker is long and sinuous while a kame is a small conical hill.
- **Kettle vs. kame:** a kettle is a water-filled depression; a kame is a deposited sediment hill.
- **Cirque vs. horn:** a cirque is a bowl-like hollow; a horn is the sharp summit remaining after erosion around a peak.
