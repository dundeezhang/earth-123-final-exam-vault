# 11.1 Introduction to Glaciers: Formation, Classification, and Anatomy

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 11 - Glaciers and Glaciation/00 - Module 11 Overview|Module 11 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Define a glacier, apply glacier-budget logic, distinguish alpine glacier forms from continental ice sheets, and explain the zones of accumulation and ablation and the moving equilibrium line.

## Core Notes

### Glacier Definition and Importance

- A **glacier** is a large ice mass made of compacted and recrystallized snow that accumulated over many years without melting and flows slowly under its own weight over geological time.
- Glaciers erode, transport, and deposit Earth materials such as topsoil and sediment.
- They contain most of the world's freshwater and cover approximately **10% of Earth's surface**.
- The course reports declining freshwater stored as ice and declining total ice cover under climate change.
- High-elevation communities in Peru, northern India, Nepal, and Chile are examples of places that rely heavily on glacial meltwater.

### Formation of Glaciers

- Compacted and recrystallized snow is compressed into glacier ice, called **firn** by the course page.
- A glacier forms and grows only with a **positive snow or ice budget**: winter snow and ice accumulation must exceed summer melting.
- A positive budget allows year-over-year increases in ice volume and creates larger, deeper ice masses.
- Valley glaciers can take years or decades to develop; continental ice sheets can take decades or centuries.
- The course states that present glacial ice originated from the last glacial maximum, approximately **20,000 years ago**, and that glaciers can disappear over decades or centuries under climate change.

### Classification of Glaciated Terrain

**Alpine glaciation** occurs at high elevations in mountainous regions. Ice streams occupy confined valleys and may receive tributary glaciers.

| Alpine form | Identifying description |
|---|---|
| Piedmont glacier | Reaches the valley floor and commonly spreads into a lobe. |
| Cirque glacier | Small ice mass confined to a bowl-shaped or amphitheatre-like cirque. |
| Valley glacier | Begins at a cirque, ice cap, or valley head and flows downslope between valley walls; always smaller than a continental glacier or ice sheet. |

The term **valley glacier** can also be used generally for alpine glaciation.

**Continental glaciation** covers large land masses in polar regions such as Antarctica and Greenland. Its ice sheets obscure almost all surface features, including some smaller mountains, and are not confined by topographic obstacles.

### Anatomy of a Glacier

- The **zone of accumulation** is the higher region where winter snow and ice accumulate and survive the warmer summer months.
- The **zone of ablation** is the lower region where summer melting occurs. It is the active area of visible advance or recession and of deposition, melt, erosion, and material transport.
- The glacier's leading edge is the **toe** or **tip**. Scientists monitor it to determine movement and velocity through time.
- The **equilibrium line** separates the two zones: accumulation dominates above it and melting dominates below it.
- The equilibrium line is not fixed. It shifts in elevation with local climate and seasonal temperature; in a warmer-than-average year it can move to a higher elevation because more snow and ice are available for melting.

## Equations and Units

Glacier budget logic can be written as:

$$
\text{net glacier budget}=\text{accumulation}-\text{ablation}
$$

- If accumulation $>$ ablation, the budget is positive and ice volume grows.
- If ablation $>$ accumulation, the budget is negative and the glacier shrinks or recedes.
- If ablation is greater than or equal to accumulation, the glacier does **not** satisfy the course condition for growth and advance.

Key quantities: approximately **10%** Earth-surface coverage and approximately **20,000 years** since the last glacial maximum.

## Common Confusions

- **Flow vs. toe position:** glacier ice flows downslope under its weight, but the toe may advance or recede depending on the budget.
- **Ablation vs. accumulation:** ablation is loss through melting; accumulation is gain of persistent snow and ice.
- **Alpine vs. continental:** topography confines alpine glaciers; ice sheets are not confined by topographic obstacles.
- **Equilibrium line:** it is a shifting boundary, not a permanently fixed elevation.
- A mass confined by mountain topography is not an ice sheet.
