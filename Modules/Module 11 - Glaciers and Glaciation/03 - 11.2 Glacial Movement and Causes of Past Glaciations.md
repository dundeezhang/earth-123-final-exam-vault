# 11.2 Glacial Movement and Causes of Past Glaciations

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 11 - Glaciers and Glaciation/00 - Module 11 Overview|Module 11 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

Compare basal slip and plastic flow, identify controls on glacier velocity, explain plate-tectonic and Milankovitch controls on glaciation, and use landscape and crustal evidence to reconstruct past ice.

## Core Notes

### Glacial Movement

Glacier movement has two components:

| Movement | Location and process | Exam distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Basal slip** | Meltwater builds at the ice-ground interface, acts as a lubricant and hydraulic jack, lifts ice, and helps it slide downslope. High pressure can produce melting below $0^\circ\text{C}$. | Requires movement at the bed; Figure 11.2.1 labels it **sliding**. |
| **Plastic flow** | Permanent deformation and internal downslope flow occur within the glacier body and can create surface cracks or crevasses. | Primary movement mechanism for both alpine and continental glaciers; Figure 11.2.1 labels it **internal flow**. |

- Most glacier movement combines plastic flow and basal slip.
- A glacier frozen to the ground moves only by plastic flow.
- Plastic flow generally produces much more movement than basal slip.
- Valley glaciers commonly move faster than continental ice sheets.
- The top of the glacier moves farther downslope over a given time than the bottom.
- Normal downslope velocity is **0-4 m/day**. A short glacial surge can increase this by **10-100 times**.
- The course associates surges with major warming events that produce excessive meltwater at the surface, sides, and bottom of the ice.

Three factors control movement rate:

1. **Slope:** a steeper surface increases the effect of gravity on basal slip and plastic deformation.
2. **Total ice volume:** more ice in the accumulation zone raises internal pressure and pushes lower ice outward, like pancake batter spreading under its own weight.
3. **Temperature:** warmer ice provides more meltwater and easier slip and deformation; colder basal ice can freeze to the ground and impede movement.

### Glacial History

- Earth has experienced many glaciation cycles, including near-global **Snowball Earth** conditions.
- The course states that more than **20 glaciation cycles** occurred during roughly the last **100,000 years**.
- At the last major Canadian glacial maximum approximately **20,000 years ago**, ice covered almost all Canada and extended from the North Pole into the central United States.
- The course identifies two principal Canadian dispersion points, one west of Hudson Bay at Iqaluit and one east of Hudson Bay in northern Quebec, from which ice flowed outward.
- Alaska remained mostly ice-free because western North American mountain ranges blocked westward ice movement from the central Canadian dispersion area.
- The ice altered rivers and valleys through erosion and meltwater, produced moraines, shaped the Niagara Escarpment, and helped carve the Great Lakes.

### Causes of Glaciation and Milankovitch Cycles

The course identifies two main controls on the occurrence and duration of glaciation cycles:

1. **Plate tectonics:** glaciation commonly begins on land, so continents near polar regions favour glacier formation. Continental positions also control major ocean and atmospheric currents and therefore climate. Plate motion is extremely slow and occurs over geological time.
2. **Milankovitch cycles:** long-term changes in Earth's orbital parameters alter climate and glacial cycles.

Greenland and Antarctica retain continental glaciation. The course classifies North Pole ice as an **ice cap**, rather than an ice sheet or continental glacier, because it is thinner and floats on the Arctic Ocean instead of resting on solid land.

Plate movement can be reconstructed using fossils, sediment layers, volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, glacier formations, GPS, satellites, and digital global mapping.

Milankovitch cycles, named for Serbian scientist Milutin Milankovitch, have three components:

| Component | Course description | Alternate term |
|---|---|---|
| Orbital changes | Earth's orbit shifts between more circular and more elliptical, altering energy received at different times of year. | Eccentricity |
| Planet's axis | The tilt angle grows or shrinks and changes solar energy reaching polar regions. The course states that a smaller angle permits more sunlight at the poles. Present tilt is approximately **23.5 degrees**. | Obliquity |
| Direction of axis alignment | The direction of Earth's rotational axis migrates over geological time; it currently points toward the North Star. | Precession |

- The three parameters change slowly and operate together.
- Figure 11.2.4 spans the present to **1 million years ago**; `kyr ago` means thousands of years ago.
- In that graph, peaks represent warmer portions and troughs represent cooler portions of each cycle.
- Together, the cycles increase or decrease summer solar forcing in the Northern Hemisphere at **65 degrees N**.
- A warming alignment sends more sunlight northward, melts snow and ice, and reduces glacial volume. A cooling alignment sends less sunlight northward and permits greater snow and ice growth.

### Evidence of Past Glaciation

Evidence ranges from continental-scale features to local landforms:

- **Large basins:** the Great Lakes and New York's Finger Lakes record the action of moving glacial ice.
- **Sediment displacement:** ice acted like a bulldozer, moving loose topsoil and sediment tens or hundreds of kilometres. Repeated glaciations stripped much of the Canadian Shield and deposited material farther south.
- **Crustal rebound:** the mass of ice compressed crust into the asthenosphere or upper mantle. After melting, the unloaded surface began to rise, like a mattress or pillow recovering after pressure is removed.
- **Modern uplift:** the Hudson Bay region is still rising at approximately **1 cm/year**, although peak ice occurred about **20,000 years ago** and the ice had melted by about **12,000 years ago**.
- **Peripheral subsidence:** areas including southern Saskatchewan and the east coast are lowering. In the pillow analogy, edges that rose under central pressure drop after that pressure is removed.
- **Raised beaches and beach ridges:** shorelines now distant from water record former meltwater lakes.
- **Glacial Lake Agassiz:** a vast inland lake formed as the North American ice sheet melted. Its remnant waters include Lake Winnipeg and adjacent water bodies.

## Equations and Units

For a surge estimate:

$$
v_{\text{surge}}=v_{\text{normal}}\times M
$$

where $v_{\text{normal}}$ is normally $0$-$4\ \text{m/day}$ and $M$ is a surge multiplier of $10$-$100$. At the upper stated normal rate of $4\ \text{m/day}$, the stated multipliers correspond to $40$-$400\ \text{m/day}$.

Other quantities to retain:

- Pressure can allow basal melting below $0^\circ\text{C}$.
- More than 20 glaciation cycles in roughly 100,000 years, as stated by the course.
- Last major glacial maximum: approximately 20,000 years ago.
- North American ice gone by approximately 12,000 years ago.
- Present axial tilt: approximately 23.5 degrees.
- Hudson Bay-area uplift: approximately $1\ \text{cm/year}$.

## Common Confusions

- **Plastic flow vs. basal slip:** internal deformation can occur even when the glacier is frozen to its bed; basal slip cannot.
- **Glacier flow vs. glacial surge:** ordinary movement is slow and persistent; a surge is a short 10-100-fold acceleration.
- **Eccentricity, obliquity, precession:** shape, angle, direction.
- **Ice cap vs. ice sheet in this course:** North Pole ice floats on ocean water; continental ice sheets rest on land.
- **Rebound vs. subsidence:** central formerly loaded regions can rise while peripheral regions that had bulged upward can fall.
- Plate tectonics and Milankovitch cycles are controls on long-term glaciation; modern human climate impacts are discussed separately from those natural processes.
