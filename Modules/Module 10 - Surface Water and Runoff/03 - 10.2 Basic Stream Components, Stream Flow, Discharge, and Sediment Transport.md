# 10.2 Basic Stream Components, Stream Flow, Discharge, and Sediment Transport

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]
Module: [[Modules/Module 10 - Surface Water and Runoff/00 - Module 10 Overview|Module 10 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

> [!important]
> Know the downstream stream-profile changes, laminar versus turbulent flow, the three velocity controls, the location of maximum velocity, and every sediment-transport term. Be ready to distinguish capacity from competence and to explain the clay anomaly in Figure 10.2.7.

## Core Notes

### Stream Components and Long Profile

- Streams and rivers carry water, sediment, and contaminants from higher to lower elevations while erosion and deposition modify terrain.
- Familiar channel components include tributaries, the main channel, meanders, banks, the bed, and the flood plain.
- **Figure 10.2.1:** a typical stream long profile is concave upward. It begins with a steeper gradient near the headwaters and gradually flattens downstream.
- Lower downstream gradient encourages more frequent and variable meanders and a wider flood plain. Over geological time, a channel can migrate from one flood-plain edge to the other.
- Streams function as geological conveyor belts that move large volumes of sediment and other Earth material downslope.

![Conceptual stream long profile from steep headwaters to a broad meandering flood plain](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.2.1-stream-long-profile.jpg)

*Figure 10.2.1. Conceptual profile and components of a common stream. Source: Tarbuck and Lutgens, 2002, p. 271.*

**What to notice:** Gradient decreases downstream while the valley and flood plain widen. Meanders become more prominent where the lower course is gentler.

### Laminar and Turbulent Flow

| Flow type | Pattern | Typical setting and effect |
|---|---|---|
| Laminar | Water moves in streamlined, parallel layers with little or no mixing between adjacent depths. | Associated with slower velocity and very little erosion; rare in nature but reproducible under controlled laboratory conditions. |
| Turbulent | Water mixes across adjacent layers and swirls or eddies around obstacles. | Caused by uneven beds and banks; supports greater erosion and transport; nearly all natural stream and river flow is turbulent. |

- **Figure 10.2.2:** roughness along the bed and banks is associated with turbulent flow, while the unobstructed middle of the flow can appear more laminar.

![River cross-section comparing streamlined laminar flow with turbulent eddies near rough boundaries](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.2.2-laminar-turbulent-flow.jpg)

*Figure 10.2.2. Conceptual river cross-section showing laminar and turbulent flow. Source: Tarbuck et al., 2012, p. 351.*

**What to notice:** Bed and bank roughness disrupts parallel flow and produces eddies. Natural channels are therefore dominated by turbulence, especially near irregular boundaries and obstacles.

- Velocity is usually lowest near the bed and banks and increases toward the channel centre. The highest velocity commonly lies just below the surface in the main flow path.
- **Figure 10.2.3:** maximum velocity shifts laterally with channel curvature. It moves toward the outside of bends, where the longer travel path requires faster movement, while the inside corner is slower.
- This velocity pattern produces outside-bank erosion and inside-bank deposition, driving meander migration.

![Plan and cross-sectional diagrams showing river flow paths and zones of maximum velocity](../../Assets/Module%2010/channel-velocity-paths.png)

*Figure 10.2.3. River flow paths and locations of maximum velocity. Source: Earle, 2015.*

**What to notice:** The fastest thread of water follows the outside of each bend and sits below the surface away from boundary friction. As the channel curves, that high-velocity zone shifts from one bank to the other.

### Three Controls on Velocity

1. **Gradient:** steeper slopes permit higher velocity; flatter slopes permit lower velocity and the lateral meandering associated with low-gradient flood plains.
2. **Channel features:** narrowing or restriction generally increases velocity, whereas widening downstream generally decreases it. Rough beds and banks create more friction, turbulence, and erosion than smooth surfaces.
3. **Discharge:** discharge is the water volume passing an observation point per time. The course describes discharge as directly proportional to velocity and notes that larger water volumes during storms or floods increase both discharge and velocity.

### Discharge

- Discharge is commonly measured in $\mathrm{m^3/s}$ and is one of the principal metrics in stream hydrology.
- Discharge helps assess flood potential, possible water extraction, erosional strength, and the ability to carry sediments and contaminants.
- **Figure 10.2.4:** discharge depends on channel cross-sectional area and representative velocity; maximum velocity is measured near the centre just below the surface.
- For a simple channel, cross-sectional area is width multiplied by average depth. Dividing an irregular channel into subsections gives a more accurate result.
- The saved page says its worked calculation will appear in Section 10.4; in the locally saved course sequence, the actual velocity-area worked example appears in Section 10.3.

![Channel cross-section labeled with width, depth, and maximum velocity for discharge calculation](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.2.4-discharge-cross-section.png)

*Figure 10.2.4. Conceptual channel cross-section used to illustrate discharge. Source: Tarbuck and Lutgens, 2002, p. 269.*

**What to notice:** Discharge is cross-sectional area multiplied by a representative average velocity, $Q=A\bar v$. The maximum-velocity point helps show the flow pattern, but using that fastest value for the whole section would overestimate discharge.

### Sediment Loads and Transport

- A stream's **load** consists of sediment and other transported material. Measuring it reveals information about upslope erosion, downslope deposition, contaminant movement, agricultural and urban runoff, and flood impacts.
- **Figure 10.2.5:** two main transport modes are bed load and suspended load, and relative velocity increases away from the bed.

| Mode or process | Testable description |
|---|---|
| Bed load | Sand and larger grains stay at the bed and move gradually downslope. |
| Traction | Bed-load particles roll or slide along the bed without entering the main flow. |
| Saltation | Slightly smaller bed-load particles hop briefly into the flow and return to the bed; impacts can dislodge other grains. |
| Suspended or dissolved load | Material remains within the main flow rather than moving along the bed. The lesson groups fine suspended silt and clay under this label. |

![River cross-section showing traction, saltation, suspended load, and increasing velocity above the bed](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.2.5-sediment-transport.jpg)

*Figure 10.2.5. Bed load and suspended load in a flowing channel. Source: McConnell and Steer, 2015, p. 296.*

**What to notice:** Coarser grains roll, slide, or hop close to the bed, while finer particles remain suspended higher in the flow. Velocity increases away from the bed because boundary friction weakens.

- Higher velocity can mobilize larger cobbles and boulders, move larger particles by saltation, or suspend grains that otherwise remain on the bed.
- **Figure 10.2.6, Powder River:** at an average discharge of only $100\ \mathrm{ft^3/s}$, the river carried approximately 1,000 tons of sediment per day in the 1956 data. That load can also contain urban waste, spills, and agricultural runoff.

![Graph of Powder River discharge against total suspended sediment load](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.2.6-powder-river-sediment-load.png)

*Figure 10.2.6. Powder River discharge and suspended sediment load, based on 1956 data. Source: Tarbuck and Lutgens, 2002, p. 284; Leopold and Maddock, 1953, p. 20; colourized by the Course Author, University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** Suspended sediment load rises sharply as discharge increases. Read both axes carefully because even moderate flow can transport hundreds to thousands of tons per day.

- **Capacity** is the amount of material the flow carries. **Competence** is the maximum particle size it can transport. Both increase with channel velocity and discharge in the course discussion.
- **Figure 10.2.7:** erosion, transport, and deposition depend mainly, but not entirely, on average grain size. Fine clay can require a velocity as high as or higher than gravel, pebbles, and small boulders before erosion begins.
- Closely packed clay forms a smooth surface with few cracks or crevices. Water cannot readily flow around individual grains to lift them, so erosion requires unexpectedly high velocity.

![Graph relating stream velocity and particle size to erosion, transport, and deposition](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.2.7-erosion-transport-deposition.png)

*Figure 10.2.7. Relationship between grain size, stream velocity, erosion, transport, and deposition. Source: Earle, 2015.*

**What to notice:** The erosion threshold is U-shaped rather than steadily increasing with grain size. Clay is difficult to detach when packed together, but once suspended it remains transportable at very low velocities.

## Equations and Units

For a simple rectangular channel section:

$$
A=w d
$$

The module's discharge relationship is:

$$
Q=A\bar{v}
$$

where $Q$ is discharge in $\mathrm{m^3/s}$, $A$ is cross-sectional area in $\mathrm{m^2}$, and $\bar{v}$ is representative average velocity in $\mathrm{m/s}$.

> [!tip]
> Natural cross-sections are irregular. Treating the entire channel as one width-depth rectangle is an approximation; Section 10.3 improves accuracy by summing thin subsection discharges.

## Common Confusions

- **Upstream vs. downstream profile:** the upstream headwater region is steeper; the downstream profile is gentler and usually has a wider flood plain and more meanders.
- **Fastest water location:** velocity is not highest against the bed or banks. It is generally highest just below the surface near the central flow path and shifts to the outside of a bend.
- **Laminar vs. turbulent:** laminar layers do not mix; turbulent flow mixes and eddies. Nearly all natural channel flow is turbulent.
- **Width vs. velocity:** with the course's channel-feature comparison, a constriction increases velocity and a wider reach decreases it.
- **Bed load vs. suspended load:** bed load remains at or near the bed; suspended material is carried within the flow.
- **Traction vs. saltation:** traction rolls or slides; saltation hops.
- **Capacity vs. competence:** capacity means total amount; competence means maximum particle size.
- **Easy-to-transport vs. easy-to-erode clay:** once suspended, fine particles can travel far, but tightly packed clay can be difficult to erode initially.
