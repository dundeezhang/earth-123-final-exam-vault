# 10.1 Introduction to Surface Water and Runoff

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 10 - Surface Water and Runoff/00 - Module 10 Overview|Module 10 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

> [!important]
> Know the sources and uses of surface water, how meander migration occurs, the four runoff components, and why urbanization both increases flow volume and decreases lag time. Be able to interpret Figures 10.1.2 and 10.1.5.

## Core Notes

### Surface-Water Sources, Pathways, and Uses

- **Surface water** includes creeks, streams, rivers, ponds, lakes, swamps, and marshes. Healthy freshwater supports humans, animals, plants, crops, and ecosystems.
- Rain, snowmelt, and groundwater springs can channelize into rills and gullies, then streams and river valleys. Small channels merge into larger rivers that eventually reach the ocean.
- A **tributary** is a smaller stream or river entering a larger water body. Tributary networks show drainage paths and help define watershed boundaries.
- **Figure 10.1.1:** all rain, snowmelt, or spring water originating inside the Mississippi River watershed boundary is routed south toward the Gulf of Mexico. The network also transports contamination, waste, and eroded topsoil. Its southern purple region is especially flood-prone during heavy rain, hurricanes, and spring snowmelt because water from the large northern area combines toward a smaller outlet region.

| Surface-water role | Course emphasis |
|---|---|
| Drinking water | Clean surface water supports human and animal consumption in communities and on farms. |
| Irrigation | Farms use substantial surface-water volumes to water crops and agricultural products. |
| Ecological habitats | Rivers, ponds, and lakes support aquatic, amphibian, and terrestrial life. The shoreline and immediately adjacent **riparian zone** is often highly diverse. |
| Transport | Flow carries sediment, nutrients, contaminants, wastes, and fertilizers long distances, making rivers major pollution pathways from urban and rural areas. |
| Flood control | Normally unfilled channels temporarily store and transport excess stormwater before banks are overtopped. |
| Power generation | Hydroelectric systems use gravity and water pressure; fossil-fuel and nuclear plants use freshwater to produce steam that spins turbines. |
| Recreation | Boating, fishing, camping, swimming, and aesthetic use can strengthen public interest in protection and management. |

### Stream Migration and Flood Plains

- A **flood plain** is land nearest or adjacent to a river shoreline that floods periodically during high water or major storms. It is often fertile but hazardous for buildings and infrastructure.
- Modern urban design attempts to preserve flood plains as natural vegetation, parks, or recreational fields that are less vulnerable to future floods.
- **Figure 10.1.2:** an approximately 28-year Google Earth sequence of Peru's Ucayali River shows substantial lateral migration across its flood plain.
- At a meander, velocity is higher on the **outside bank**, causing erosion and channel widening. Lower velocity on the **inside corner** permits deposition. Acting together, erosion and deposition shift the channel laterally.
- A meander cutoff can leave a small curved **oxbow lake**.
- Long-term shoreline evolution should be checked before purchasing or building property near a river because the channel can migrate beneath infrastructure.

### Laurel Creek Example

- **Figure 10.1.3, May 2013:** Laurel Creek undercut its right bank near Waterloo's Weber Street bridge. Tilting steel fencing and cracks in the adjacent parking lot showed slope failure. A nearby monitoring shack collected stream velocity and stage data.
- **Figure 10.1.4, September 2016:** four stacked rows of large rectangular boulders armoured the eroding bank; the upper slope and parking surface were rebuilt, failed fencing was removed, and the monitoring shack was moved across the creek.
- The street sewer shown in Figure 10.1.4 collects polluted Weber Street runoff and discharges it through a short pipe directly into Laurel Creek, without filtration or an opportunity for pollutants to settle.

### Runoff Components

- **Runoff** is the water volume carried in or delivered to a channel or water body at a location over a measured time.
- River and large-water-source studies commonly express runoff as volume per time, such as $\mathrm{m^3/s}$.
- Smaller precipitation, evaporation, and water-balance studies can use depth per time, such as $\mathrm{cm/s}$, because depth is directly comparable with $P$ and $ET$.

| Component | Process |
|---|---|
| Direct precipitation | Rain reaches the surface, moves downslope, and can channelize into larger overland flows. |
| Interflow | Surface and flood water moves through shallow subsurface material parallel to the surface when soil is saturated from depth to surface. |
| Groundwater discharge | Aquifer springs or re-emerging shallow interflow add water to the surface, especially where slopes intersect subsurface water. |
| Snowmelt | Melting snow and ice produces runoff and channelization, particularly at high elevation or in high headwaters. |

### Human Effects and Urbanization

- **Dams, flow controls, and channelization** alter natural flow. Although intended to protect people and assets, concrete channels and faster directed pathways can reduce ecological diversity and accelerate pollution delivery.
- **Agriculture and irrigation** can overuse impounded surface supplies and contaminate runoff with chemical and organic fertilizers.
- **Urbanization** expands asphalt, concrete, roofs, and eaves. These impervious surfaces limit infiltration, collect precipitation, and rapidly direct water to streams and rivers.
- **Figure 10.1.5:** compared with pre-urban conditions, urbanization produces a larger flow peak and a shorter lag time.
- The shorter **lag time** results from rapid routing through impervious surfaces and stormwater systems instead of infiltration and groundwater storage.
- The larger urban flow volume occurs because little precipitation infiltrates; more of the storm volume reaches nearby channels quickly and can exceed their capacity.

## Equations and Units

The section defines runoff using either of these dimensions:

$$
\text{runoff}=\frac{\text{water volume}}{\text{time}}
\quad \text{such as } \mathrm{m^3/s}
$$

$$
\text{runoff}=\frac{\text{water depth}}{\text{time}}
\quad \text{such as } \mathrm{cm/s}
$$

- Use volume per time for rivers and large water sources.
- Use depth per time when comparing runoff directly with precipitation $P$ or evapotranspiration $ET$.

## Common Confusions

- **Tributary vs. main channel:** a tributary enters a larger stream, river, or water body; the main channel carries the combined flow toward the outlet.
- **Riparian zone vs. flood plain:** the riparian zone is the shoreline and immediately adjacent ecological area; the flood plain is land periodically inundated during high-water events.
- **Outside vs. inside meander:** faster outside-bank flow erodes; slower inside-bank flow deposits.
- **Runoff components vs. human controls:** direct precipitation, interflow, groundwater discharge, and snowmelt are runoff components. Dams, agriculture, and urbanization are human influences.
- **Urban peak flow vs. lag time:** urbanization raises the peak flow but shortens the lag time.
- **Armouring vs. filtration:** the boulders at Laurel Creek protect the bank from erosion; they do not filter roadway runoff entering through the sewer.
