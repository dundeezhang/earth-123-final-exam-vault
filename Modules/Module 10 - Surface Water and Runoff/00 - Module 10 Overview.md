## Highest-Yield Exam Points

- Surface water comes from rain, snowmelt, and groundwater springs; tributaries combine water and transported material within a watershed and ultimately route it toward an outlet.
- Meanders migrate because faster flow erodes the outside bank while slower flow deposits sediment on the inside bank; cutoffs can form oxbow lakes.
- Runoff has four main components: direct precipitation, shallow interflow, groundwater discharge, and snowmelt.
- Urbanization expands impervious surfaces, reduces infiltration, shortens lag time, and increases peak flow and flood risk.
- Nearly all natural streamflow is turbulent. Velocity is generally lowest near rough beds and banks and highest just below the surface near the main flow path.
- Stream velocity is controlled mainly by gradient, channel size and roughness, and discharge. Maximum velocity shifts toward the outside of a meander.
- Bed load moves by traction and saltation; fine material travels as suspended or dissolved load. Capacity is the total load, while competence is the largest particle that can be transported.
- Fine clay can require unexpectedly high erosional velocity because tightly packed particles form a smooth, cohesive surface that water cannot easily get around.
- Discharge is cross-sectional area times average velocity. Accurate field work divides a channel into roughly 20-30 slices, each 3%-5% of total width, and sums subsection discharges.
- Average subsection velocity is best represented by measurements at 20% and 80% of depth; one measurement at 60% depth is an acceptable but less accurate substitute.
- A stage–discharge relationship estimates discharge from water-surface elevation but is valid only near the measurement section used to construct it.
- A return period is an annual probability, not a schedule. A 1-in-50-year flood has a 2% chance in every year.

## Equations and Quantitative Skills

$$
A_i=w_i d_i
$$

$$
\bar{v}_i=\frac{v_{0.2D,i}+v_{0.8D,i}}{2}
$$

$$
Q_i=A_i\bar{v}_i
\qquad
Q_{\text{total}}=\sum_i Q_i
$$

$$
H_{\text{stage}}=H-H_0
$$

- Keep area in $\mathrm{m^2}$ and velocity in $\mathrm{m/s}$ so discharge is in $\mathrm{m^3/s}$.
- Convert discharge to volume by multiplying by elapsed time: seconds per hour, day, or month as required.
- Interpret a stage–discharge graph by locating stage on its axis and reading the estimated discharge from the regression line.
- Convert a return period $T$ to annual probability using the course examples: $P=1/T$ and multiply by 100 for percent.
- Read hydrograph shape: peak height represents flood magnitude, width represents duration, and slope reflects how quickly floodwater arrives or recedes.
