# 10.4 Flooding and Flood Hydrographs

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]
Module: [[Modules/Module 10 - Surface Water and Runoff/00 - Module 10 Overview|Module 10 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

> [!important]
> Know how to interpret return periods, distinguish the five flood processes, compare structural and non-structural mitigation, and read peak height, width, slope, baseflow, and quickflow on a hydrograph. Connect urbanization with higher peaks and shorter lag times.

## Core Notes

### Flooding and Annual Probability

- Stream and river floods are natural and inevitable when water overtops channel banks and spreads into the adjacent flood plain.
- Triggers include long-duration storms, heavy precipitation, snowmelt, and failure of naturally or artificially impounded dams.
- A **return period** is a statistical annual probability, not the guaranteed interval between events.
- A **1-in-50-year flood** has a $1/50=0.02$, or **2%**, chance of occurring in every year. More than one such event can occur within 50 years.
- Flooding occurs when tributaries, overland flow, groundwater, and direct precipitation deliver water faster than downstream flow, evaporation, and infiltration can remove it, or when the channel is blocked.

### Five Flood Processes

| Process | Cause and spatial effect |
|---|---|
| Regional flood | Broad meteorological events such as heavy storms and runoff, rapid snowmelt, or rain-on-snow. |
| Local flash flood | Localized, intense precipitation that floods a small area or limited watershed rather than a region. |
| Ice-jam flood | Ice fragments temporarily obstruct and impound a channel, often where bridges or culverts restrict flow. |
| Constructed-dam failure | Rare but potentially catastrophic downstream flooding after an artificial dam fails. |
| Natural-dam flood | A landslide, glacial event, or other natural blockage floods upstream as water accumulates and floods downstream if the blockage breaches. |

### Canadian Flood Risk and Mitigation

- **Figure 10.4.1:** four Canadian areas experienced many major 20th-century floods: the Winnipeg region; southern and southwestern Ontario; Montreal and Quebec City; and most of New Brunswick and bordering areas.

![Map showing the locations and numbers of major twentieth-century flood events across Canada](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.4.1-canadian-flood-disasters.jpg)

*Figure 10.4.1. Locations and numbers of major Canadian flood events during the twentieth century. Source: Brooks et al., 2001, p. 113.*

**What to notice:** Flood disasters cluster around Winnipeg, southern Ontario, the St. Lawrence corridor, and New Brunswick. The pattern shows where major recorded events were concentrated, not a complete map of all possible flood hazards.

- Structural mitigation includes artificial levees, flood-control dams, artificial channelization, and floodways.
- The course identifies **non-structural flood-plain management** as the most cost-effective option where it is still possible. It limits new residential, commercial, or industrial construction in known flood plains.
- Flood plains can instead remain natural or support parks, trails, and recreational fields. This approach is less useful where development already occupies the flood plain.

### Hydrology Highlight: Winnipeg Floodway

- Repeated Red River floods at Winnipeg are commonly caused by excessive snowmelt and rain-on-snow.
- A 1950 flood caused more than **$100 million** in damage and displaced thousands of residents.
- Completed in the 1960s, the floodway is almost **50 km** long and diverts excess water around the east side of the city.
- It cost approximately **$60 million** at the time; the course states that inflation would put that cost above half a billion dollars today.
- Since completion, it has saved Winnipeg from flooding more than **20 times**, including the major 1997 event.

### Flood Hydrographs

- A hydrograph plots streamflow or discharge over time and helps show flood evolution. It can be paired with precipitation data to show the rainfall-runoff response.
- **Figure 10.4.2:** **baseflow** is normal river flow; **quickflow** is the floodwater volume above it.
- A higher quickflow peak means greater flood magnitude.
- A narrow quickflow response means the flood wave moved through quickly. A wide response means the flood took longer to subside and land remained inundated longer.
- A steep rising limb indicates faster onset of floodwater.

![Conceptual flood hydrograph labeled with baseflow, quickflow, rising limb, peak, and recession](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.4.2-flood-hydrograph.jpeg)

*Figure 10.4.2. Conceptual flood hydrograph separating quickflow from baseflow. Source: Hendriks, 2010, p. 244.*

**What to notice:** Quickflow is the event response above the baseflow line. Peak height shows flood magnitude, curve width shows duration, and rising-limb steepness shows how quickly flooding begins.

- Human interference through urbanization, impervious surfaces, clear-cut deforestation, and flow-control structures changes flood timing and volume.
- **Figure 10.4.3:** compared with the blue rural hydrograph, the yellow urbanized hydrograph has a much shorter lag time and higher discharge.

![Rural and urbanized hydrographs showing a higher earlier peak after development](../../Assets/Module%2010/urbanization-hydrograph.png)

*Figure 10.4.3. Rural and urbanized hydrographs after human interference. Source: Oke et al., 2017, p. 250.*

**What to notice:** The urbanized response peaks higher and sooner because rapid surface routing replaces slower infiltration and storage. A shorter lag time means the stream responds more quickly after precipitation, while the larger peak means more water arrives near the same time.

## Equations and Units

For a return period of $T$ years, the annual probability pattern used by the course is:

$$
P=\frac{1}{T}
$$

$$
P(\%)=\frac{1}{T}\times100
$$

> [!example]
> For $T=50$ years, $P=1/50=0.02=2\%$ in each year.

Hydrographs use flow or discharge on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. Interpret:

- peak height as flood magnitude;
- quickflow width as flood duration;
- rising-limb steepness as speed of onset;
- lag time as the delay between precipitation and the stream response.

## Common Confusions

- **Return period vs. schedule:** “1 in 50 years” means a 2% chance each year, not exactly one event every 50 years.
- **Regional vs. local flash flood:** regional floods affect broad areas and may involve storms, snowmelt, or rain-on-snow; flash floods are localized and intense.
- **Ice jam vs. natural dam:** an ice jam is specifically an ice-fragment obstruction; natural dams include landslides and glacial events.
- **Upstream vs. downstream natural-dam flooding:** impoundment floods upstream; breach or failure sends floodwater downstream.
- **Structural vs. non-structural mitigation:** levees, dams, channels, and floodways alter infrastructure; flood-plain management limits vulnerable land use.
- **Baseflow vs. quickflow:** baseflow is normal flow; quickflow is event-driven floodwater.
- **Peak height vs. response width:** height shows magnitude; width shows duration.
- **Urban hydrograph:** urbanization produces a higher peak and shorter lag time, not a higher peak with a longer delay.
