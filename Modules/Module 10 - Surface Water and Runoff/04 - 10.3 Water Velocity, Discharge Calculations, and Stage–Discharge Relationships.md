# 10.3 Water Velocity, Discharge Calculations, and Stage–Discharge Relationships

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 10 - Surface Water and Runoff/00 - Module 10 Overview|Module 10 Overview]]

## Exam Focus

> [!important]
> Be able to calculate subsection area, average velocity, subsection discharge, and total discharge. Memorize the 20%-80% and 60% depth methods, the recommended subsection size, and why a stage–discharge relationship is site-specific.

## Core Notes

### Why Velocity-Area Measurement Is Needed

- Stream discharge $Q$ depends on channel shape, width, roughness, gradient, water volume, and flow velocity.
- Discharge is the total water volume travelling through a channel per unit time, commonly $\mathrm{m^3/s}$.
- **Figure 10.3.1:** velocity varies from bank to bank, between straight and meandering reaches, and with depth. Frictional resistance from bed and bank material slows water near channel boundaries.
- Because neither depth nor velocity is uniform across a natural channel, one whole-channel measurement can be unrepresentative.

![Plan and cross-sectional diagrams showing how river velocity varies through bends and with depth](../../Assets/Module%2010/channel-velocity-paths.png)

*Figure 10.3.1. General water-velocity patterns in plan and cross-section. Source: Earle, 2015.*

**What to notice:** Maximum velocity lies below the surface and shifts toward the outside bank through a bend. Slower water near the bed and banks means a single fastest-point reading cannot represent the whole channel.

### Field Measurement Procedure

1. Stretch a measuring tape across the channel and divide the cross-section into narrow vertical rectangles or **slices**, as in Figure 10.3.2.
2. Use equal subsection widths and measure the changing depth at each subsection midpoint.
3. Measure velocity with a current meter at the chosen depth or depths in each subsection.
4. Stand downstream of the meter so the observer does not disturb the approaching flow. **Figure 10.3.3** shows Dr. Brewster Conant, Jr. collecting data in the Pine River, with water moving left to right.
5. Calculate each subsection area, representative velocity, and discharge.
6. Sum every subsection discharge to obtain total channel discharge.

![Irregular stream cross-section divided into narrow vertical slices for velocity-area measurement](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.3.2-velocity-area-slices.jpeg)

*Figure 10.3.2. Stream cross-section divided into slices for discharge measurement. Source: Water Science School, 2018.*

**What to notice:** Each slice has its own width, depth, area, and representative velocity. Calculate $Q_i=A_i\bar v_i$ for every slice, then add the $Q_i$ values to obtain total discharge.

![Hydrologist standing downstream of a current meter while measuring a river subsection](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.3.3-current-meter-fieldwork.jpeg)

*Figure 10.3.3. Dr. Brewster Conant, Jr. measuring depth and velocity in the Pine River, Angus, Ontario. Source: Rudolph, n.d.*

**What to notice:** The meter is placed in the selected subsection while the observer stands downstream. This position keeps the observer from disturbing the water before it reaches the instrument.

- The most accurate representative velocity taught in the course is the average of measurements at **20% and 80% of subsection depth**.
- If conditions make two readings unsafe or impractical, one reading at **60% of depth** is acceptable, but less accurate.
- Recommended slices are each **3%-5% of total channel width**, producing approximately **20-30 subsections**.

### Worked Course Example

- **Figure 10.3.4:** a $7\ \mathrm{m}$-wide stream is divided into seven subsections, each $1\ \mathrm{m}$ wide.
- Velocities are measured at 20% and 80% of depth at each subsection midpoint.

![Seven-section stream cross-section used for a worked discharge calculation](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.3.4-discharge-worked-cross-section.jpeg)

*Figure 10.3.4. Stream cross-section divided into seven subsections. Source: Hendriks, 2010, p. 230.*

**What to notice:** For each 1 m slice, multiply width by midpoint depth to get area, average the two velocity readings, then multiply area by that average velocity. Total discharge is the sum of the seven subsection discharges, not their average.

|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.5 | 1.5 | 1.50 | 1.500 |
| 2 | 1.5 | 1.5 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 1.75 | 2.625 |
| 3 | 2.0 | 2.0 | 1.5 | 2.5 | 2.00 | 4.000 |
| 4 | 2.0 | 2.0 | 1.5 | 2.5 | 2.00 | 4.000 |
| 5 | 2.0 | 2.0 | 1.5 | 2.5 | 2.00 | 4.000 |
| 6 | 1.5 | 1.5 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 1.75 | 2.625 |

### Stage–Discharge Relationships

- Direct discharge surveys are time-consuming, labour-intensive, seasonal, and sometimes unsafe or impossible in cold, frozen, or inaccessible channels.
- **Stage** is the channel water-surface height, elevation, or depth. Figure 10.3.5 shows a measuring stick fixed under a bridge beside a glacial river in Peru.

![Measuring stick fixed beneath a bridge beside a glacial river in Peru](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.3.5-stage-gauge-peru.jpeg)

*Figure 10.3.5. Simple stage-measuring stick beside a glacial river in Peru. Source: Course Author.*

**What to notice:** A fixed scale makes water level quick to read at the same location over time. Stage is measured relative to a defined local datum, so the reference elevation must stay consistent.

- **Figure 10.3.6:** a stilling well can record stage digitally at intervals from seconds to weeks, store a remotely accessible record, and continue measuring through winter because it is sheltered and can have inlets below ice.

![Diagram of a stilling well connected to a river for sheltered stage measurement](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.3.6-stilling-well.jpeg)

*Figure 10.3.6. Example stilling-well setup. Source: Hendriks, 2010, p. 227.*

**What to notice:** Inlets let the well water level track the river while sheltering the recorder from waves, debris, weather, and surface ice. The instrument records stage continuously, but discharge still comes from a locally calibrated relationship.

- A stage–discharge relationship uses paired field measurements of discharge $Q$ at known stages $H$. A regression line estimates discharge between measured points.
- **Figure 10.3.7:** measured values are shown as diamonds, with a fitted line used to estimate $Q$ at other stages.
- This relationship supports faster discharge estimates and flood-hazard prediction, but it applies **only near the channel section and gauge used to create it**.
- True stage is water-surface elevation above the local datum. In the course example, a surface at $155\ \mathrm{m}$ above sea level and a riverbed or stilling-well bottom at $140\ \mathrm{m}$ gives a stage of $15\ \mathrm{m}$.
- **Figure 10.3.8 knowledge check:** at a recorded stage of $30\ \mathrm{m}$, the graph estimates discharge as $70\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$.

![Rating curve with measured stage-discharge points and a fitted line](../../Assets/Module%2010/fig-10.3.7-stage-discharge-rating-curve.jpeg)

*Figures 10.3.7 and 10.3.8. Hypothetical river stage-discharge relationship, repeated in the course knowledge check. Source: Hendriks, 2010, p. 240.*

**What to notice:** Diamonds are paired field measurements; the fitted rating curve converts a new stage reading into estimated discharge. Read from stage to the curve and then to discharge, and use the curve only at the channel section where it was calibrated.

## Equations and Units

For subsection $i$:

$$
A_i=w_i d_i
$$

$$
\bar v_i=\frac{v_{0.2D,i}+v_{0.8D,i}}{2}
$$

$$
Q_i=A_i\bar v_i
$$

For the whole channel:

$$
Q_{\text{total}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_i
$$

For true stage above a datum:

$$
H_{\text{stage}}=H-H_0
$$

| Quantity | Course unit |
|---|---|
| Width and depth | $\mathrm{m}$ |
| Cross-sectional area | $\mathrm{m^2}$ |
| Velocity | $\mathrm{m/s}$ |
| Discharge | $\mathrm{m^3/s}$ |
| Stage or elevation | $\mathrm{m}$ |

> [!example] Calculation order
> Compute width $\times$ depth, average the two velocity readings, multiply area $\times$ average velocity, and only then sum all subsection discharges. Carry units through every step.

## Common Confusions

- **Maximum vs. average velocity:** the fastest point in the channel is not the representative velocity needed for discharge. Use the subsection's 20%-80% average or the less accurate 60%-depth reading.
- **20%-80% method vs. 60% method:** the first uses two readings and is preferred; the second uses one reading when field conditions require a shortcut.
- **Area vs. discharge:** area is $\mathrm{m^2}$; multiplying by velocity produces $\mathrm{m^3/s}$.
- **Subsection vs. total discharge:** calculate $Q_i$ for every slice, then sum. Do not average subsection discharges.
- **Stage vs. surface elevation:** true stage is surface elevation minus datum elevation, $H-H_0$.
- **Stage–discharge transferability:** a relation calibrated at one gauge cannot be used for another point or another river.
- **Field points vs. regression line:** diamonds are actual paired measurements; the line estimates values between them.
