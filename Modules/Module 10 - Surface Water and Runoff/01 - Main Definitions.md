# Main Definitions

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]  
Module: [[Modules/Module 10 - Surface Water and Runoff/00 - Module 10 Overview|Module 10 Overview]]

| Term | Definition | Course section | Example / where / application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface water | Liquid freshwater in creeks, streams, rivers, ponds, lakes, swamps, and marshes. | 10.1, opening discussion | The Mississippi drainage network routes rain, snowmelt, and spring water toward the Gulf of Mexico. |
| Tributary | A smaller stream or river that enters a larger body of water. | 10.1, opening discussion | Tributaries combine into the Mississippi's main channel and help delineate its watershed. |
| Riparian zone | The shoreline or immediately adjacent zone around a river; often rich in species diversity. | 10.1, opening discussion | Vegetation beside a river provides habitat for aquatic, amphibian, and terrestrial species. |
| Flood plain | Land nearest or adjacent to a river shoreline that is periodically inundated during high-water or large-storm events. | 10.1, "Stream Migration and Flood Plains" | The Ucayali River migrated laterally across its flood plain during the course's 28-year image sequence. |
| Meander | A curve or bend in a channel; also the lateral migration produced by outside-bank erosion and inside-bank deposition. | 10.1, "Stream Migration and Flood Plains" | A Ucayali River bend shifts as fast outside-bank flow erodes and slow inside-bank flow deposits sediment. |
| Oxbow lake | A small curved lake formed when a meandering river cuts off part of its former channel. | 10.1, "Stream Migration and Flood Plains" | A Ucayali cutoff can isolate a crescent-shaped reach beside the active river. |
| Runoff | Water carried in or to a channel or water body at a location over time; expressed as volume per time or depth per time. | 10.1, "Runoff and Urbanization" | A hydrologist reports river runoff in $\mathrm{m^3/s}$ or uses depth per time to compare it with precipitation and evapotranspiration. |
| Direct precipitation | Rainfall that reaches the surface, travels downslope, and may combine into channelized overland flow. | 10.1, "Runoff and Urbanization" | Rain moving downslope gathers into rills and gullies before entering a stream. |
| Interflow | Surface or flood water moving through shallow subsurface material parallel to the surface when soil is saturated from depth to surface. | 10.1, "Runoff and Urbanization" | After saturation, shallow water moves laterally through a hillslope toward a channel. |
| Groundwater discharge | Water added to the surface by groundwater springs or by shallow interflow re-emerging at the surface. | 10.1, "Runoff and Urbanization" | A spring emerges where a slope intersects groundwater and feeds a surface channel. |
| Snowmelt | Runoff and channelization caused by direct melting of snow and ice, especially at high elevation or in high headwaters. | 10.1, "Runoff and Urbanization" | Rapid spring snowmelt contributes to recurrent Red River flooding at Winnipeg. |
| Impervious surface | Asphalt, concrete, roofs, and other built surfaces that limit infiltration and rapidly collect and direct runoff. | 10.1, "Runoff and Urbanization" | Weber Street pavement drains polluted stormwater through a short pipe into Laurel Creek. |
| Lag time | The interval between the start of a precipitation event and the arrival of excess runoff or floodwater in nearby streams and rivers. | 10.1, "Runoff and Urbanization" | The course's urban hydrograph peaks sooner than the rural hydrograph because stormwater is routed rapidly. |
| Channelization | Confining or directing flow through barriers or constructed channels, often accelerating water and pollutant delivery. | 10.1, "Runoff and Urbanization" | A concrete stormwater channel sends runoff and contaminants quickly toward a river. |
| Headwaters | The high, upstream beginning of a stream or river profile, commonly associated with steeper gradient. | 10.2, "Stream Components" | On the course's concave-up long profile, the steepest reach is near the upstream headwaters. |
| Confluence | A location where tributary flows join. | 10.2, "Stream Flow" | Discharge increases where two tributary channels merge into one downstream flow. |
| Channel | The main watercourse path of a stream or river. | 10.2, "Stream Components" | During ordinary flow, the channel stores and conveys water before it reaches the tops of the banks. |
| Bank | The side or shore of a stream or river. | 10.2, "Stream Components" | Laurel Creek undercut its right bank beside the Weber Street bridge, cracking the parking lot. |
| Bed | The bottom or floor of a stream or river. | 10.2, "Stream Components" | Coarse material on the bed slows near-boundary flow and provides a surface for traction. |
| Laminar flow | Streamlined, parallel movement with little or no mixing between adjacent layers; slow and weakly erosive, and rare in nature. | 10.2, "Stream Flow" | Controlled laboratory flow can remain in parallel layers with little erosion. |
| Turbulent flow | Flow in which water mixes between adjacent layers and swirls around bed and bank obstacles, enabling greater erosion and transport. | 10.2, "Stream Flow" | Water forms eddies around boulders and uneven banks in nearly all natural streams. |
| Gradient | Channel slope; steeper gradient generally permits higher velocity. | 10.2, "Stream Flow" | Steep headwater reaches flow faster than the flatter, more meandering downstream profile. |
| Channel roughness | Unevenness of the bed and banks caused by sediments, boulders, stumps, or other obstacles; it increases friction and turbulence. | 10.2, "Stream Flow" | Boulders along a bank create eddies and lower velocity near the channel boundary. |
| Discharge ($Q$) | Total water volume passing a specified channel point per unit time, commonly in $\mathrm{m^3/s}$. | 10.2, "Discharge"; 10.3, "Discharge Calculations" | Summing the seven course subsections gives $Q=20.25\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$. |
| Cross-sectional area ($A$) | Area of flowing water across a channel, calculated for a simple section as width multiplied by average depth. | 10.2, "Discharge" | A $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$-wide, $1.5\ \mathrm{m}$-deep slice has $A=1.5\ \mathrm{m^2}$. |
| Load | Sediment or other Earth material transported by a stream or river. | 10.2, "Sediment Loads and Transport" | At about $100\ \mathrm{ft^3/s}$, the Powder River carried roughly 1,000 tons of sediment per day. |
| Bed load | Sediment that remains at or near the channel bed and moves by sliding, rolling, or saltation. | 10.2, "Sediment Loads and Transport" | Sand and larger grains move gradually downstream while staying close to the bed. |
| Traction | Rolling or sliding movement of sediment along the channel bed. | 10.2, "Sediment Loads and Transport" | A gravel grain rolls downstream without entering the main flow. |
| Saltation | Repeated short hops in which particles lift from the bed, travel briefly through the flow, and return to the bed. | 10.2, "Sediment Loads and Transport" | A sand grain briefly lifts, lands, and dislodges another grain from the bed. |
| Suspended or dissolved load | The finest particles, such as silt and clay, carried within the flow rather than along the bed. | 10.2, "Sediment Loads and Transport" | Fine silt and clay can remain in the water and travel far downstream before settling. |
| Capacity | The amount of material carried within a stream or river flow. | 10.2, "Sediment Loads and Transport" | The Powder River's approximately 1,000 tons of sediment per day is a measure of transported amount. |
| Competence | The maximum particle size a stream or river can transport. | 10.2, "Sediment Loads and Transport" | During high-velocity flow, a river may become able to move cobbles or boulders. |
| Velocity-area technique | A discharge method that divides a channel cross-section into slices, calculates area and representative velocity for each, and sums their discharges. | 10.3, "Discharge Calculations" | The course divides a $7\ \mathrm{m}$ stream into seven slices and sums their discharges to obtain $20.25\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$. |
| Stage | The height, elevation, or depth of a stream or river water surface. | 10.3, "Stage–Discharge Relationships" | A measuring stick fixed below a bridge records the water level of a glacial river in Peru. |
| Stilling well | A sheltered, often digital installation that records river stage at chosen time intervals and can function below winter ice. | 10.3, "Stage–Discharge Relationships" | Below-ice inlets let a stilling well continue recording stage through winter. |
| Stage–discharge relationship | A site-specific relation that estimates discharge from measured stage using field observations and a fitted regression line. | 10.3, "Stage–Discharge Relationships" | In Figure 10.3.8, a stage of $30\ \mathrm{m}$ corresponds to about $70\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$ at that gauge. |
| Datum ($H_0$) | The reference elevation from which true stage is calculated; the course example uses the riverbed or stilling-well bottom. | 10.3, "Stage–Discharge Relationships" | Subtracting a $140\ \mathrm{m}$ bed datum from a $155\ \mathrm{m}$ water-surface elevation gives a $15\ \mathrm{m}$ stage. |
| Flood | Water overtopping channel banks and spreading into the adjacent flood plain when inputs exceed outflow capacity or the channel is blocked. | 10.4, opening discussion | Tributaries, overland flow, groundwater, and rain can raise a river above its banks during a major storm. |
| Return period | A statistical expression of annual flood probability; a 1-in-$T$ event has a $1/T$ chance in each year. | 10.4, opening discussion | A 1-in-50-year flood has a 2% chance in each year, and two can occur within 50 years. |
| Regional flood | Flood caused by broad meteorological inputs such as heavy storms and runoff, rapid snowmelt, or rain-on-snow. | 10.4, opening discussion | Excessive snowmelt and rain-on-snow produce widespread Red River flooding around Winnipeg. |
| Local flash flood | Flood from localized, intense precipitation affecting a small area or limited watershed. | 10.4, opening discussion | An intense thunderstorm can rapidly flood one small watershed while nearby areas remain unaffected. |
| Ice-jam flood | Flood produced when ice fragments obstruct a channel, commonly near restricted bridges and culverts. | 10.4, opening discussion | Broken ice lodges at a narrow bridge opening and impounds water upstream. |
| Constructed-dam failure flood | Potentially catastrophic downstream flooding caused by failure of an artificial dam. | 10.4, opening discussion | A breached artificial reservoir releases a sudden flood wave into downstream communities. |
| Natural-dam flood | Upstream impoundment or downstream release caused by a natural blockage such as a landslide or glacial event. | 10.4, opening discussion | A landslide blocks a valley, floods upstream, then sends water downstream if the blockage breaches. |
| Levee | An artificial structure used to limit floodwater spreading. | 10.4, opening discussion | A raised barrier keeps high river flow from spreading into developed flood-plain land. |
| Floodway | A constructed channel that diverts excess floodwater around a vulnerable area. | 10.4, "Hydrology Highlight" | Winnipeg's nearly $50\ \mathrm{km}$ floodway routes Red River water around the city's east side. |
| Hydrograph | A graph of stream or river flow volume over time. | 10.4, "Flood Hydrographs" | A hydrologist compares rural and urban hydrographs to evaluate flood timing and magnitude. |
| Baseflow | Normal river flow shown beneath the flood response on a hydrograph. | 10.4, "Flood Hydrographs" | On Figure 10.4.2, the background flow line continues beneath the event response. |
| Quickflow | Floodwater volume represented by the event response above baseflow on a hydrograph. | 10.4, "Flood Hydrographs" | A narrow quickflow response shows a flood wave that passed quickly. |
| Peak flow | The maximum discharge reached on a hydrograph; a higher peak represents greater flood magnitude. | 10.4, "Flood Hydrographs" | The urbanized hydrograph reaches a higher, earlier maximum than the rural hydrograph. |

## Commonly Confused Terms

- **Runoff vs. discharge:** runoff describes water delivered to or carried in a water body over time; discharge is the water volume passing a specific channel observation point per time.
- **Flood plain vs. riparian zone:** a flood plain is periodically inundated land adjacent to a channel; a riparian zone is the shoreline and immediately adjacent ecological zone.
- **Interflow vs. groundwater discharge:** interflow moves laterally through shallow saturated material; groundwater discharge is water emerging at the surface from an aquifer spring or re-emerging interflow.
- **Laminar vs. turbulent flow:** laminar layers remain parallel with little mixing; turbulent flow mixes and eddies and is the usual natural stream condition.
- **Bed load vs. suspended load:** bed load stays at or near the bed; suspended load remains within the moving water.
- **Traction vs. saltation:** traction is rolling or sliding on the bed; saltation is short hopping motion.
- **Capacity vs. competence:** capacity is how much material can be carried; competence is the largest particle size that can be carried.
- **Stage vs. discharge:** stage is water-surface height or depth; discharge is water volume per time. Their relationship must be calibrated for a specific channel section.
- **Return period vs. recurrence schedule:** a return period expresses annual probability and does not guarantee a fixed interval between floods.
- **Baseflow vs. quickflow:** baseflow is normal river flow; quickflow is the event-driven floodwater response.
