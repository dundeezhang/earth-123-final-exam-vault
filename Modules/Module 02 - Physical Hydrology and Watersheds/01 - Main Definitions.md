# Main Definitions

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

Module: [[00 - Module 02 Overview]]

| Term | Definition | Course section | Example / where / application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical hydrology | Study of the occurrence, movement, and physical properties of water on and within Earth and its atmosphere, in all forms. | 2.1 | Measuring rainfall, streamflow, groundwater, and snowpack within the Grand River watershed is physical hydrology. |
| Hydrological cycle / water cycle | Conceptual model of interconnected water flows and storages among the atmosphere, surface, subsurface, oceans, and cryosphere. | 2.1-2.3 | Ocean water evaporates, condenses, falls as precipitation, and returns by runoff or groundwater flow. |
| Atmospheric water | Water in the atmosphere as vapour, clouds, or precipitation. | 2.1 | A cloud over southern Ontario and rain falling from it are atmospheric water. |
| Surface water | Water visible at Earth's surface in oceans, lakes, rivers, streams, and wetlands. | 2.1 | Lake Erie and the Grand River are surface-water resources. |
| Groundwater | Water stored and moving below the land surface, including water in aquifers and near the water table. | 2.1 | Waterloo Region pumps much of its municipal supply from more than 120 groundwater wells. |
| Cryosphere | Water stored in solid form as glaciers, ice caps, or snow. | 2.1 | A winter snowpack stores water until melting releases it. |
| Evaporation | Conversion and transfer of liquid water to atmospheric water vapour. | 2.1, 2.3 | Solar heating causes evaporation from oceans and the Great Lakes. |
| Transpiration | Movement of water vapour from vegetation to the atmosphere. | 2.3 | Plants in a watershed release water through leaves. |
| Evapotranspiration, $ET$ | Combined atmospheric water loss through evaporation and transpiration. | 2.3 | A watershed balance may combine both losses into one $ET$ output. |
| Precipitation, $P$ | Atmospheric water transferred to Earth's surface as rain, snow, hail, sleet, fog, frost, or dew under the course's broad description. | 2.1, 2.3 | Rain entering a watershed is a precipitation input. |
| Runoff / overland flow | Water moving across the land surface toward channels and other water bodies. | 2.1 | Rain flowing from an agricultural field into a stream can carry eroded soil. |
| Surface-water flow, $Q$ | Surface runoff or channel discharge crossing a system boundary; subscripts distinguish inflow and outflow. | 2.2-2.3 | $Q_{out}$ is the river leaving the conceptual watershed at its lowest outlet. |
| Groundwater flow, $G$ | Water moving through the subsurface; subscripts distinguish flow entering and leaving a system. | 2.2-2.3 | $G_{in}$ enters the conceptual watershed through its subsurface boundary. |
| Infiltration | Downward entry of surface water into soil or rock. | 2.1 | Rain infiltrating a field may eventually recharge groundwater. |
| Percolation | Continued downward movement of water through soil or rock after entry. | 2.1 | Infiltrated water percolates toward an aquifer. |
| Hydrologic system | Region with defined physical boundaries that receives inputs, provides outputs, and stores a conservative quantity such as water. | 2.2 | A lake, watershed, or rain barrel can each be treated as a hydrologic system. |
| System boundary | Chosen physical limit used to decide which water movements are inputs, outputs, or internal storage. | 2.2-2.3 | A watershed divide forms the lateral surface boundary of a drainage system. |
| Input, $I$ | Water or another conserved quantity entering a system during the study period. | 2.2-2.3 | Rainfall and groundwater inflow are inputs to the conceptual watershed. |
| Output, $O$ | Water or another conserved quantity leaving a system during the study period. | 2.2-2.3 | Evapotranspiration and river discharge are watershed outputs. |
| Storage, $S$ | Water already held inside a system at a given time. | 2.2-2.3 | Water inside a lake or rain barrel is storage. |
| Change in storage, $\Delta S$ | Difference in stored water over a defined period, equal to inputs minus outputs. | 2.2-2.3 | If a rain barrel receives $50\ \mathrm{L}$ and releases $20\ \mathrm{L}$, $\Delta S=+30\ \mathrm{L}$. |
| Conservation equation | Balance stating that change in a conserved quantity equals total input minus total output: $\Delta S=I-O$. | 2.2 | A reservoir operator uses measured inflows and releases to estimate storage change. |
| Water balance | Application of conservation to all measured water inputs, outputs, and storage changes in a hydrologic system. | 2.3 | $\Delta S=(P+G_{in}+Q_{in})-(ET+G_{out}+Q_{out})$. |
| Steady state | Condition in which storage does not change during the study period, so $\Delta S=0$ and inputs equal outputs. | 2.3 | A full rain barrel is steady when eaves inflow equals overflow, even while water moves through it. |
| Flux | Movement or change of a quantity through time or across a boundary. | 2.2 | $\Delta S$ records a storage change, while discharge records water crossing an outlet over time. |
| Length per time, $L/T$ | Rate expressed as a water depth or distance per unit time. | 2.2 | Rainfall intensity can be recorded in $\mathrm{mm/h}$. |
| Volume per time, $V/T$ | Flow rate expressed as a volume crossing a point or boundary per unit time. | 2.2 | River discharge can be recorded in $\mathrm{m^3/s}$. |
| Hydrograph | Graph of discharge or flow volume through time, sometimes paired with precipitation. | 2.2 | A storm hydrograph shows rainfall on Day 1 and peak river discharge on Day 2. |
| Discharge | Volume of water passing a specified point per unit time. | 2.2 | The course reports an average Grand River flow of $55\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$. |
| Baseflow | Ordinary sustained river flow shown beneath the storm-response portion of a hydrograph. | 2.2 | Groundwater contribution can maintain streamflow between storms. |
| Watershed / drainage basin / catchment | Total geographic area drained by a river and its tributaries toward a defined outlet. | 2.2 | The Mississippi watershed drains toward the Gulf of Mexico. |
| Tributary | Smaller watercourse that flows into a larger stream or river. | 2.2 | The Missouri and Ohio rivers drain subwatersheds within the Mississippi system. |
| Topographic divide | Elevated boundary separating drainage toward different outlets. | 2.2 | A valley ridge sends rainfall down opposite sides into different streams. |
| Subwatershed | Smaller drainage area nested inside a larger watershed. | 2.2 | The Missouri watershed lies within the larger Mississippi watershed. |
| Continental divide | Large topographic divide separating drainage toward different sides of a continent. | 2.2 | West of North America's major divide, water drains toward the Pacific rather than the Gulf of Mexico. |
| Interfluve | Higher land between neighbouring stream channels or drainage paths. | 2.2 | Dotted divides in the real-world watershed figure follow interfluves between streams. |
| Outlet / mouth | Defined low point where surface water leaves a watershed. | 2.2-2.3 | The Mississippi watershed outlet is at the Gulf of Mexico. |
| Contaminant pathway | Route by which moving water carries pollution from a source to another location. | 2.1 | Leakage from a gas station tank can enter groundwater and migrate away from the property. |
| Erosion | Removal and transport of soil or rock by water or another agent. | 2.1 | Runoff from uncovered fields carries topsoil into the Mississippi River. |
| Deposition | Settling or accumulation of previously transported sediment or contaminants. | 2.1 | The Mississippi plume deposits material near the Gulf of Mexico. |
| Lake-effect snow | Snow produced when cold, dry air gains moisture over warmer open lake water and releases it over colder land. | 2.4 | Prevailing west-to-east winds favour heavy snow east and southeast of the Great Lakes. |
| Frost-free period | Part of the year when surface frost is absent; large lakes can lengthen it near shore by storing and releasing heat. | 2.4 | Great Lakes shorelines have more frost-free days than areas farther inland. |
| Monitoring well | Well used to observe groundwater level or quality without interrupting nearby production. | 2.4 | Waterloo Region places monitoring wells near municipal production wells. |
| Production well | Well used to extract groundwater for supply. | 2.4 | Waterloo Region's municipal wells provide much of its freshwater demand. |
| Global scale | Planet-wide hydrologic scope involving processes such as atmospheric circulation, oceans, and global precipitation. | 2.4 | A world map of annual precipitation is a global dataset. |
| Regional scale | Hydrologic scope covering a large connected area within part of a continent. | 2.4 | The Great Lakes system is a regional freshwater system. |
| Local scale | Hydrologic scope focused on a community, site, habitat, or small engineered system. | 2.4 | A stormwater pond or rain barrel is studied at local scale. |

## Commonly Confused Terms

- **Storage vs. change in storage:** $S$ is the amount currently present; $\Delta S$ is how that amount changes during the study period.
- **Steady state vs. no flow:** steady state requires $I=O$ and $\Delta S=0$; water may still enter and leave continuously.
- **Watershed vs. stream:** a stream is a channel; a watershed is the entire land area draining to a common outlet.
- **Topographic divide vs. outlet:** the divide is the high boundary; the outlet is the low exit.
- **Runoff vs. discharge:** runoff describes water moving over land or into channels; discharge is the measured volume per time passing a defined point.
- **Infiltration vs. percolation:** infiltration is entry into the ground; percolation is subsequent movement through subsurface material.
- **Groundwater vs. surface water:** groundwater is below the surface; surface water occupies visible water bodies and channels.
- **Length rate vs. volume rate:** $\mathrm{mm/day}$ is a depth rate; $\mathrm{m^3/day}$ is a volumetric rate. Area is required to convert between them.
- **Global vs. regional vs. local:** scale changes the system boundary and relevant processes, not the conservation principle.
