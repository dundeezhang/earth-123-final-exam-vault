# 2.4 A Look at Global, Regional, and Local Hydrology

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

Module: [[00 - Module 02 Overview]] | Definitions: [[01 - Main Definitions]]

## Exam Focus

- Match hydrologic questions to global, regional, watershed, or local scales.
- Interpret global precipitation and groundwater distributions.
- Recall key Great Lakes dimensions, storage, and climate effects.
- Explain why lake-effect snow favours east and southeast shores.
- Recall the Grand River watershed's direction, scale, uses, and groundwater dependence.

## Core Notes

### Hydrologic scale

The conservation principle works at every scale, but the boundary and dominant processes change.

| Scale | Typical focus | Course example |
|---|---|---|
| Global | Atmosphere, oceans, climate, global precipitation | Worldwide annual-precipitation distribution |
| Regional | Large connected water systems and climate effects | Great Lakes system |
| Watershed | Tributaries, divides, discharge, land use | Grand River watershed |
| Local | Site drainage, wells, stormwater, habitat | Waterloo production wells or a rain barrel |

### Global precipitation and groundwater

![Global distribution of annual precipitation](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.4-global-precipitation.jpg)
*Figure 2.4.1: Global annual precipitation distribution. Thakur (n.d.).*

**What to notice:** Precipitation is uneven. Wet tropical and coastal regions contrast with dry subtropical and continental areas, so global totals cannot represent local supply.

![Global distribution of groundwater resources](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.4-global-groundwater.jpg)
*Figure 2.4.2: Global groundwater-resource distribution. Barnett (2013).*

**What to notice:** Groundwater occurs beneath most regions, including deserts, but depth, salinity, geology, contamination, drilling cost, and treatment needs affect usability.

### Great Lakes regional hydrology

The Great Lakes contain the world's largest volume of surface freshwater, over $20{,}000\ \mathrm{km^3}$. Less than $1\%$ of the lakes' total volume drains through the St. Lawrence River each year, so most water remains in storage.

| Lake | Surface area ($\mathrm{km^2}$) | Water volume ($\mathrm{km^3}$) | Elevation (m asl) | Average depth (m) | Maximum depth (m) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Erie | 25,700 | 480 | 174 | 19 | 64 |
| Huron | 60,000 | 3,500 | 176 | 59 | 228 |
| Michigan | 58,000 | 4,900 | 176 | 85 | 282 |
| Ontario | 19,000 | 1,640 | 75 | 86 | 245 |
| Superior | 82,000 | 12,000 | 183 | 147 | 406 |

![Great Lakes elevations and average depths](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.4-great-lakes-topography.png)
*Figure 2.4.3: Great Lakes topography, elevation, and average depth. United States Environmental Protection Agency and Government of Canada (1995).*

**What to notice:** Lake Superior is highest, largest by area and volume, and deepest in the table. Lake Ontario is lowest in elevation, reflecting downstream flow toward the St. Lawrence River.

The Great Lakes support water supply, habitats, fisheries, shipping, recreation, industry, and trade. Their large watersheds also receive contaminants from many urban and rural tributaries.

### Great Lakes climate effects

Large lakes store heat and release it slowly. This delays cooling near shore and lengthens the frost-free period.

![Great Lakes influence on frost-free periods](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.4-great-lakes-frost-free.png)
*Figure 2.4.4: Great Lakes influence on regional frost-free periods. United States Environmental Protection Agency and Government of Canada (1995).*

**What to notice:** Areas near shore have more frost-free days than inland areas. The lakes act as thermal storage that moderates local temperatures.

In winter, cold dry air moving over warmer open water gains moisture through evaporation. When this moist air reaches colder land, snow falls. Prevailing winds generally move west to east, so the strongest snowbelts occur east and southeast of the lakes.

![Lake-effect snowbelts around the Great Lakes](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.4-great-lakes-lake-effect-snow.png)
*Figure 2.4.5: Lake-effect snow distribution. United States Environmental Protection Agency and Government of Canada (1995).*

**What to notice:** Snowfall changes over short distances because shoreline orientation, wind direction, open-water temperature, and downwind land control the moisture plume.

### Grand River watershed

The University of Waterloo lies within the Grand River watershed. The river flows south into **Lake Erie**, not Lake Ontario.

![Grand River watershed and major population centres](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.4-grand-river-watershed.png)
*Figure 2.4.6: Grand River watershed and major population centres. Holysh et al. (2000).*

**What to notice:** The watershed links Fergus, Guelph, Waterloo, Kitchener, Cambridge, Paris, and Brantford along a drainage network ending at Lake Erie.

Key course values:

- Drainage area: approximately $7{,}000\ \mathrm{km^2}$
- River length: approximately $300\ \mathrm{km}$
- Average flow: $55\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$
- Average maximum flow: $1{,}800\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$
- Average minimum flow: $6\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$

Flow-control structures reduce flood risk and store water for release during dry periods.

![Land uses within the Grand River watershed](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.4-grand-river-land-use.png)
*Figure 2.4.7: Land uses in the Grand River watershed. Grand River Conservation Authority (2008).*

**What to notice:** Agricultural, urban, natural, and water areas coexist within one drainage system. Land use changes runoff, water quality, habitat, and contaminant loading.

The watershed supports recreation, drinking-water supply, wastewater transport, habitats, and settlements. Heavy storms can also produce flooding that affects ecosystems and infrastructure.

### Waterloo Region groundwater supply

The course page states that Waterloo Region extracts about $5$ to $10$ million gallons per day from the Grand River and about $40$ million gallons per day from more than 120 groundwater wells.

![Groundwater wells and treatment plants in Waterloo Region](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.4-waterloo-wells.png)
*Figure 2.4.8: Groundwater wells and water-treatment plants in Waterloo Region. Middleton (2010).*

**What to notice:** Wells cluster near major population centres. Monitoring wells near production wells track groundwater quality and extraction effects without interrupting municipal supply.

## Common Confusions

- **The Grand River drains to Lake Erie.** It does not drain to Lake Ontario.
- **The Great Lakes store most of their water.** Annual St. Lawrence outflow is less than $1\%$ of total lake volume.
- **Lake-effect snow is spatially uneven.** Prevailing winds favour east and southeast shores.
- **Groundwater occurrence does not guarantee usable supply.** Depth, quality, geology, and treatment cost matter.
- **Large scale does not remove local variation.** Global maps reveal broad patterns but cannot replace site measurements.
