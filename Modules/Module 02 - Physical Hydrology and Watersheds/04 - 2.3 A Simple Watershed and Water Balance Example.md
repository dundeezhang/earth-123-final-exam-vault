# 2.3 A Simple Watershed and Water Balance Example

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home|Home]]

Module: [[Modules/Module 02 - Physical Hydrology and Watersheds/00 - Module 02 Overview|Overview]] | Definitions: [[Modules/Module 02 - Physical Hydrology and Watersheds/01 - Main Definitions|Definitions]]

## Exam Focus

- Identify each water-balance variable and its direction.
- Write a balance that matches a specific system boundary.
- Determine whether storage increases, decreases, or remains constant.
- Explain steady state using both words and equations.
- Use the rain-barrel analogy to simplify watershed reasoning.

## Core Notes

### Water balance as a conservation problem

A water balance works like a bank account. Deposits are inputs, withdrawals are outputs, and the account balance is storage. The water remaining after a study period changes according to:

$$
\Delta S=\text{inputs}-\text{outputs}
$$

![Hydrological cycle used to identify water-balance terms](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.3-hydrological-cycle-hotspots.png)
*Figure 2.3.1: Static background from the course hydrological-cycle hotspot activity. VectorMine/iStock/Getty Images; Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** The activity identifies precipitation, atmospheric losses, surface runoff, groundwater, and storage. These processes remain the same when the system is reduced from the global cycle to a watershed.

### Water-balance terms

| Process | Symbol | Direction relative to system |
|---|---|---|
| Precipitation | $P$ | Input |
| Evaporation | $E$ or $Ev$ | Output |
| Transpiration | $T$ or part of $ET$ | Output |
| Evapotranspiration | $ET$ or $EVT$ | Output |
| Surface-water flow | $Q_{in}$, $Q_{out}$ | Input or output |
| Groundwater flow | $G_{in}$, $G_{out}$ | Input or output |
| Surface-water storage change | $\Delta S_{SW}$ | Internal change |
| Groundwater storage change | $\Delta S_{GW}$ | Internal change |
| Total storage change | $\Delta S$ | Internal change |

> [!important] Use unambiguous notation
> The source page uses $ET$ once for transpiration and also for combined evapotranspiration. These notes use $T$ for transpiration and $ET$ for combined evapotranspiration. The water-balance equations below use the combined $ET$ term.

The general balance is:

$$
\Delta S=(P+G_{in}+Q_{in})-(ET+G_{out}+Q_{out})
$$

Terms may be combined when separate measurements are impractical. Evaporation and transpiration are commonly combined as $ET$, while surface-water and groundwater storage changes may be combined as $\Delta S$.

### Applying the balance to a watershed

![Conceptual watershed with water-balance flows](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.2-conceptual-watershed.jpg)
*Figure 2.3.2: Conceptual watershed. Dingman (2002, p. 12).*

**What to notice:** This watershed receives $P$ and $G_{in}$. It loses water through $ET$, $G_{out}$, and surface discharge $Q$.

For the pictured system:

$$
\Delta S=(P+G_{in})-(ET+G_{out}+Q)
$$

$Q$ is the river leaving at the lowest surface elevation. A surface-water input term is absent because the chosen boundary contains the complete headwater watershed and shows no upstream channel crossing into it.

### Rain-barrel analogy

A rain barrel is a small hydrologic system. Its physical boundary is easy to see, so inputs, outputs, and storage can be identified directly.

![Residential rain barrel installed beneath a roof outlet](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.3-residential-rainbarrel.jpg)
*Figure 2.3.3a: Residential rain barrel. City of Vancouver (n.d.).*

**What to notice:** The roof and eaves collect water from an area larger than the barrel opening, so eaves inflow can dominate direct rainfall input.

![Rain-barrel inputs from eaves and direct rainfall](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.3-rainbarrel-inputs.png)
*Figure 2.3.3b: Rain-barrel inputs $I_E$ and $I_R$. Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** $I_E$ is inflow from the eaves and $I_R$ is rain entering through the barrel opening. Water already in the barrel is storage.

![Rain-barrel outputs through overflow and spigot](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.3-rainbarrel-outputs.png)
*Figure 2.3.4: Rain-barrel outputs $O_O$ and $O_S$. Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo.*

**What to notice:** $O_O$ leaves through the overflow and $O_S$ leaves through the lower spigot. The balance is $\Delta S=(I_E+I_R)-(O_O+O_S)$.

### Steady state

Steady state means no storage change during the selected period:

$$
\Delta S=0
$$

Equivalent forms include:

$$
I_E+I_R=O_S+O_O
$$

$$
I_E+I_R-O_S-O_O=0
$$

Three rain-barrel conditions clarify the concept:

1. **No flow:** all inputs and outputs are zero, so storage is unchanged.
2. **Balanced flow:** water enters and leaves at equal rates, so storage is unchanged.
3. **Filling or draining:** inputs and outputs differ, so storage changes.

During a storm with the spigot closed, eaves inflow raises storage until the barrel fills. Once overflow equals eaves inflow, the full barrel reaches steady state even though water continues moving through it.

## Worked Examples

### Storage increase

A rain barrel receives $80\ \mathrm{L}$ from the eaves and $5\ \mathrm{L}$ directly. It releases $20\ \mathrm{L}$ through the spigot and does not overflow.

$$
\Delta S=(80+5)-(20+0)=+65\ \mathrm{L}
$$

Storage increases by $65\ \mathrm{L}$.

### Storage decrease

A watershed receives $P=15\ \mathrm{mm}$ and loses $ET=8\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $Q=12\ \mathrm{mm}$, with groundwater terms neglected.

$$
\Delta S=15-(8+12)=-5\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

Storage decreases by $5\ \mathrm{mm}$ over the period.

## Common Confusions

- **Steady state does not mean every term is zero.** It means total input equals total output.
- **A positive $\Delta S$ is a gain.** A negative $\Delta S$ is a loss.
- **Do not add an absent boundary crossing.** Include $Q_{in}$ only when surface water enters the selected system.
- **Inputs and outputs require matching units and periods.** Convert before adding or subtracting.
- **A full reservoir is not automatically steady.** It is steady only when its stored amount stops changing during the study period.
