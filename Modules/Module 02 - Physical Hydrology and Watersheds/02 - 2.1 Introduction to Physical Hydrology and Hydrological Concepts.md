# 2.1 Introduction to Physical Hydrology and Hydrological Concepts

## Exam Focus

- Define physical hydrology and the hydrological cycle.
- Classify water as atmospheric, surface, groundwater, or cryospheric.
- Recall the approximate global distribution of saltwater and freshwater.
- Explain how moving water creates contaminant pathways and transports eroded soil.
- Connect Canadian residential and industrial water use to water-resource management.

## Core Notes

### Why hydrology matters

Water supports life, habitats, drinking-water systems, agriculture, industry, hydroelectric power, and recreation. Hydrologists monitor its location, movement, quantity, and physical properties so that water supplies and ecosystems can be protected.

Canada has abundant surface water and groundwater, yet high availability can encourage high consumption. The saved course page reports that an average Canadian may use more than $300\ \mathrm{L/day}$ in residential activities.

### Physical hydrology and the water cycle

The course defines **physical hydrology** as the study of the occurrence, movement, and physical properties of water on and within Earth and its atmosphere, in all forms.

The **hydrological cycle** is a simplified model linking water flows and storages. A general sequence is:

1. Solar energy drives evaporation, especially from oceans.
2. Water vapour rises, cools, and condenses into clouds.
3. Water returns as precipitation.
4. Water moves over land as runoff or enters the ground by infiltration.
5. Surface water and groundwater return toward oceans or remain in storage.

Water can move through this cycle over periods ranging from minutes to centuries.

### Four major water-resource categories

| Category | Forms and locations | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Atmospheric water | Vapour, clouds, and precipitation | Rain falling over Waterloo |
| Surface water | Lakes, rivers, streams, wetlands, and oceans | Lake Erie and the Grand River |
| Groundwater | Water below the surface, including aquifers and the water table | Municipal wells in Waterloo Region |
| Cryosphere | Snow, glaciers, and ice caps | Seasonal Canadian snowpack |

### Global water distribution

Earth contains roughly $1.36$ billion $\mathrm{km^3}$ of water. About $97\%$ is saline ocean water. Freshwater is under $3\%$, and most freshwater is frozen or stored underground. Read the nested circles from total water to freshwater and then to surface water.

![Distribution of water on and within Earth](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.1-earth-water-distribution.png)
*Figure 2.1.4: Distribution of Earth's water. Water Science School, USGS (2019).*

**What to notice:** Groundwater contains much more liquid freshwater than lakes, rivers, and swamps combined. The readily visible surface fraction is extremely small.

### Water as a transport agent

Water can dissolve, mobilize, transport, and deposit contaminants. Potential sources include landfills, septic systems, gas stations, industrial sites, farms, and waste facilities. The source alone does not determine impact; the flow pathway controls where contaminants travel.

![Infrastructure and waste sources creating contamination pathways](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.1-contamination-pathways.png)
*Figure 2.1.5: Water and infrastructure create contamination pathways. Fetter et al. (2018, p. 26).*

**What to notice:** Contaminants can enter surface water or infiltrate into groundwater. Once mobile, they may move beyond the original property and reach wells, streams, or lakes.

Water also erodes, transports, and deposits sediment. Rainfall and runoff can remove exposed agricultural topsoil and carry it through a large watershed.

![Sediment and pollution plume at the Mississippi River outlet](../../Assets/Module%2002/2.1-mississippi-erosion.jpg)
*Figure 2.1.6: Mississippi River sediment and pollution plume entering the Gulf of Mexico on February 6, 2007. European Space Agency (2007).*

**What to notice:** The visible plume integrates material collected across the Mississippi watershed. Downstream water can contain the combined effects of many distant cities, farms, and industries.

## Common Confusions

- **Abundant water does not mean unlimited usable water.** Most global water is saline, and much freshwater is frozen or underground.
- **Groundwater is the larger liquid freshwater store.** Lakes and rivers are more visible, but they contain less water by volume.
- **A contaminant source is not the same as a pathway.** Leaking infrastructure supplies pollution; moving water transports it.
- **The hydrological cycle is conceptual.** It simplifies many paths and residence times into a readable model.
- **Water changes form and location.** Solid, liquid, and gaseous water remain part of the same connected cycle.
