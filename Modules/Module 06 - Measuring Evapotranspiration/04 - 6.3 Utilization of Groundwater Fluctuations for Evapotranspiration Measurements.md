# 6.3 Utilization of Groundwater Fluctuations for Evapotranspiration Measurements

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

## Exam Focus

- Explain why a water table can rise at night and fall during the day under vegetation.
- State the site condition required for groundwater-based ET measurement.
- Define $S_y$, $a$, and $b$ with their correct time intervals and signs.
- Calculate $a$ and $b$ from water-table observations.
- Apply $ET=S_y(24a+b)$ and convert among metres per day, millimetres per day, millimetres per hour, and centimetres.

## Core Notes

During dry weather, plant uptake can create measurable water-table fluctuations. Large trees, crops, or broad vegetated areas may lower the water table strongly during the day, when plant water use is greater. Overnight recovery reflects groundwater flow and recharge.

The method works only when the water table is **close to the surface and directly accessible to plant roots**. With a good groundwater-fluctuation record, it offers a quick way to estimate total ET water loss.

### Equation variables

- $S_y$ is the soil storage coefficient or **effective specific yield**: how much water the soil can hold and provide to plants. It is commonly taken from a table based on grain size, porosity, permeability, and other geologic properties.
- $a$ is the water-table rise rate from **12:00 a.m. to 4:00 a.m.**, representing water-table flow and recharge. Calculate it as elevation rise divided by 4 hours.
- $b$ is the **signed** total water-table elevation change between measurements 24 hours apart. In the worked example, the second level is lower, so $b$ is negative.

The factor 24 projects the hourly nocturnal recovery rate across one day. Thus, $24a$ and $b$ are both daily-interval water-depth terms before multiplication by dimensionless $S_y$.

### Figure 6.3.1 worked example

![Groundwater-level fluctuation graph with recovery intervals](../../Assets/Module%2006/figure-6.3.1-groundwater-fluctuation.png)

*Figure 6.3.1. Conceptual groundwater levels over a 42-hour period (Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo).*

**What to notice:** The dotted green interval marks the midnight-to-4:00 a.m. recovery used for $a$, while the dashed red lines compare levels 24 hours apart for $b$. The lower second-day peak records the net water-table decline caused by ET.

Given $S_y=0.13$:

1. Read $0.5$ m at 12:00 a.m. and $0.6$ m at 4:00 a.m.
2. Calculate the recovery rate:

$$
a=\frac{0.6\ \mathrm{m}-0.5\ \mathrm{m}}{4\ \mathrm{h}}=0.025\ \mathrm{m/h}
$$

3. Read $0.62$ m at the first 6:00 a.m. and $0.50$ m 24 hours later.
4. Calculate the signed daily change:

$$
b=0.50\ \mathrm{m}-0.62\ \mathrm{m}=-0.12\ \mathrm{m}
$$

5. Substitute:

$$
ET=0.13\left[(24\ \mathrm{h})(0.025\ \mathrm{m/h})-0.12\ \mathrm{m}\right]
$$

$$
ET=0.0624\ \mathrm{m/day}=62.4\ \mathrm{mm/day}=2.6\ \mathrm{mm/h}
$$

## Equations and Units

$$
ET=S_y(24a+b)
$$

General calculation sequence:

1. Read the midnight and 4:00 a.m. elevations and calculate $a=(h_{04:00}-h_{00:00})/4\ \mathrm{h}$.
2. Choose two observations exactly 24 hours apart and calculate $b=h_{later}-h_{earlier}$.
3. Evaluate $24a+b$, keeping the sign of $b$.
4. Multiply by $S_y$.
5. Convert the resulting daily depth to the requested unit or rate.

Useful conversions:

$$
1\ \mathrm{m}=1000\ \mathrm{mm}=100\ \mathrm{cm}
$$

$$
\mathrm{mm/h}=\frac{\mathrm{mm/day}}{24}
$$

## Common Confusions

- The method is not suitable when roots cannot access the water table.
- $S_y$ is not read from the water-level graph; it comes from soil/geologic information.
- $a$ is a rate over 4 hours, not simply the total rise.
- $b$ is measured over exactly 24 hours and is final elevation minus initial elevation.
- A falling water table makes $b$ negative. Do not discard the sign.
- The 24 in the equation multiplies $a$ because $a$ is hourly; it does not multiply $b$ again.
- $ET$ from the equation is a daily depth. Divide by 24 only when an hourly rate is requested.
- In the worked example, $0.0624\ \mathrm{m/day}$ is $62.4\ \mathrm{mm/day}$, not $6.24\ \mathrm{mm/day}$.
