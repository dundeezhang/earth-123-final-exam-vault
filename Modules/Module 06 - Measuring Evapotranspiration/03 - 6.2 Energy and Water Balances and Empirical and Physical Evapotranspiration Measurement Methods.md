# 6.2 Energy and Water Balances and Empirical and Physical Evapotranspiration Measurement Methods

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

## Exam Focus

- Explain why balance methods can extend beyond the localized conditions represented by pans and lysimeters.
- Rearrange the energy and lake water balances for evaporation.
- Define every variable in the energy-balance and empirical equations.
- State when the lake water-balance method is most useful.
- Distinguish Penman-Monteith, empirical models, and direct chamber measurements.
- Explain how paired chamber measurements isolate vegetation ET.

## Core Notes

Field instruments provide good data only for localized conditions. Energy and water balances can extend estimates across wider regions, while modern physical instruments can focus on a single plant or plant group.

### Energy balance method

It takes approximately **600 calories** to evaporate **1 gram of water**. The energy-balance method therefore estimates how much incoming solar energy remains available for evaporation when environmental conditions permit it.

A **pyranometer** records incoming radiation from the atmosphere and reflected radiation from the surface. Their difference represents energy absorbed by the surface, but some absorbed energy warms air, soil, or water or supports plant growth rather than evaporation.

**Figure 6.2.1:** Pyranometer arranged to measure incoming radiation at the top and reflected radiation at the bottom. The exam takeaway is that both directions must be measured to estimate absorption.

The expanded balance assigns absorbed energy to four uses: evaporation ($H_E$), warming overlying air ($H_A$), warming the surface soil or water ($H_B$), and plant growth ($H_C$). The course describes $H_C$ as often very small. Measuring $H_A$, $H_B$, and $H_C$ directly becomes difficult, especially as the study area grows.

The simplified course form retains only measurable radiation terms to estimate $H_E$. This is energy **available** for evaporation, not a guarantee that all of it produces ET; suitable conditions, such as dry overlying air, are still required.

### Water balance method

If all water inputs, outputs, and storage changes can be monitored, evaporation can be solved as a residual. The course applies the method to a freshwater lake and includes precipitation, surface-water flow, groundwater flow, and change in storage.

Groundwater movements can be especially hard to measure. The method is most useful when:

- surface inflow and outflow are low;
- the subsurface has low permeability; and
- records span many years.

It is most often applied to larger studies of ponds, lakes, and even oceans.

### Penman-Monteith method

Penman-Monteith calculates **potential ET** from a diverse and complex set of atmospheric and surface data. The course highlights its agricultural value: farmers need to replace crop water losses without under-watering, flooding, or waterlogging soils. The page identifies it as the Food and Agricultural Organization of the United Nations' main ET model.

### Empirical methods

An empirical method is fitted from experimental observations and years of local data. A long lake record containing evaporation, air temperature, wind speed, water temperature, and relative humidity could be used to predict local evaporation from weather observations.

The fitted relationship will probably fail at a different lake, forest, or field because atmospheric, water, and soil conditions differ. The coefficient $K$ tunes the equation to the study site.

### Physical method: evapotranspiration chambers

An ET chamber or dome is a large, clear, completely sealed glass or plastic enclosure placed over a plant or a small area of vegetation. It attempts to capture vapour directly rather than model ET.

- Changes in internal temperature and humidity are monitored.
- Rising humidity indicates that evaporation or transpiration is occurring.
- The method is labour intensive and limited to plants small enough to seal completely inside the chamber.
- A chamber over vegetation measures vapour from both the plant and the underlying ground.
- A second chamber over a bare or otherwise comparable surface provides the surface contribution. Subtracting it from the vegetated measurement isolates vegetation ET.

**Figure 6.2.2:** Chamber being placed over a single plant. Learn that the seal between chamber, surface, and atmosphere must be complete.

**Figure 6.2.3:** Vapour density rises sharply within minutes after the chamber is placed over a plant. The rapid increase is the observed signal of vapour accumulation.

## Equations and Units

### Expanded energy balance

$$
R-R_E-R_A=H_E+H_A+H_B+H_C
$$

- $R$: total incoming solar radiation
- $R_E$: direct reflection from the surface (albedo)
- $R_A$: longwave radiation moving from the surface to the atmosphere
- $H_E$: energy available for evaporation
- $H_A$: heat used to warm overlying air
- $H_B$: heat used to warm surface soil or water
- $H_C$: energy used for plant growth

Rearranged for evaporation energy:

$$
H_E=R-R_E-R_A-H_A-H_B-H_C
$$

Simplified course form:

$$
H_E=R-R_E-R_A
$$

Keep every term in the same energy or energy-flux units.

### Lake water balance

$$
\Delta S=\text{Inputs}-\text{Outputs}
$$

$$
\Delta S=(P+SW_{in}+GW_{in})-(E+SW_{out}+GW_{out})
$$

Solve for evaporation:

$$
E=P+SW_{in}+GW_{in}-SW_{out}-GW_{out}-\Delta S
$$

All terms must describe the same area and time period and use compatible units.

### Empirical method

$$
E=Kf(u)(e_s-e_a)
$$

- $K$: local empirical constant
- $f(u)$: wind-speed function
- $e_s$: saturated vapour pressure at the air temperature above the surface
- $e_a$: actual vapour pressure at that same air temperature

Worked pattern: calculate the vapour-pressure difference, multiply by the wind-speed function, and then apply the local constant. The resulting units follow the calibrated form of $K$ and $f(u)$.

### Chamber partitioning

$$
ET_{vegetation}=ET_{vegetated\ chamber}-ET_{comparable\ bare\ surface}
$$

## Common Confusions

- Radiation absorbed by a surface is not automatically equal to evaporation energy in the expanded balance; other heat sinks exist.
- $R_E$ is reflected shortwave radiation (albedo), while $R_A$ is longwave radiation leaving the surface.
- In the water balance, a positive $\Delta S$ is subtracted when solving for evaporation because some input water remained in storage.
- Every water-balance term must share an area, time period, and unit basis.
- Penman-Monteith and empirical equations are models; a chamber is a physical vapour-capture method.
- Empirical $K$ is site specific. A well-fitted local model is not necessarily transferable.
- A single vegetated chamber includes ground evaporation. Use the comparable bare-surface measurement to isolate plant ET.
- Rising chamber humidity is evidence of vapour accumulation, but an incomplete seal would compromise the measurement.
