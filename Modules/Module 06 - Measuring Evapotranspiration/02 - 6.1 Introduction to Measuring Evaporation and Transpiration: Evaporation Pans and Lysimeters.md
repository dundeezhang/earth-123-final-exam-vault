# 6.1 Introduction to Measuring Evaporation and Transpiration: Evaporation Pans and Lysimeters

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

## Exam Focus

- Distinguish potential ET, actual ET, and reference crop evaporation.
- Select among field measurements, water balances, energy balances, and empirical methods.
- Explain how surface, sunken, and floating evaporation pans differ.
- Correct pan loss with a pan coefficient and report a rate.
- Distinguish weighing and water balance lysimeters.
- Convert lysimeter mass loss to ET depth and then to ET rate.

## Core Notes

### Why ET is measured indirectly

Precipitation can be captured visibly in a container, but evaporation and transpiration transform liquid water into nearly invisible vapour. Scientists therefore infer ET from water or mass loss, water balances, energy balances, or fitted models.

The course introduces four method families:

- **Field measurements:** evaporation pans estimate potential evaporation; lysimeters estimate actual ET.
- **Water balances:** ET is found from measured water entering, leaving, and being stored in a plant, soil, or watershed system.
- **Energy balances:** absorbed solar energy is used to estimate the energy potentially available for evaporation and transpiration.
- **Empirical methods:** equations calibrated to specific places, soils, or plants predict ET, but their transferability is limited.

Method choice depends on site characteristics, the required data, and the study duration.

### Potential, actual, and reference crop evaporation

- **Potential evaporation or transpiration** is the maximum possible with an infinite supply of water and unsaturated air. Real sites may not reach it because water, wind, or other required conditions are limiting.
- **Actual evaporation or transpiration** is the amount occurring under current environmental conditions and is more useful for scientists, farmers, and modellers.
- **Reference crop evaporation** is a modelled potential-ET value for crop and vegetation types. It supports estimates over large monocultures or monocrops.

> [!important]
> $ET_{actual}\le ET_{potential}$ at all times in the course treatment.

### Evaporation pans

A standard evaporation pan is approximately **120 cm in diameter** and **25 cm deep**. A ruler or similar device records water-depth changes. A floating thermometer records water temperature and sits under a floating block so accidental contact with the pan bottom or sides does not affect it.

Pan loss divided by elapsed time gives a rate, commonly in $\mathrm{mm/h}$ or $\mathrm{mm/day}$. The pan must represent the region of interest: a grassy-field pan is not automatically representative of a nearby lake or asphalt surface.

- **Surface pan:** slightly elevated; wind cools the sides and bottom and carries away heat. It may record less evaporation than a sunken pan.
- **Sunken pan:** insulated from wind and cold and may retain more heat, producing a slightly higher rate. Leaves, debris, and overland runoff can contaminate the measurement.
- **Floating pan:** placed on a pond, lake, or reservoir. It may approach the surrounding water temperature on cloudy days, but can warm more than the water body on sunny days and require correction. Accessing it without splashing water into it is difficult.

*Figure 6.1.1. Standard evaporation pan in a grassy field (Rudolph, n.d.).*

**What to notice:** The upright white bar records water-depth change, while the white floating block protects the thermometer. The grassy setting also shows why a pan reading represents local conditions rather than every nearby surface.

![Surface, sunken, and floating evaporation pan setups](../../Assets/Module%2006/figure-6.1.2-pan-setups.png)

*Figure 6.1.2. Surface, sunken, and floating pan setups (Course Author(s) and University of Waterloo).*

**What to notice:** Elevation exposes the surface pan to wind cooling, burial insulates the sunken pan but increases debris and runoff risk, and the floating pan exchanges heat with the lake. Each setup therefore needs its own pan coefficient.

### Pan coefficients

The pan coefficient $K_p$ is expressed as a decimal or percentage, such as 0.65 or 65%. It corrects measured pan water loss to provide a more representative regional evaporation estimate. It depends on pan type, pan location, month, and season.

![Monthly pan coefficients compared with an average of 0.71](../../Assets/Module%2006/figure-6.1.3-monthly-pan-coefficients.png)

*Figure 6.1.3. Example monthly pan-coefficient chart for one environment (Dingman, 2002, p. 290).*

**What to notice:** The coefficients range from about 0.15 in February to 1.40 in November around an average of 0.71. A single annual value can therefore conceal substantial seasonal variation.

> [!tip]
> Apply $K_p$ to the total measured loss first, then divide by the full measurement period.

### Lysimeters

A lysimeter is an open-atmosphere hollow cylinder, bowl, or container filled completely with local soil and vegetation, including grasses or crops.

#### Weighing lysimeter

The soil-and-plant container rests on a large scale. Rainfall and plant/soil water uptake increase its mass; subsequent evaporation and transpiration reduce its mass. With no outflow, the mass decrease can be converted to water volume, equivalent water depth over the lysimeter area, and ET rate.

![Field weighing lysimeter planted with native grasses](../../Assets/Module%2006/figure-6.1.4-weighing-lysimeter.png)

*Figure 6.1.4. Field weighing lysimeter containing native grasses and local soil (ETH Zurich, n.d.).*

**What to notice:** The planted white container is the measured unit and sits on a scale. Its use of local soil and vegetation makes whole-container mass loss a field-relevant estimate of actual ET.

#### Water balance lysimeter

This type replaces the scale with a water collection and monitoring system. Percolating water enters lower soil or reservoirs, and a float and recorder monitor level changes along with inflows and outflows. A reservoir-level decline is interpreted as loss through evaporation and transpiration after the other water-balance terms are considered.

![Cross-section of a water balance lysimeter](../../Assets/Module%2006/figure-6.1.5-water-balance-lysimeter.png)

*Figure 6.1.5. Conceptual water balance lysimeter (Hendriks, 2010, p. 34).*

**What to notice:** The reservoir, float, pump, and drain monitor water levels, inflows, and outflows beneath native soil and vegetation. This design tracks water movement rather than total container mass.

## Equations and Units

### Uncorrected pan rate

$$
E_{pan}=\frac{\Delta h}{\Delta t}
$$

For 4 mm of water loss in 24 hours:

$$
E_{pan}=\frac{4\ \mathrm{mm}}{24\ \mathrm{h}}=0.167\ \mathrm{mm/h}
$$

### Pan-coefficient correction

$$
E_{adjusted}=\frac{\Delta h\,K_p}{\Delta t}
$$

For 9 mm lost in 3 days with $K_p=0.5$:

$$
\text{corrected loss}=(9\ \mathrm{mm})(0.5)=4.5\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

$$
E_{adjusted}=\frac{4.5\ \mathrm{mm}}{3\ \mathrm{days}}=1.5\ \mathrm{mm/day}
$$

> [!warning]
> The saved course page displays **0.9 mm** in one substitution line, but its stated input (9 mm), next line (4.5 mm), and final answer (1.5 mm/day) show that the intended calculation is $9\times0.5=4.5$ mm.

The embedded course check uses 5 mm over 2 days and $K_p=0.4$:

$$
E_{adjusted}=\frac{(5)(0.4)}{2}=1\ \mathrm{mm/day}
$$

### Weighing-lysimeter pattern

Useful conversions and geometry:

$$
1\ \mathrm{L\ water}=1\ \mathrm{kg\ water},\qquad 1000\ \mathrm{L}=1\ \mathrm{m^3}
$$

$$
V=A d,\qquad d=\frac{V}{A},\qquad 1\ \mathrm{m}=1000\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

Course example:

1. Dry lysimeter mass $=210$ kg; add 15 L $=15$ kg, so starting mass $=225$ kg.
2. Final mass after 5 days $=215$ kg; water loss $=10$ kg $=10$ L $=0.01\ \mathrm{m^3}$.
3. Area $=10\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so

$$
d=\frac{0.01\ \mathrm{m^3}}{10\ \mathrm{m^2}}=0.001\ \mathrm{m}=1\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

4. Divide by 5 days:

$$
ET=\frac{1\ \mathrm{mm}}{5\ \mathrm{days}}=0.2\ \mathrm{mm/day}
$$

The second embedded check starts at 200 kg dry, adds 25 L, ends at 220 kg after 1 day, has area $10\ \mathrm{m^2}$, and records no outflow. The loss is 5 L $=0.005\ \mathrm{m^3}$, giving $0.5\ \mathrm{mm/day}$.

## Common Confusions

- A raw pan reading is not automatically the ET of the surrounding field or lake; siting and $K_p$ matter.
- A coefficient of 0.65 and 65% are equivalent. Do not multiply by 65.
- Apply the coefficient to total loss, then divide by time.
- A mass difference is not yet an ET rate. Convert mass to water volume, volume to depth over area, and depth to a rate.
- Include added water when finding the lysimeter's starting wet mass.
- "No outflow" makes the worked weighing-lysimeter water loss attributable to ET; an unaccounted outflow would invalidate that simple inference.
- A weighing lysimeter tracks mass; a water balance lysimeter tracks inflows, outflows, and water levels.
