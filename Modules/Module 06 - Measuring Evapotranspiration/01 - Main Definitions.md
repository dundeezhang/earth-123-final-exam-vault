# Main Definitions

Up: [[00 - EARTH 123 Final Exam Home]]

Module: [[00 - Module 06 Overview]]

| Term | Definition | Course section | Example / where / application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Field measurement | A localized measurement using an instrument such as an evaporation pan or lysimeter. | 6.1 | Figure 6.1.1 places a standard pan in a grassy field with a depth bar and protected floating thermometer. |
| Potential evaporation / potential ET | The maximum evaporation or transpiration possible with an infinite water supply and unsaturated air. | 6.1 | A water-filled evaporation pan estimates the site's potential evaporation because water remains available at the measuring surface. |
| Actual evaporation / actual ET | The evaporation or transpiration occurring under current environmental conditions; it is always less than or equal to potential ET. | 6.1 | In the course check, a $10\ \mathrm{m^2}$ lysimeter loses 5 L in one day, giving actual ET of $0.5\ \mathrm{mm/day}$. |
| Reference crop evaporation | A modelled potential-ET value for crop or vegetation types, used to help estimate total ET over large monocultures or monocrops. | 6.1 | A farmer applies the reference value across a large monocrop when estimating its total crop-water demand. |
| Evaporation pan | A water-filled field device that estimates evaporation from the change in water depth over time. | 6.1 | The course's standard pan is about 120 cm in diameter and 25 cm deep, with a ruler for reading water-level change. |
| Surface pan | An evaporation pan elevated slightly above land, allowing wind to cool its sides and bottom. | 6.1 | In Figure 6.1.2, the land-based surface pan may lose less water than the nearby sunken pan because wind carries heat away. |
| Sunken pan | An evaporation pan placed within the ground; it is insulated from wind and cold but is vulnerable to leaves, debris, and overland runoff. | 6.1 | Figure 6.1.2 shows this pan buried near the lake, where retained heat may raise evaporation and runoff may contaminate readings. |
| Floating pan | A pan placed on a pond, lake, or reservoir to represent evaporation from the water body. | 6.1 | On a cloudy day it can approach the lake's temperature; on a sunny day overheating requires a correction. |
| Pan coefficient, $K_p$ | A decimal or percentage correction applied to pan water loss; it depends on pan type, location, month, and season. | 6.1 | For 9 mm lost in 3 days with $K_p=0.5$, the corrected rate is $(9)(0.5)/3=1.5\ \mathrm{mm/day}$. |
| Lysimeter | An atmosphere-open container filled with local soil and plants that measures actual ET through mass change or a monitored water balance. | 6.1 | Figure 6.1.4 uses native grasses and local soil so the instrument represents actual field ET. |
| Weighing lysimeter | A lysimeter mounted on a scale; precipitation increases its mass and evaporation plus transpiration reduce its mass. | 6.1 | In the worked example, a 10 kg mass loss over $10\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and 5 days converts to $0.2\ \mathrm{mm/day}$. |
| Water balance lysimeter | A lysimeter with a reservoir, float, drains, and monitoring equipment used to record water levels, inflows, and outflows. | 6.1 | Figure 6.1.5 uses a float and recorder in a lower reservoir to track level decline beneath native soil and vegetation. |
| Energy balance method | An approach that estimates energy available for evaporation by accounting for incoming radiation, reflected radiation, outgoing longwave radiation, and other heat uses. | 6.2 | A hydrologist measures radiation, then subtracts $R_E$, $R_A$, $H_A$, $H_B$, and $H_C$ from $R$ to estimate $H_E$. |
| Pyranometer | A device used to measure incoming atmospheric radiation or radiation reflected from the surface. | 6.2 | Figure 6.2.1 points the instrument upward for incoming radiation and downward for surface-reflected radiation. |
| Albedo / direct reflection, $R_E$ | The part of incoming radiation reflected directly from the surface. | 6.2 | The downward-facing measurement in Figure 6.2.1 supplies the $R_E$ term subtracted from incoming solar radiation. |
| Longwave radiation, $R_A$ | Radiation moving from the surface to the atmosphere. | 6.2 | In the simplified course balance, a hydrologist subtracts $R_A$ with $R_E$ from $R$ to obtain $H_E$. |
| Evaporation energy, $H_E$ | Energy left available for evaporation; the target quantity in the energy balance. | 6.2 | Since evaporating 1 g of water takes about 600 calories, measured $H_E$ can be converted into a possible water loss. |
| Air-heating loss, $H_A$ | Heat used to warm overlying air masses. | 6.2 | In the expanded balance, energy warming air above a study surface is removed before the remainder is assigned to evaporation. |
| Surface-heating loss, $H_B$ | Heat used to warm the soil or surface water body. | 6.2 | Over a lake, solar energy that warms the surface water is counted as $H_B$ rather than evaporation energy. |
| Plant-growth energy, $H_C$ | Energy converted to plant growth; the course notes describe it as often very small. | 6.2 | At a vegetated study site, the expanded balance includes this small energy allocation before solving for $H_E$. |
| Water balance method | An approach that estimates evaporation as the residual after inputs, outputs, and change in storage are measured. | 6.2 | For a freshwater lake, a hydrologist solves $E=P+SW_{in}+GW_{in}-SW_{out}-GW_{out}-\Delta S$. |
| Change in storage, $\Delta S$ | The change in the amount of water stored over the balance period, equal to total inputs minus total outputs. | 6.2 | If a lake level rises during the study period, positive $\Delta S$ is subtracted because some incoming water stayed in the lake. |
| Penman-Monteith method | A model that calculates potential ET from a diverse set of atmospheric and surface data. | 6.2 | Farmers use the model to schedule water replacement for a large monocrop without under-watering or waterlogging it. |
| Empirical method | A locally calibrated formula derived from experimental observations and years of local data. | 6.2 | A long lake record of evaporation, air and water temperature, wind speed, and relative humidity can produce a local forecasting equation. |
| Empirical constant, $K$ | A site-specific coefficient used to tune an empirical ET model to local conditions. | 6.2 | A $K$ fitted at one lake stays with that lake's calibration and should not be transferred unchanged to a forest or field. |
| Wind-speed function, $f(u)$ | The component of the empirical ET equation that represents the effect of wind speed. | 6.2 | A hydrologist inserts the lake's observed wind speed into $f(u)$ before multiplying by the vapour-pressure difference. |
| Saturated vapour pressure, $e_s$ | Saturated vapour pressure at the temperature of the air above the surface. | 6.2 | In $E=Kf(u)(e_s-e_a)$, $e_s$ is evaluated at the measured air temperature above the lake. |
| Actual vapour pressure, $e_a$ | Actual vapour pressure at the temperature of the air above the surface. | 6.2 | Relative-humidity observations from the lake record help establish $e_a$, which is subtracted from $e_s$. |
| Evapotranspiration chamber / dome | A sealed, clear glass or plastic enclosure used to collect and monitor vapour emitted by a plant and the covered ground. | 6.2 | In Figure 6.2.3, vapour density rises sharply within minutes of sealing a chamber over a plant. |
| Effective specific yield / storage coefficient, $S_y$ | A soil storage value describing how much water the soil can hold and provide to plants; it is commonly obtained from a table based on geologic properties. | 6.3 | The Figure 6.3.1 calculation uses $S_y=0.13$, selected from soil information such as grain size, porosity, and permeability. |
| Nocturnal rise rate, $a$ | The hourly water-table rise from 12:00 a.m. to 4:00 a.m., representing water-table flow and recharge. | 6.3 | In Figure 6.3.1, the table rises from 0.5 m to 0.6 m in 4 hours, so $a=0.025\ \mathrm{m/h}$. |
| Daily fluctuation, $b$ | The signed water-table elevation change between observations 24 hours apart. | 6.3 | From 6:00 a.m. to 6:00 a.m. in Figure 6.3.1, $b=0.50-0.62=-0.12\ \mathrm{m}$. |

## Commonly Confused Terms

- **Potential vs. actual ET:** potential is the environmental maximum under unlimited water and unsaturated air; actual is what occurs under present conditions. Thus, $ET_{actual}\le ET_{potential}$.
- **Evaporation pan vs. lysimeter:** a pan observes open-water depth loss and estimates potential evaporation; a lysimeter contains local soil and vegetation and estimates actual ET.
- **Weighing vs. water balance lysimeter:** one observes mass change on a scale; the other observes water movement and reservoir-level change.
- **Energy balance vs. water balance:** the energy method solves for energy available to drive evaporation; the water method solves for evaporation as an unmeasured water flux.
- **Empirical vs. physical measurement:** an empirical method predicts from a locally fitted equation; a chamber attempts to capture vapour directly.
- **$a$ vs. $b$ in the groundwater method:** $a$ is a rate in depth per hour over the 12:00-4:00 a.m. recovery interval; $b$ is a signed depth change over 24 hours.
- **Depth vs. rate:** a depth such as millimetres becomes a rate only after division by the elapsed time, such as millimetres per day.
